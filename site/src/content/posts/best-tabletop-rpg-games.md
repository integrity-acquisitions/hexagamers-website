Most of what we write about here comes in a box with a board in it. This one's different. A tabletop RPG is a book, some dice, and a group of people making things up together. No board, no win condition, and nobody's counting victory points at the end.

I came to RPGs the long way around, through board games, and I want to name the thing that kept me out for years: I assumed you had to be a certain kind of person to play them. Someone with voices. Someone with a binder. That's not true, and the games below are the ones that proved it. Several of them get you playing in under an hour.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](#best-tabletop-rpg-games-comparison-table)

A quick definition, because the term gets stretched. By "tabletop RPG" I mean the pen-and-paper kind: one person runs the world (the GM), everyone else plays a character, and the rules exist to resolve what happens when you try something risky. That's different from a dungeon crawl board game like Gloomhaven, where the box tells you what's in the next room. Great games, but a different hobby, and if that's what you're after, our board game lists are the better stop.

What I've tried to do is spread these across the range. A couple of big campaign systems you can sink years into, a couple you can learn over a lunch break, and some that only exist to give you one unforgettable night. Each entry gets the honest version: what people love about it, and where it frustrates them. Because "best" here depends almost entirely on what your group wants out of an evening, and pretending otherwise wouldn't help you.

## Top Ten Tabletop RPG Games

## 1. Dungeons & Dragons (5th Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/dungeons-and-dragons-5e.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0786969822/?tag=hexagamers-20)

There's no honest version of this list that doesn't start here. D&D isn't the best-designed RPG on the table and plenty of people will tell you so at length, but it's the one your friends have heard of, the one with a decade of free adventures online, and the one where you can find a group in any city on a Tuesday. That last part matters more than any mechanic.

The pitch is the one everybody already knows. You make a character (a class, a species, a handful of numbers) and you go into the world looking for trouble. When the outcome is uncertain, you roll a twenty-sided die, add a modifier, and find out. Combat is the part with the most rules by a wide margin, and the game is at its most confident when swords are out.

**Why people love it:** the sheer weight of support. There is a video, a map, a pre-written adventure, and a character-builder app for anything you want to do, and the 2024 revision cleaned up a lot of the rough edges without breaking compatibility with everything already on your shelf. For a first RPG, the odds that someone at your table already knows the rules are higher than for every other game here combined.

**The catch:** it's a game about fighting things, and if your group wants intrigue or investigation you'll be doing that in spite of the rules rather than with them. Character creation takes a new player a solid hour, combat can crawl at higher levels, and the Dungeon Master carries a genuinely heavy prep load. Wizards of the Coast has also burned a lot of community goodwill in the last few years, which is why so many of the games below exist.

Start here if you want the biggest possible on-ramp and an easy time finding players. It's the default for a reason.

## 2. Call of Cthulhu (Starter Set)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/call-of-cthulhu-starter-set.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1568824637/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If D&D is about becoming powerful, Call of Cthulhu is about finding out how little that would help. You play ordinary people in the 1920s, a professor or a journalist or a doctor, who pull on a loose thread and discover something underneath the world that was never meant to be seen. It has been running since 1981 and it still does horror better than almost anything else.

The mechanical hook is Sanity, and it's the best implementation of the idea anyone's managed. It's a stat, like your strength, and it goes down when you witness things that don't fit inside a human mind. Spend it long enough and your character develops phobias, delusions, and eventually stops being playable. Knowledge and survival are directly opposed: the more you learn, the less of you is left.

**Why people love it:** the investigation actually works. You're reading documents, interviewing witnesses, and piecing a mystery together, and the system supports that instead of treating it as filler between fights. Because characters are fragile and death is common, groups play it in self-contained scenarios rather than long campaigns, which suits people who can't commit to a weekly slot for two years. I've linked the Starter Set rather than the full Keeper Rulebook because it's one of the best-designed introductions in the hobby: it teaches you the system through a solo adventure first, then hands you three more to run for a group, dice included.

**The catch:** combat is short and lethal in a way that surprises people coming from D&D, and "shoot it" is almost never the answer. Some players find the arc of inevitable decline dispiriting rather than thrilling. You are not building toward triumph. The tone also demands a group that will take it seriously; one person doing bits will sink the entire mood.

For a group that wants tension and mystery over power fantasy, this is the peak of the form. The Keeper Rulebook is there when you want the whole system.

## 3. Daggerheart

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/daggerheart.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DWLWMRK1/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Daggerheart came out of Critical Role's publishing arm in 2025 and it's the most interesting thing to happen to mainstream fantasy RPGs in years. It's aimed squarely at the enormous group of people who like the *idea* of D&D but wish the rules cared as much about story as they do about tactical positioning.

The core roll is the clever part. You roll two twelve-sided dice of different colours, Hope and Fear, and which one comes up higher changes what happens next even when you succeed. Succeed with Hope and you gain a resource. Succeed with Fear and it works, but the GM gets to twist the knife. So there's no flat "you did it," and the fiction keeps moving even on your good rolls. Combat drops rigid turn order for a fluid spotlight that passes around the table.

**Why people love it:** it's built for the kind of collaborative, character-driven game that people were already trying to run in other systems. Card-based domains and subclasses make character building tactile instead of a spreadsheet exercise, and the box is beautiful. It landed in Foundry's top ten most-played systems within months of release, which for a brand-new game is remarkable.

**The catch:** it's new, so the adventure library is thin compared to anything with a thirty-year back catalogue and you'll be writing more of your own material. The card component means more table space and more setup than a book and dice. And if your group's favourite part of an RPG is grid tactics and precise ranges, the loose spotlight structure will feel like something was taken away rather than added.

For a group that wants D&D's feel with better storytelling machinery, this is the one to look at first.

## 4. Pathfinder (Second Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pathfinder-player-core.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1640785531/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Pathfinder is where people go when they want more rules, not fewer. It started as a fork of an older edition of D&D and has grown into its own thing entirely, with a second edition that's tighter and better balanced than the sprawl it came from. If character building is your favourite part of the hobby, this is the deep end.

Every turn gives you three actions to spend however you like (move, strike, cast, raise a shield, whatever the situation needs) and that single change makes combat far more flexible than a rigid move-and-attack structure. Character options are enormous. Ancestries, backgrounds, classes, and a mountain of feats at nearly every level mean two players who picked the same class can end up playing completely different characters.

**Why people love it:** the rules are precise and they hold up. Things are balanced, edge cases are covered, and the GM isn't constantly making judgment calls to paper over gaps. Paizo also puts the entire rule set online for free and legally, which is genuinely unusual. You can run this game without buying anything, then buy the books because you want them.

**The catch:** it is a lot. New players routinely need help to build a first character, the volume of feats can paralyze people, and the tight math means an unprepared party can walk into an encounter and get taken apart. It rewards system mastery, which is exactly the appeal for some groups and exactly the barrier for others. This is not the game you hand to four people who have never rolled a die.

If your table likes crunch and wants a fantasy system with real depth, Pathfinder earns the shelf space.

## 5. Blades in the Dark

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/blades-in-the-dark.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1613171323/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Blades in the Dark is a heist game, and it solves the problem that ruins most heists at the table. You know the one: the group spends ninety minutes planning, the plan collapses on contact, and everyone's a bit deflated. Blades cuts that out by starting you mid-job. You pick the type of score, roll to see how well you got in, and you're already inside.

The planning still happens. It just happens *backwards*. Partway through, when you need a rope or a bribed guard or a second exit, you spend a flashback and establish that you set it up beforehand. Your crew is competent by default, so you get to play them as competent instead of proving it in advance. It's one of those designs where you immediately wonder why nobody did it sooner.

**Why people love it:** almost no GM prep, which for a lot of tables is the difference between a campaign that runs and one that quietly dies. Between jobs you manage your gang's territory, rivalries, and heat with the law, so the city changes shape around what you did last session. The industrial-ghost-haunted setting is distinct enough to carry a whole campaign without much invention.

**The catch:** the position-and-effect system takes a few sessions to feel natural, and GMs coming from D&D often struggle with how much authority they're handing to the players. Improvisation is not optional here, and a table that wants to be led through a story will find it exposing. It's also firmly built for one setting, so it doesn't bend easily to other genres without a hack.

For a group that wants sharp, low-prep sessions with real momentum, this is the best-designed game on the list.

## 6. Shadowdark

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/shadowdark.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DMK1MPW3/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Shadowdark won four gold ENNIEs in 2024, including Product of the Year, and it deserved every one. The idea behind it is simple: take the fast, dangerous feel of the very earliest dungeon crawls, and rebuild it with rules a modern player can actually read.

The mechanic everyone talks about is the torch. Light sources burn for one real hour, an actual timer running on the table, and when it goes out, you're in the dark, where things go badly very quickly. It turns dungeon exploration into a genuine pressure clock. Suddenly nobody wants to search the third empty room, and the debate about whether to press deeper or turn back has real stakes because the timer is ticking while you argue.

**Why people love it:** it's fast in every direction. Character creation takes about five minutes, the rules fit in your head, and a session moves without the constant book-checking that bogs heavier systems down. Everything is written in tight two-page spreads, which makes it one of the easiest RPG books to actually use at the table. The random tables for rumours, loot, and encounters mean a GM can prep a night in fifteen minutes.

**The catch:** characters are fragile and they die, sometimes to bad luck in the first session, which is the point but isn't for everyone. It's deliberately sparse on character options compared to Pathfinder or D&D. You're a fighter, and what makes you interesting is what you do, not what's on your sheet. The real-time torch timer is also divisive; some groups find it thrilling, others find it stressful in a way that spoils the evening.

If you want old-school danger without an old-school rulebook, start here.

## 7. Monster of the Week

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/monster-of-the-week.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1613172036/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If your reference point is *Buffy*, *Supernatural*, or *The X-Files*, this game is built precisely for you. Each session is one episode: something is killing people in a small town, your crew shows up, you figure out what it is, you find out what hurts it, and you deal with it. Roll credits.

Your character is a playbook (the Chosen, the Spooky, the Expert, the Monstrous) and each one comes pre-loaded with a role in the group and a reason to be there. You roll two six-sided dice against a short list of moves, and the range in the middle is where the game lives: a partial success where you get what you wanted but it costs you something. That single band of results generates more story than any critical hit ever has.

**Why people love it:** the episodic structure is a gift to real adult schedules. Nobody has to remember a plot from three months ago, missing players is trivial to explain away, and a satisfying complete story fits in one evening. GM prep is a mystery sketch on a single page: the monster, what it wants, and what happens if nobody stops it. That is about as light as it gets.

**The catch:** the playbooks are strong personalities and two players can end up with overlapping niches if you don't coordinate. Because the monster investigation follows a familiar shape, long campaigns can start to feel samey without deliberate variation. And the rules-light frame means the GM improvises constantly; there's no tactical scaffolding to fall back on when the table goes quiet.

For a group that wants a complete story in one night, this is the easiest recommendation here.

## 8. The One Ring (Starter Set)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/the-one-ring-starter-set.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/9189143485/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Plenty of games let you play in a fantasy world that borrowed from Tolkien. This is the one that actually understands him. The One Ring is set in the years between *The Hobbit* and *The Lord of the Rings*, and it's built around the parts of those books that other systems skip entirely: the long road, the weight of the journey, and the cost of being away from home.

Travel is a real system here, not a line you narrate past. A journey across the Wilderland has phases, fatigue, and events, and arriving somewhere exhausted matters mechanically. The other half is Shadow: a corruption track that rises when you do something cruel or dwell too long on dark things. Hope and despair are stats. A hero can survive every battle and still be lost.

**Why people love it:** the tone is exactly right. Between adventures you take a Fellowship Phase to rest, heal, and pursue something personal, which gives the campaign a rhythm of danger and recovery that mirrors the books closely. The Starter Set in particular is beautifully produced and has one of the gentlest on-ramps of any game on this list, with pre-made characters, dice, maps, and a solid opening adventure in the box.

**The catch:** it's a licensed game with a specific setting and a specific mood, so if your group wants to play space pirates you're in the wrong book. Combat is deliberately dangerous and unglamorous, which disappoints players who came in wanting to be Aragorn from session one. The custom dice are convenient but you'll want the real ones, and it's a system with enough moving parts that a first session runs slow.

If your table loves Middle-earth, nothing else comes close to this.

## 9. MÖRK BORG

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/mork-borg.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/9188805646/?tag=hexagamers-20)

MÖRK BORG is a doom metal album that someone printed as a rulebook. Neon yellow pages, black sludge everywhere, text running sideways. It's the most aggressively designed book in the hobby and it fights you a little on purpose. The world is ending, definitively, and your characters are scavenging through the last of it.

The rules take up almost no space at all. You can learn them in about ten minutes, characters are rolled up in two, and death arrives fast and often. What the book spends its pages on instead is atmosphere and content you can steal: random tables of scum and villainy, cursed loot, broken prophecies, and a doomsday clock that ticks the apocalypse closer as you play. It's less a system than a mood with dice attached.

**Why people love it:** it's the perfect one-shot filler. Somebody cancelled, the campaign's on hold, you have three hours, and you can hand out characters and be playing in five minutes. The art direction alone makes it a thing people pick up off the shelf and flip through, and its open licence has produced a huge library of third-party material, including full conversions like Pirate Borg and CY_BORG.

**The catch:** the layout that makes it wonderful to look at makes it genuinely awkward to reference mid-session, and you will lose a rule inside a two-page spread of black ink. Character death is frequent enough that emotional investment is difficult, which rules out the long campaign for most groups. The relentless bleakness is also a specific taste. Some tables find it hilarious, others find it exhausting after two hours.

As a palate cleanser between serious campaigns, it's excellent, and it's cheap enough to be an easy yes.

## 10. Ten Candles

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ten-candles.webp)](https://cavalrygames.com/ten-candles-info)

Ten Candles is the strangest thing on this list and the one I'd most like you to try. It's a horror game with one rule that governs everything: your characters all die. Not "might die." The game is built so that by the end of the session, nobody is left. What you're playing for is what they do with the time.

You play in a dark room by the light of ten actual tea light candles. The sun went out ten days ago, something is out there in the dark, and each candle you snuff out takes a piece of the game with it. As they go, the dice pool shrinks and the odds turn steadily against you. Before you start, everyone writes down truths about their character on cards, and those cards get burned to buy successes. Literally burned, in the candle flame. When the last one goes out, the story is over.

**Why people love it:** nothing else produces an evening like it. The candles do work that no mechanic could. The room genuinely darkens as things get worse, and by the final scene people are speaking quietly without being asked to. It needs no prep, no GM screen, and no campaign commitment, and groups who have played it tend to talk about that one night for years afterward.

**The catch:** it's a one-shot by design, so it's not a game you build a hobby around, and the price is high for something you may play twice. It demands genuine buy-in from everyone at the table, and one person treating it as a joke breaks it completely, more than any other game here. It's also emotionally heavy by intent, and it's worth checking that everyone actually wants that before you light the first candle.

Not a weekly game. But as a one-night experience, it's unmatched.

## Conclusion

If you're starting from zero and want the path of least resistance, **Dungeons & Dragons** is still the answer, because the community and free material around it outweigh every design complaint people fairly make. If you want the same fantasy feel with rules that push story instead of tactics, **Daggerheart** is the modern alternative worth your money. And if you only have one evening and want to actually finish something, **Monster of the Week** or **Ten Candles** will give you a complete story before you go home.

The advice I'd give my past self is this: the barrier to RPGs is smaller than it looks, and it isn't the rules. It's finding four people who'll show up on the same night. Pick a system that survives a cancelled session (the episodic ones on this list all do) and you'll actually play, which beats owning the perfect rulebook you never open. Start with a one-shot before anyone commits to a campaign.
