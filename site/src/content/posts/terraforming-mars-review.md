## Overview

You're running a corporation racing to make Mars livable. Raise the temperature, pump oxygen into the atmosphere, melt ice into oceans — hit the three global targets and the game ends. Along the way you're buying project cards that build your own private engine: energy production, plant production, the kind of card that quietly turns your next fifteen turns into free points. Nobody's fighting over territory here so much as racing the clock, and the clock is the whole table's terraforming progress. What makes it interesting is how much of your game gets decided in the first few rounds — the cards you keep and the ones you pass on set the shape of everything after.

## Ryan's Review

### Likes
- Card pool is enormous, so no two games build the same engine
- Genuinely satisfying to watch your production numbers compound turn over turn
- Global parameters give every game a built-in countdown instead of a vague "until someone wins"
- Placing tiles on the Mars board still matters even though the game is mostly about your own tableau
- Milestones and awards give you something to fight over besides the terraforming race itself

### Dislikes
- First game is rough — the card icons take real effort to parse before anything clicks
- Runs long, and a slow player at 4-5 can make it feel longer
- Little direct interaction most of the game; it can drift into everyone quietly doing their own thing
- Setup and teardown take a while with this many card types and tokens

## Getting It to the Table

A friend who'd been raving about it for months finally brought his copy over. I remember looking at the card layout before we started — production icons, resource icons, tags, requirement icons, all crammed onto one card — and thinking this was going to be a slog to learn. It kind of was, for about half a round. Then the icons stopped needing translation and started just being information, the way a Catan number token stops requiring math after your third game.

## Thoughts

**The core loop is buying and playing cards.** Every generation you draft a few cards from your hand, pay to play the ones you want, and either build immediate effects or ongoing production. Production is the real engine — a card that gives you +2 energy production isn't a one-time bump, it's two extra energy every single generation for the rest of the game. Early purchases compound, which means the corporation you pick and the first three or four cards you commit to basically write the outline of your whole game.

**The global parameters are doing more work than they look like.** Temperature, oxygen, and oceans climb as players raise them, and the game ends the moment all three hit their caps. That's a shared clock nobody controls alone, which changes how you play — sometimes the smart move is to NOT raise a parameter yourself, because you're better off letting someone else pay for progress you also benefit from. Watching the temperature track creep toward the ceiling in the back half of the game adds real tension even though nobody's attacking anyone directly.

**The moment it clicked for me was realizing tile placement isn't decoration.** I'd assumed the actual Mars board was set dressing around the card game happening in everyone's hand. It's not. Ocean tiles pay out bonuses to adjacent players, greenery tiles both raise oxygen and score points at the end, and city tiles want to be next to greenery for the income. Fighting over the good hexes on that board turned out to be one of the sharpest decisions in the whole game, and I didn't see it coming from the rulebook alone.

**There's very little direct conflict, and that's either a feature or a problem depending on your group.** Nobody's stealing resources from you or attacking your production. The competition is almost entirely about who builds the better engine and who claims the milestones and awards first — a handful of bonus objectives that reward being first to hit a threshold. Those awards are honestly where most of our table talk happens, since everyone's watching the same three or four race conditions.

**Components are solid without being flashy.** The cards are the star and they hold up fine through shuffling. The player boards track your resources with cubes, which works but eats table space once you've got five players' worth of boards and a shared Mars map in the middle. Budget the table real estate before you commit to a full player count.

**Price and time are the two honest complaints.** It's a $65-80 box depending on where you catch it, and two hours is a real commitment on a weeknight. Neither is a dealbreaker if you're already the kind of group that plays Agricola or Gloomhaven, but it's not a game you pull out to fill twenty minutes before people head home.

## Conclusion

Terraforming Mars rewards the group willing to sit through one confusing first game to get to a genuinely deep engine-builder on the other side. It's for players who like Agricola-style long strategy games and don't mind a quiet table where the real fights happen over milestones and board space instead of direct confrontation. Worth borrowing or trying at a game night first if the runtime or the card density gives you pause — but if engine building is your thing, this earns the shelf space.
