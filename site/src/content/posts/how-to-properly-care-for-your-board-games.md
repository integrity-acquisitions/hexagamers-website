I own games I've had for over a decade that still shuffle like they're new, and I own games that are barely two years old with cards so soft at the corners they're practically round. The difference isn't luck. It's whether I bothered to sleeve them, store them flat, and keep them out of a hot garage.

None of this is complicated. Most of it takes an extra five minutes at the end of game night. But board games aren't cheap anymore (a lot of the good ones run $50 to $80), and a little bit of care is the difference between a collection that lasts and a shelf of soft, warped, missing-piece boxes you're embarrassed to bring to a friend's house.

Here's what actually matters.

## Sleeve anything that gets handled a lot

If a game gets played often, its cards take the worst of it. Shuffling, dealing, sweaty hands, someone setting a drink too close. Cards absorb all of it. Sleeving is the single highest-value thing you can do for a card-heavy game.

You don't need to sleeve everything you own. I don't sleeve a game I've played twice and will probably trade away. But anything in regular rotation, [Catan](/catan-review/), [Wingspan](/wingspan-review/), [Codenames](/codenames-review/), any deck-builder, gets sleeved. Standard "board game sized" sleeves fit most Euro games; check card dimensions before buying, because "standard American" (poker-sized) and "standard European" sleeves are genuinely different sizes and a mismatch will fight you the whole time you're trying to shuffle.

One thing worth knowing: sleeves add bulk. A sleeved deck of 100+ cards might not fit back in the original box insert, which is its own small annoyance covered below.

## Store the box flat, not on its spine

This one's boring but it matters more than people think. Standing boxes upright on a shelf, spine-out like books, puts weight and gravity to work against you over months and years. Cards slide to one side of the box, lids warp, and thin cardboard boxes (the kind a lot of mid-weight Euro games ship in) slowly bow outward.

Store boxes flat and stack them, the way they'd sit in a game store's back room. If shelf space forces you to stand some up, at least rotate which ones sit at the bottom of a stack so the same box isn't permanently bearing the load.

## Keep them out of heat, humidity, and direct sun

A garage, an attic, or a closet against an exterior wall all swing in temperature and humidity more than the inside of your house does. That swing is what actually wrecks components: cardboard warps, glue on box art can start to lift, and cards absorb moisture and get that slightly wavy, no-longer-crisp feel.

Direct sunlight is its own problem: box art fades fast, sometimes within a single summer on a windowsill. If you're proud enough of your collection to display it, keep the display shelf away from a window that gets direct light for a chunk of the day.

## Actually use the insert (or replace it)

A lot of modern games ship with a molded plastic insert designed around exactly where every component goes. Use it. A good insert keeps pieces from sliding around and grinding against each other during transport, which is where a surprising number of chipped miniatures and worn tokens come from.

If the stock insert is bad (some are, especially in cheaper editions) or sleeved cards no longer fit, this is where the board game accessory market actually earns its keep. Modular foam inserts, small ziplock bags for tokens, and rubber-banded card stacks in a spare box all work. If you're the type who wants a nicer version of "rubber-banded stacks," we've also covered [the best dice towers](/best-dice-towers/) for games that need somewhere for dice to land besides the table edge. Not storage, but the same instinct: a little bit of gear that protects the experience.

## Wash your hands, skip the snack table

I know how this sounds. But greasy fingerprints on cards and tiles are permanent in a way most people don't expect until they've actually done it. Chip crumbs and soda are worse: a spilled drink at a table can end a game night and a $60 game in the same ten seconds.

This doesn't mean banning food from game night. It means keeping drinks off the actual table surface where the board and cards live, and a quick hand-wipe before anyone deals a hand of something you actually care about. Cheap party decks you'll play once? Don't worry about it. The game you've been building a collection engine in for the last 45 minutes? Worth the ten seconds.

## Don't force a jammed box lid

Every collection has one: the box that used to close fine and now needs a knee on it to shut. That's usually a sign the insert is overstuffed, often because sleeved cards or an expansion got crammed in without a plan. Forcing it warps the lid permanently and stresses the corners until they split.

If a box won't close without a fight, that's the cue to reorganize. Pull the insert out, figure out what's actually taking up the extra space (sleeves are the usual culprit), and either thin it out or move the overflow into a small separate box. A slightly bulging box you can still close gently is fine. A box you have to lean on is not.

## Wipe down components, don't submerge them

Wooden meeples, plastic miniatures, and resin tokens all pick up dust and hand oil over time. A damp (not wet) cloth handles most of it. Skip the dishwasher and skip soaking anything: wood pieces can swell and crack, and painted miniatures can lose paint in a sink faster than you'd think. For genuinely sticky or grimy components, a barely damp cloth with a little dish soap, then dry immediately, is about as aggressive as you should get.

Paper money and cardboard punchboard components don't get this treatment at all. Dry cloth only, or leave them alone entirely.

## Punch carefully, and punch everything at once

Punchboard, the cardboard sheets with tokens and pieces you push out before your first game, tears if you rush it. Push from the back, not the front, and go slow on any piece with a tight inner corner. A little bit of gentle back-and-forth flexing before you punch (not aggressive bending) softens the connector tabs so they separate cleanly.

Punch the whole sheet the first time you open the box, even pieces you won't need until later expansions or later player counts unlock. Half-punched sheets get lost or bent in storage, and you'll be digging through a box seven months later trying to remember which chits you still need to pop out mid-setup.

## The verdict

None of this requires buying a single product if you don't want to. Storing games flat, keeping them out of heat and direct sun, and wiping down components costs nothing and takes minutes. Sleeves are the one place worth spending a little money if a game is going to see real table time. They're the cheapest insurance in the hobby.

Treat the games you actually love like they're worth keeping around for a decade, and most of them will be. The ones you don't care as much about? Let them get a little beat up. That's what they're for.
