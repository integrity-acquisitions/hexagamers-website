People mean two completely different things when they go looking for a drinking board game, and mixing them up is how you end up with a bad night.

The first is a game built to make you drink, where the rules exist to move alcohol around the table. The second is a real board game that holds up after everyone's had a few. Light enough that nobody has to track a rule they've forgotten, social enough that being a bit loose actually improves it.

We've put both on this list, because most groups want both over the course of a night. The purpose-built ones burn hot and fade fast. The real games are what you reach for at 11pm when everyone's warmed up but still wants to play something.

The games that work here are the ones with rules you can hold in your head while distracted. Anything with an upkeep phase will collapse. We've watched it happen more than once. Someone suggests a "quick game" of something medium-weight at the wrong point in the evening, and forty minutes later three people are on their phones and one guy is quietly re-reading the rulebook.

> Looking for something for a sober crowd? Our [best party board games](/best-party-board-games-2026/) list covers the same energy without the drinking, and [best board games for adults](/best-board-games-for-adults/) leans risqué rather than boozy.

## What Makes a Good Drinking Board Game?

Rules you can explain in under two minutes. That's the main one. If somebody has to keep the rulebook open, you've picked the wrong game.

Short turns matter almost as much. Downtime is where drinking nights die, so everyone should be doing something, or at least watching something happen, on every single turn.

Losing has to be funny rather than annoying. This rules out anything with a big skill gap, because the good player wins every time and the table stops caring by the third game.

Then there's player count. People wander off to the kitchen, people show up an hour late, and a game that breaks when the sixth person sits down is a bad fit for that.

Last one is components. Say what you want about cardboard, but a board that lives on a table covered in cans is going to take damage eventually. Cards and wooden blocks forgive a lot more.

## Top Ten Drinking Board Games

## 1. Skull

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Skull_kfpcyd.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08R2BPLSN/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Skull is the best game on this list and it isn't close. Each player gets four discs (three roses and one skull) and takes turns laying them face down in a stack. At some point someone bids that they can flip a certain number of discs without hitting a skull. Bids escalate, someone wins the bid, and then they have to actually do it, starting with their own stack.

The discs look like beer mats. Make of that what you will.

What makes it work while drinking is that there's nothing to track. No points to add up mid-game, nothing to read on a board. You're looking at faces and stacks of cardboard, which is about the amount of information a slightly drunk person can handle. The whole game is nerve and reading people, and alcohol makes people worse at hiding both of those.

Skull needs at least three players and it's much better at five or six. At three it's thin. It's also the only game here a sober person would take seriously, so it survives the transition when the night winds down and people start making coffee.

## 2. Jenga (The Drinking Version)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/jenga.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07CW84Q8B/?tag=hexagamers-20)

You already know how Jenga works. The drinking version is the same game with a permanent marker involved. You write a rule on each block, and pulling that block means doing what it says.

We'd argue this is still the best value option on the list, because a standard Jenga set costs less than most purpose-built drinking games and you get to write the rules yourself. Rules tuned to your actual group land harder than anything printed at a factory. Inside jokes, references to what happened last time, a rule aimed squarely at one person.

The physical element is the real draw. Everyone watches every turn, because a tower falling is inherently funny and everyone wants to see it. Compare that to a card game where half the table is looking at their own hand.

Write the rules with a light touch. The blocks are permanent, and a set where every third block says "take a shot" gets used exactly once and then sits in a cupboard. Aim for mostly silly, occasionally a sip.

## 3. Buzzed

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/buzzed.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07Z75QZNP/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Buzzed is the most popular of the purpose-built drinking card games, and it's the one we'd point a first-time buyer at. You draw a card, you read it out, and it tells someone at the table to drink based on something true about them. Never been on a plane. Tallest person. Wearing shoelaces.

It plays with anywhere from three to twenty people, which is the real selling point, because very few games on this list still function at fifteen players.

There's no actual game here, which is worth being upfront about. You draw, you read, someone drinks. It's an icebreaker with a deck attached, and it does that job well for about the first half hour. Best used early in a night with a group that doesn't all know each other, because it handles the introductions nobody wants to do manually.

As the main event it falls apart. The cards run out of surprise much faster than you'd expect, and once your group has heard most of the deck there's nothing left to find.

## 4. Liar's Dice

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Liar_s_Dice_bjg847.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FBTGWGQQ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Everyone rolls five dice under a cup and looks at their own. Then you take turns making claims about how many of a given number are on the table across everyone's cups ("four fives") with each claim having to be higher than the last. At any point someone can call you a liar, cups come up, and whoever was wrong loses a die.

This is a pub game with centuries of history behind it, and there's a reason it survived: it's basically a conversation with dice as an excuse. The bidding is the game, and the bidding is talking.

It scales its own difficulty in a way that suits a drinking night. As players lose dice there's less information on the table, so the bluffs get wilder and the game accelerates toward an ending instead of dragging. Nobody has to decide when to stop playing. The game decides that for you.

The honest downside is that it's slightly more mental arithmetic than anything else on this list. Not much. But if your group is well past "a few drinks," Skull does a similar job with no numbers at all.

## 5. Cards Against Humanity ([Full Review Here](/cards-against-humanity-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Cards-Against-Humanity_ebqnyd.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004S8F7QM/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The obvious one. A judge plays a black prompt card, everyone else submits a white card to fill the blank, judge picks the funniest.

It earns its spot on a drinking list for a specific reason: it is the only game here that actively gets better as people get less inhibited. A sober Cards Against Humanity game is often a bit flat, with everyone playing the card they think is safe. Three drinks in, people play the card they actually think is funny, and the game finally works the way it's supposed to.

The drinking rule most groups use is that whoever's card doesn't get picked drinks, though we'd suggest the reverse — the winner drinks — because otherwise the least funny person at the table gets punished all night and stops enjoying themselves.

Its reputation has taken a hit over the years and some of that is deserved. The shock-value cards age badly and a lot of the deck is a punchline you've already heard. Know your group before you put it on the table.

## 6. Exploding Kittens ([Full Review Here](/exploding-kittens-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Exploding-kittens_lkkjfw.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B010TQY7A8/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Draw cards until someone pulls an exploding kitten and is out, unless they're holding a defuse. Everything else in the deck is a way to avoid drawing, make someone else draw, or peek at what's coming.

The rules are printed on the cards, which matters more than it sounds. Nobody has to remember anything, and when someone asks "what does this one do again?" for the fourth time, the answer is sitting right there in their hand.

Games take fifteen minutes, so being knocked out isn't really a punishment. You're back in on the next hand almost immediately, which beats a game where losing means sitting out for an hour watching everyone else have fun. Explode, drink. That's all the drinking layer it needs.

Two to five players is the real limit, and that's the thing that keeps it off the top of this list. There's an NSFW edition if that's your group, though it's the same game with ruder art.

## 7. Drink-A-Palooza

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/drink-a-palooza-v2.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B008RQ1PSK/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the maximalist option. Drink-A-Palooza is a board that strings together short versions of the classic drinking games (beer pong, flip cup, quarters, kings) and you collect bottle caps by winning them. Move around the board, land on a space, play whatever that space says.

If your group's idea of a good night is the party games from a college house rather than anything resembling a board game, this is the one to get. It packages the whole evening into a box so nobody has to organise anything.

It's also the most expensive thing here, it needs real table space, and it commits you to a direction. This isn't a game you play for twenty minutes and put away. Pulling it out is a decision about how the whole night is going to go, and it's genuinely built for drinking, so there's no sober version of this one to fall back on.

## 8. Bang! The Dice Game ([Full Review Here](/bang-the-dice-game-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Bang-The-Dice-Game_ydbqhk.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00DH8D0HC/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A western shootout with hidden roles. You're a Sheriff, Deputy, Outlaw or Renegade, only the Sheriff is public, and you roll dice to shoot the people next to you, take arrows, or heal. Figure out who's on your side while shooting at each other.

Rolling dice and shooting your friends is about the right cognitive load for a table three drinks deep. There's a hidden-role layer for anyone still paying attention, and pure dice chaos for anyone who isn't, and both groups can sit at the same table without either being bored.

It handles up to eight players, which makes it one of the better options here for a bigger group, and turns move fast because a turn is "roll dice, resolve, done."

The dice can be cruel. You can get shot out early through no fault of your own, which would annoy me in a serious games session. On a drinking night it doesn't matter, because the next game starts in ten minutes anyway.

## 9. Spot It!

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/spot-it.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07NQLG6B3/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Every card has eight symbols, and any two cards share exactly one match. Flip two cards, race to shout the match first. That's the whole game.

Spot It is here because it's the funniest thing on the list to watch other people play while drinking. Reaction time and visual processing are the first two things alcohol takes, and this game tests nothing else. Watching someone stare directly at the matching symbol and fail to produce the word is a kind of comedy you can't design on purpose.

It's also the answer to the awkward end of the night, when people are too far gone for anything with rules but nobody wants to leave yet. Last person to spot it drinks. Done.

The tin costs almost nothing and fits in a pocket, which makes it the one to bring to someone else's place. Eight players officially. Four or five in practice, before people start crowding the table and knocking things over.

## 10. One Night Ultimate Werewolf ([Full Review Here](/one-night-ultimate-werewolf-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/One_night_ultimate_werewolf_pvnehl.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HS7GG5G/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Werewolf with the boring part removed. One night phase, one day phase, one vote, ten minutes. A free phone app handles the narration, so nobody has to sit out as moderator, and nobody gets eliminated and forced to watch.

That second point is why this is on the list and regular Werewolf isn't. Games that knock people out leave them sitting there with nothing to do, and on a drinking night that means half the room drifts into the kitchen and never comes back.

The arguing is the game. Drinking makes people argue more confidently with worse reasoning, which is exactly the failure mode you want here. Someone commits hard to a theory built on nothing and then refuses to back down for a full three minutes.

It does ask for more focus than anything else on this list, though. There's a real hidden information puzzle underneath, and past a certain point it stops being a game and becomes ten people shouting over each other. Play it earlier in the evening.

## Conclusion

If you're only buying one game off this list, buy Skull. It's the best game here on its own merits, it costs less than most of the purpose-built drinking games, and it works whether your group is drinking or not, so it doesn't turn into a box you open twice a year and then feel guilty about.

If you specifically want something that does the drinking for you, Buzzed is the easy pick for a big group and a marked-up Jenga set is the cheap one for a small group.

The thing we keep noticing is that games designed around alcohol have a short shelf life, while the light social games stay in rotation for years and happen to survive a few drinks along the way. We'd build a shelf around the second kind.
