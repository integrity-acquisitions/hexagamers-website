The 1980s toy aisle had a very specific idea about what a board game should be, and that idea was: **it should have a large plastic thing in the middle of it.**

A volcano. A tower with a computer inside. A mall with a talking speaker. A castle you built out of bricks and then destroyed with a rubber-band catapult. The board was almost an afterthought — a surface to arrange the plastic thing on. And it worked, because a nine-year-old walking past a shelf does not evaluate elegant design. A nine-year-old sees a volcano that shoots marbles.

This is a list of those games. If you want the other side of the decade — the hidden movement games and dungeon crawls that the hobby still builds on — that's in [Best Board Games of the 80s](/best-board-games-of-the-80s/).

### A Note on Dates

Same rule as our [90s nostalgia list](/90s-nostalgia-board-games/): **everything here was released between 1980 and 1989.** That's stricter than most lists like this bother to be, and it means some regulars didn't make it.

Mouse Trap is 1963. Operation is 1965. Hungry Hungry Hippos is 1978. Guess Who? is 1979, agonisingly. Twister is 1966. They were all *everywhere* in the 80s, and none of them are from the 80s. The Omega Virus, which feels like it belongs here, is actually 1992.

## 1. Fireball Island (1986)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/fireball-island.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07GN1F11X/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The high water mark of the entire genre. If you owned this, you remember it.

The board is a moulded plastic island with a giant idol called Vul-Kar at the top. You're racing to steal his jewel and get back to the boat. When someone plays a fireball card, a marble comes rolling down channels cut into the island — and if it hits your explorer, you're knocked flat and lose your turn and possibly your jewel.

**The 3D board is the entire product and it is glorious.** Bridges, caves, ridges, three separate fireball entry points. Marbles genuinely rattling down a plastic mountain at your little plastic guy. Nothing about it is subtle and nothing about it needed to be.

Is it a good game? Not really. You roll, you move, you hope the marble misses. There's a bit of card management around when to launch a fireball, and that's about the extent of the decisions. It's a toy that keeps score.

**But here's the thing** — Restoration Games rebuilt it in 2018 as *Fireball Island: The Curse of Vul-Kar*, and they actually fixed the game underneath while keeping the marble chaos intact. You get a hand of cards, you choose your movement, there are real decisions now. It's the rare nostalgia reissue that's better than the thing you remember.

The Restoration version had a limited run and stock comes and goes, so grab it if you see it. Goliath also has a version called *Race to Adventure* in wider circulation.

## 2. Dark Tower (1981)

A plastic tower with a computer inside it, in 1981, sitting in the middle of a board game. It's hard to overstate how astonishing this was at the time.

You travelled between four kingdoms building an army, hunting three keys, and eventually assaulting the tower itself. Every single thing that happened was adjudicated by the tower — you pressed buttons, it displayed your army with tiny LED-lit illustrations on a rotating drum, it made noises, it decided whether the brigands robbed you. There was no rulebook arbitration because the tower simply told you what had happened.

**It's the direct ancestor of every app-driven board game on the shelf today**, and it got there forty-odd years early using a rotating disc and some light bulbs.

The game underneath is a fairly simple resource-gathering loop, and it's very luck-dependent. But the *presentation* was so far ahead of everything else that nobody cared. Also worth knowing: two inventors successfully sued Milton Bradley over it, winning a $737,000 judgment in 1985 after arguing the company had taken their prototype.

**Current status: the original is long gone**, and surviving towers frequently don't work — the electronics are over forty years old and the rotating drum mechanism is the first thing to fail. A non-functioning Dark Tower is a paperweight, because the tower *is* the game.

The good news is that Restoration Games made *Return to Dark Tower* in 2022, a full modern sequel with an app-connected electronic tower. It's a genuinely well-reviewed game rather than a nostalgia cash-in, and it's the sensible way to scratch this itch.

## 3. Trivial Pursuit (1981)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/trivial-pursuit.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01N4MXCK4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The biggest board game phenomenon of the decade, and arguably of all time outside Monopoly.

Two Canadian journalists invented it in 1979 after discovering pieces missing from their Scrabble set. It launched in 1981, sold slowly for two years, and then absolutely detonated — over 20 million copies in 1984 alone. For a stretch in the mid-80s it was simply what adults did at parties.

**The wedge is a perfect piece of design.** Six categories, six coloured wedges, a little plastic pie that fills up as you succeed. It's immediately legible, it's satisfying to physically click a piece into place, and everyone at the table can see exactly how everyone else is doing at a glance.

**It has aged badly in one specific way**, and it's worth being blunt about it: the original Genus edition's questions are from 1981. They assume you followed 1970s politics and sports. Playing the original box now is less a trivia game than a test of whether you were an adult during the Carter administration.

The Classic Edition currently in print keeps the look and updates the questions, which is the right call. There are also roughly nine hundred themed editions, of varying quality.

Still a solid pick for a mixed-age group where everyone knows different things. Just don't buy a 1980s copy off eBay expecting a fair fight.

## 4. Mall Madness (1988)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/mall-madness.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B083YKSNN8/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A talking electronic shopping mall, and an absolutely ruthless piece of 1988 marketing.

You race around a two-storey plastic mall buying six items off a shopping list and getting back to the parking lot first. The mall itself talks — a synthesised voice announcing sales, sending you to the ATM, telling you a store has a special. Prices change while you shop, so the store you were heading for might get more expensive before you arrive.

**The voice is the whole memory.** "Attention shoppers!" delivered in that flat 80s speech-synthesis tone. It's the same design instinct as Dream Phone a few years later — same designer, in fact, Michael Gray, who also did The Omega Virus.

As a game it's a roll-and-move with a random price generator attached. There's a small amount of routing strategy in what order you hit the stores, and that's genuinely it. It is not deep and was never trying to be.

What it does have is *pace*. Games are quick, the voice keeps the table moving, and the escalating panic as someone gets to five items is real.

Hasbro reissued it in 2020 with a 3D board and updated characters, and versions remain available. The electronics on vintage copies are a coin flip, so buy new unless you specifically want the old artwork.

## 5. Crossbows and Catapults (1983)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/crossbows-and-catapults.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CSLVLQP7/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Two castles made of plastic bricks, two rubber-band powered siege weapons, and one very simple objective: knock the other one down.

You build your fortification, you load a plastic disc into a catapult or crossbow, you pull it back, and you let fly. Hit a wall and bricks scatter. Hit a Viking and he's captured. There were caltrops and barricades and rules about what counted as a hit, and nobody who played this game has ever once consulted them.

**It's a physical skill game pretending to be a war game**, and the physical skill part is what makes it. Learning the arc of your catapult, figuring out that a low flat crossbow shot punches through a wall while a lobbed catapult disc scatters it, adjusting after every miss — that's a genuine learning curve, and it lives entirely in your hands.

The rebuild between rounds was half the appeal too. You'd lose a tower, then reconstruct your castle differently, trying to guess where your opponent would aim next.

**The eternal problem:** the discs go *everywhere*. Under the sofa, behind the radiator, into the dog. Every surviving copy is missing pieces, which is worth knowing before you buy a used one.

It's had several lives with different publishers — Lakeside, Tomy, Base Toys — and Goliath currently sells a version as *Crossbows & Catapults Castle Battle*. The 2007 reissue swapped Vikings for Orcs versus Knights. None of them quite match the original's brick quality, but the core still works.

## 6. Pictionary (1985)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pictionary.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07STLTFHG/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The one game on this list that's a legitimately great design, and it got made because a waiter kept beating his friends at charades with a pencil.

Rob Angel wrote out the first word lists on a typewriter and self-published 1,000 copies in 1985. By 1987 it was the best-selling game in America. The idea is so simple it's almost invisible: draw the word, your team guesses, no letters and no numbers.

**What makes it good is the category system.** Person/Place/Animal, Object, Action, Difficult, and the All Play. The categories tell you roughly what kind of thing you're looking at, which stops guessing from being pure noise, and the All Play — where every team draws simultaneously — is where the actual chaos lives.

It also has the rarest quality in a party game: **being bad at it is fun.** A terrible drawing generates more table energy than a good one. The person who can actually draw is often at a disadvantage, because they spend forty seconds on a beautiful horse while someone else scribbles four lines and gets "horse" in three seconds.

Still in print through Mattel, still cheap, still works at any gathering with more than four people. Of everything on this list, it's the one most likely to earn a place on your shelf today rather than in a memory.

## 7. Girl Talk (1988)

Truth or dare in a box, and a genuine cultural artifact of the late 80s.

You spin, you draw a card, and you either answer a question about boys and secrets and embarrassing moments or you perform a dare. Fail, and you put a zit sticker on your face. That's the punishment mechanic: visible plastic acne.

**The zit stickers are why anyone remembers this game.** As a design choice for a game marketed at teenage girls it's remarkable, and it says a great deal about 1988 that nobody involved appears to have paused over it.

Underneath the packaging it's a conversation game, and conversation games work. The fortune cards — where you'd find out your future husband and how many kids you'd have — were basically a paper version of MASH, and the actual play was mostly an excuse for a group of friends to talk. Which is what a sleepover game should be.

It was invented by Catherine Rondeau and first published by Western Publishing in 1988, then picked up and redesigned by Milton Bradley in 1995, which is why plenty of people file it under 90s. The 1988 original is the real one.

**Current status: gone**, and unlikely to return in this form. Used copies circulate but the question cards are extremely of their era. Read a few before handing it to a modern ten-year-old.

## Where to Start

Honestly? **Pictionary.** It's the only game here we'd recommend on merit alone, it costs almost nothing, and it will improve a party tonight.

If you want the nostalgia hit with a game attached, **Fireball Island: The Curse of Vul-Kar** is the pick. Restoration Games did the rare thing and made the reissue genuinely better than the original, and the marbles still rattle down the mountain exactly the way you remember.

For the electronic-tower experience, don't chase a 1981 **Dark Tower**. Buy **Return to Dark Tower** instead and get a working machine and a modern game.

And the decade that followed took everything in a completely different direction — quieter, cleverer, and much less plastic. That's over in [90s Nostalgia Board Games](/90s-nostalgia-board-games/) and [Best Board Games of the 90s](/best-board-games-of-the-90s/).
