Walk down the aisle at a game shop and you'll spot three boxes with "Century" on the spine (Spice Road, Eastern Wonders, A New World), plus a fourth called Golem Edition covered in glowing crystals. Are they sequels? Expansions? The same game four times? None of the labels tell you.

Here's the short version. They're three standalone games that share one core idea, and they're built to snap together. You can play any one on its own, combine two of them, or run all three at once. That's seven different ways to play out of three boxes. The Golem stuff is a separate wrinkle we'll get to.

We spent a while sorting out how this all fits together so you don't have to. Below we break down each game, explain what actually happens when you combine them, and give you a straight answer on which version (or combination) is worth your money.

## The One Idea Behind All Three

Every Century game runs on the same engine. You have four goods of increasing value. Think of them as a ladder, where the cheapest sits at the bottom and the most valuable at the top. You collect the cheap stuff, then trade it up the ladder for better stuff, and cash certain combinations in for point cards. First to a set number of points triggers the end.

That's it. That's the spine of all three. What changes is *how* you do the trading and upgrading:

- **Spice Road** is a card game. You build a little hand of trade and upgrade cards and chain them together.
- **Eastern Wonders** puts you on a board, sailing a ship between islands to trigger those same trades.
- **A New World** is worker placement. You drop markers on a shared board to claim the trades before anyone else does.

Same destination, three different roads. If you've played one and liked the puzzle, the other two will feel familiar in the best way while still asking something new of you.

Now the games themselves.

This is the one that started it all, and it's the easiest to teach. On your turn you do one of four things: play a card, acquire a new card from the row in the middle, claim a point card, or pick your played cards back up to use again. Your spice cubes sit in a little bowl, and you're constantly converting the yellow ones up into red, brown, and eventually green, the good stuff.

The pleasure here is the combo. You start with two weak cards and slowly acquire better ones, and there's a real moment where your hand clicks and you fire off a chain that turns a pile of cheap cubes into exactly what a point card wants. It plays fast, teaches in five minutes, and the cubes-in-bowls presentation is tidy and satisfying.

**Likes:**

- Teaches in about five minutes, plays in thirty
- The card-chaining "engine" moment is genuinely satisfying to pull off
- Small footprint that fits on a coffee table with room to spare
- Scales cleanly from 2 to 5

**Dislikes:**

- Player interaction is thin; you're mostly racing solitaire
- The base cubes and bowls are functional but a little plain (Golem fixes this, more below)
- Late game can come down to who drew into the right upgrade cards

  <div class="stat"><span class="stat-value">30 – 45 min</span><span class="stat-label">Play Time</span></div>
  <div class="stat"><span class="stat-value">8+</span><span class="stat-label">Age</span></div>
</div>

The sequel takes the same trade-up puzzle and drops it onto a modular island board. Instead of playing cards, you sail your ship from island to island. Each island triggers a trade or upgrade, and landing on a spot where another player's ship already sits costs you extra, so movement and blocking suddenly matter.

There's a second layer: you can build trading posts on the islands, which lock in bonuses for the rest of the game and score at the end. That pushes you to think about where you want to plant roots early versus staying nimble. It's a meatier game than Spice Road, with more to weigh each turn, and the route-planning gives it a spatial feel the card game doesn't have.

**Likes:**

- Route planning and ship movement add a spatial puzzle Spice Road lacks
- Trading posts create a real "where do I commit?" decision
- Blocking opponents' islands introduces actual player interaction
- Modular board changes the map between plays

**Dislikes:**

- Heavier to teach and slower to play than Spice Road
- Bigger table footprint
- The extra rules can feel fiddly for a game that's still fairly light overall

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/century-a-new-world.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07PKDF6WT/?tag=hexagamers-20)

</div>

<div class="game-stats">
  <div class="stat"><span class="stat-value">2 – 4</span><span class="stat-label">Players</span></div>
  <div class="stat"><span class="stat-value">30 – 45 min</span><span class="stat-label">Play Time</span></div>
  <div class="stat"><span class="stat-value">8+</span><span class="stat-label">Age</span></div>
</div>

The third game reworks the formula as worker placement. You place your explorer figures on a shared board to claim trades and upgrades, and here's the key twist: a spot's cost depends on how many pieces are already there. Land on a busy space and you pay more figures to use it. So you're constantly reading the board, trying to grab what you need before it gets crowded and expensive.

Of the three, this is the most interactive. Everyone's fighting over the same board, and the "it costs more when it's crowded" rule means you feel your opponents' choices on every turn. It still plays in well under an hour, and it's arguably the most approachable of the three for people who already know worker placement games.

**Likes:**

- Most player interaction of the three; you're all fighting over the same spaces
- The crowding-cost rule creates constant, readable tension
- Fast for a worker placement game
- A natural pick if your group already loves the genre

**Dislikes:**

- Least distinctive on its own; it's "Century, but worker placement"
- The board can feel tight at higher player counts
- Some randomness in which point cards come out

## Century: Golem Edition (The Pretty Reskin)

<div class="century-box">

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/century-golem-edition.webp)](https://www.amazon.ca/dp/B079DDN9SR/?tag=hexagamers-20)

</div>

Here's where people get confused. Century: Golem Edition is not a fourth game in the story. It's Spice Road with a full fantasy makeover. Same rules, same card effects, same turns. What changed is the skin: instead of trading spice cubes, you're trading glowing crystals to feed golems, and the artwork is a big step up. The crystals look better than the cubes, the card art is more vivid, and even the bowls are shaped to hold the pieces more snugly.

So if the mechanics are identical, which do you buy? For most people, **Golem Edition is the better version of Spice Road.** It's the same great game with nicer components and art, and it's often the easier one to find in stock. There's one real catch: the standalone Golem Edition uses different crystal colors than the numbered Century games, so **it does not combine with Spice Road's sequels.** If your plan is to eventually own and combine the trilogy, you want the original Spice Road cubes, not the Golem crystals.

Plan B did release Golem-themed versions of the other two games as well. Golem: Eastern Mountains is the Eastern Wonders equivalent and Golem: An Endless World is the A New World equivalent, so the Golem line has its own matching trilogy if you'd rather collect the prettier versions across the board. Just don't mix a Golem box with a numbered box and expect them to combine.

## Combining the Games

This is the part that makes the series special, and the part the boxes explain worst. Each game past the first ships with extra rules and components to combine with what came before. Put together, you get seven ways to play: each of the three on its own, each pair, and all three at once.

### Spice Road + Eastern Wonders

This is the flagship combo, and it even has its own name: **From Sand to Sea.** The rules and pieces to run it live in the Eastern Wonders box. You end up sailing the ship *and* playing cards. You can acquire cards, play them for actions, move your ship (paying with cards instead of cubes), or rest to recover your hand. It blends the card-chaining of Spice Road with the spatial planning of Eastern Wonders into something richer than either alone. If you only ever try one combination, make it this one.

### Spice Road + A New World

The A New World box includes rules to fold Spice Road into its worker-placement board. You get the card engine from the first game layered onto the "claim it before it gets crowded" tension of the third. It's a solid pairing that leans more toward direct competition than From Sand to Sea does, since the worker board is where the friction lives.

### Eastern Wonders + A New World

Combining the two board games gives you the heaviest of the three pairs: ship movement and trading posts on one side, worker placement and crowding costs on the other. It's the least talked-about combination and the most demanding to set up, so it's really one for people who already love both games and want to see how far the system stretches.

### All Three Together

Run everything at once and the series turns into a full-sized worker-placement game that pulls the ship movement, card play, and placement mechanics into one board. It's the biggest, longest, most involved version of Century, and honestly, more game than most groups will reach for on a casual night. It's a fun "let's see what this thing can do" experiment more than a regular go-to, but the fact that three light games stack into one meaty one is a genuinely clever bit of design.

If you want everything in a single package, Plan B put out a **Century: Big Box** that bundles all three games together. It's the tidiest way to own the whole system if you already know you want the full set.

## Which Version Should You Buy?

Most people don't need all seven modes. Here's how we'd actually spend the money.

**If you want one great game and nothing else:** get **Golem Edition.** It's Spice Road with better art and components, it teaches in minutes, and it's the smoothest entry point to the whole style. Just know it won't combine with the sequels.

**If you think you might collect and combine the series:** start with the original **Spice Road** (the cubes, not the Golem crystals), then add **Eastern Wonders** for the From Sand to Sea combo. That pairing is the sweet spot: two good standalone games that become a third, better one together, without the setup burden of running all three.

**If your group loves worker placement:** **A New World** is the most interactive on its own and the most immediately grabby for that crowd.

**If you want the whole system in one shot:** the **Big Box** is the cleanest buy, with every game and every combination in one box on the shelf.

The one combo we'd steer you toward trying is From Sand to Sea. It's the clearest example of why this series exists: three small, breezy games that were designed from the start to become something bigger.

## Conclusion

Century is a rare thing: a family of light games that actually improve when you snap them together, without any of them feeling like an unfinished half. For most people, Golem Edition alone is all the Century they'll ever need. For anyone who catches the bug, Spice Road plus Eastern Wonders is the combination that shows off what the whole system is really about. Buy in wherever fits your table, and know that the ladder up to the full set is there whenever you want it.
