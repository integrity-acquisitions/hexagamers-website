A dungeon crawl is a specific thing, and we're being strict about it here. You've got a party of characters, a space that gets revealed room by room instead of all at once, and monsters standing between you and whatever you came for. That's different from the open-world exploration in our [Best Adventure Board Games](/best-adventure-board-games/) list — this is tighter, more tactical, and almost always built around combat as the core loop rather than a piece of it.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](#best-dungeon-crawl-board-games-comparison-table)

Some of these are pure hack-and-slash with barely a story attached. Others carry a full campaign with characters that level up scenario to scenario. What they share is the room-by-room reveal and the promise that the next door might be the one that ends your run.

If you want the wider genre — sailing, questing, and campaigns that leave the dungeon behind entirely — check our [Best Adventure Board Games](/best-adventure-board-games/) list instead.

## 1. Gloomhaven ([Is It Worth It?](/is-gloomhaven-worth-it/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Gloomhaven_tvaycr.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H4HZ1JCJ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

We've already got a full piece on whether this one's worth the money, so we'll keep this short. As a pure crawler, the combat system is still the best argument for the whole genre: a hand of cards replaces dice, so every attack is a choice about whether to burn a strong card now or save it for later in the scenario. That tension never lets up.

Dozens of dungeons, a roster of classes that play nothing like each other, and a campaign that remembers every choice your party made. It's also a monster to store and set up, which is the honest tradeoff for everything it does well.

If you want the deepest tactical crawler on the market and you're willing to commit the shelf space, this is still the one everyone else gets measured against.

## 2. Descent: Legends of the Dark

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/descent-legends-of-the-dark.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08LVZ71JW/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is what happens when a companion app runs the entire dungeon-master side of a crawler. No one at the table needs to read a monster's AI script or track initiative by hand — the app does it, and it does it well enough that this plays closer to a video game co-op session than a board game with a rulebook.

The heroes still level up across a full campaign, and the app unlocks new mechanics and story beats as you go, so the game itself teaches you as you play rather than front-loading fifty pages of rules. Combat is fast because the bookkeeping is gone; you're making decisions, not managing a spreadsheet.

It needs a phone or tablet at the table, which is a dealbreaker for some groups and a non-issue for others. For anyone who wants the crawler experience without the setup overhead, this is the smoothest one on the list.

## 3. Zombicide: Black Plague

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/zombicide-black-plague.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B011MIVWV4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Zombicide takes the modern zombie-survival version of the game and drops it into a medieval fantasy dungeon, and the fantasy reskin actually makes it a tighter fit for this list than the original. You're a small band of survivors clearing tile-by-tile rooms while zombie hordes spawn faster than you can kill them.

The escalation is the whole game. Early rooms are manageable, but every noisy action draws more zombies, so the tension builds from "we've got this" to "we need to leave right now" inside a single session. It rewards loud, aggressive play just as much as careful play, which keeps every session feeling different.

This is also one of the more approachable minis-heavy crawlers to actually get to the table. Rules are light, turns move fast, and a single scenario wraps in about an hour, so it's easy to fit into a regular game night instead of clearing a whole evening.

## 4. Warhammer Quest: Blackstone Fortress

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/warhammer-quest-blackstone-fortress.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07K8XXXZ4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Games Workshop's take on the crawler swaps the usual fantasy dungeon for a derelict space hulk, and the shift in setting changes the mood more than you'd expect. You're not clearing rooms of goblins, you're picking through a wreck that's been abandoned for centuries, not knowing what's still alive in it.

What sets this one apart mechanically is that it plays without a dedicated Game Master. The dungeon itself generates procedurally as you explore, using a deck of room and event cards, so the whole party is discovering the space together instead of one player running it against the rest.

The miniatures are genuinely excellent, which matters if that's part of why you play these games in the first place. It's a smaller box than most crawlers on this list, and a good pick if your group wants sci-fi dread over fantasy combat.

## 5. Too Many Bones

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/too-many-bones.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08HY7969G/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Too Many Bones throws out the usual dungeon tiles and movement grid entirely. Combat plays out on a neoprene mat with oversized poker chips standing in for dice, and your character build comes from stacking ability chips you unlock as you level up between fights.

The result feels less like exploring a dungeon and more like building a fighter and then throwing them at a series of increasingly nasty boss encounters. Each of the playable Gearlocs plays completely differently, so a second campaign with a different character barely resembles the first one.

The component quality is a real selling point here — the chips have genuine heft and the production is a step above most of the genre. It's a pricier entry, but if your group is bored of tile-by-tile movement and wants a crawler built entirely around character building and boss fights, this is the one.

## 6. Sword & Sorcery: Ancient Chronicles

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/sword-and-sorcery.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08R6GSRX7/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Ancient Chronicles leans hardest into monster AI of anything on this list. Every enemy type behaves according to its own decision tree, so a room of skeletons reacts differently than a room of wolves, and positioning against them actually matters instead of just being flavor.

The campaign structure ties everything together across linked scenarios, with your heroes carrying gear, wounds, and growth from one fight into the next. It reads dense on the shelf, and it is — this is one of the heavier crawlers here — but the AI system means you're not spending your session flipping through a rulebook to figure out what a monster does next.

If your group likes the tactical crunch of a wargame more than the narrative pacing of something like Gloomhaven, this is worth the setup time.

## 7. Massive Darkness 2: Hellscape

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/massive-darkness-2.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09KC7978S/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the crawler for a group that wants the loot to matter as much as the fighting. Massive Darkness 2 leans hard into gear drops and character progression — you're constantly swapping in better weapons and armor, and the difference between an early-game and late-game hero is dramatic.

It plays in the classic dungeon-tile style, room by room, with a genuine horde of monster miniatures on the table at once. The light-and-dark mechanic changes what enemies can see and do depending on whether a room is lit, which adds a layer most straightforward crawlers don't bother with.

It's a miniatures-forward game through and through, so expect a lot of setup and a lot of table space. For a group that likes the "kill things, take their stuff" loop above all else, this delivers it without much narrative weight slowing things down.

## 8. Mansions of Madness

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/mansions-of-madness.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01J4NB6CO/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Mansions of Madness swaps the usual monster-slaying focus for Lovecraftian dread, and it's the pick on this list for a group that wants tension over combat crunch. An app runs the mystery, the map, and the enemies, revealing rooms and clues as your investigators explore a haunted house that's actively working against them.

The horror lands because the app can hide information from the players the way a human GM would — a sound cue, a room that changes when you're not looking, a monster that might not even be real. That unpredictability is hard to replicate in a game with a fixed rulebook and open information.

Sessions run long, closer to a full evening than a quick game night filler, and the theme is genuinely unsettling rather than cartoonish. If your group wants dread instead of dice-chucking, this is the standout.

## 9. Dungeons & Dragons: Adventure Begins

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/dungeons-and-dragons-adventure-begins.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B083YL5VCY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Every crawler on this list assumes some tolerance for setup time and rules overhead. This one doesn't. Adventure Begins strips the dungeon crawl down to its simplest form — move through rooms, fight monsters, find treasure — and teaches the whole thing in about ten minutes.

It's built as a genuine entry point into the D&D board game line and, honestly, the genre as a whole. The rules are light enough for a family with younger kids, but the core loop of exploring a space you haven't seen and hoping the next room isn't a monster closet is fully intact.

This is the one to hand someone who says dungeon crawlers sound intimidating. It won't hold a veteran group's attention for long, but it's the cheapest and fastest way to find out if the genre clicks for your table before spending real money on something like Gloomhaven.

## Conclusion

If your group is brand new to this genre, start with Dungeons & Dragons: Adventure Begins or Zombicide: Black Plague — both teach fast and won't burn a whole evening on setup. If you've already got a taste for it and want the deepest possible commitment, Gloomhaven is still the benchmark, with Too Many Bones and Sword & Sorcery close behind for groups that want more character-building crunch. And if what you actually want is dread over combat, skip straight to Mansions of Madness.
