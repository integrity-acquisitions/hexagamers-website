Most "games that teach probability" lists are really just lists of games with dice in the box. Rolling a die is not a probability lesson. Deciding whether to roll it again, knowing what's already gone and what's left, is.

That's the filter for this list. Every game below asks you to weigh a real chance against a real cost, more than once, with information you can actually use. Not "roll and hope." Actual expected-value thinking, whether or not the player at the table would ever call it that.

We've touched on this before in our [best educational board games](/best-educational-board-games/) roundup and our piece on [games that teach maths skills](/top-rated-board-games-that-teach-math-skills/). This list narrows in on probability specifically, and it skews slightly older and sharper than those two, because the games that do this well tend to reward the kind of player who's paying attention to what's already been drawn or rolled.

## How We Picked These

**The decision has to be repeatable.** One big gamble at the end of a game isn't a probability lesson, it's a coin flip. We wanted games where you make the same kind of bet ten or twenty times a session, so the odds actually start to mean something instead of just being luck either way.

**You need enough information to be right.** Pure dice games where nothing is visible or countable didn't make the cut. The games here give you something to track: cards already played, dice already rolled, a shrinking pool of possibilities.

**Losing to the odds has to feel earned, not random.** A good probability game makes you nod when you bust, not throw the box across the room. If the correct play and the losing play look identical from the outside, that's a sign the maths was actually working.

## 1. Zombie Dice

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Zombie_Dice_cff5vz.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B003IKMR0U/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Thirteen dice in a cup, three colours, and a stack of brain, shotgun and footprint faces in different ratios per colour. You roll three, keep the brains, re-roll the footprints, and stop whenever you want. Three shotguns and you lose everything from that turn.

This is the cleanest probability game we know of, because the entire game is one decision made over and over: do I roll again. Green dice are mostly brains, red dice are mostly shotguns, and once a few dice have come up you can actually count what's left in the cup. Good players are quietly tracking colour ratios. Everyone else is just pushing their luck and hoping.

The tension is real because the bust is total. You don't lose a little for pushing too far, you lose the entire turn's brains, and that all-or-nothing swing is what makes people groan out loud when they go one roll too far.

It's a five-minute rules explanation and it plays in a round or two per player, so it works as a warm-up as easily as a whole session. The zombie theming is thin dressing on top of a genuinely sharp mechanic, and we mean that as a compliment.

## 2. Deep Sea Adventure

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Deep_Sea_Adventure_ud2j6h.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DKP7Q966/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A shared submarine with one shared oxygen tank. Divers move along a tile path picking up treasure, and every treasure you're carrying adds weight that costs you extra oxygen on the way back. Run out of air before you're home, and you lose everything you're holding.

What earns this a spot is that the risk isn't abstract, it's arithmetic you're doing in your head in real time. Every extra tile of treasure is one more space you have to move, which is oxygen you and everyone else in the sub are burning. You watch the shared tank tick down and have to decide, turn by turn, whether the gem two tiles ahead is worth the walk back.

It's also one of the few games here where your risk directly taxes other players, since the tank is shared. That turns a personal probability call into a social one, and our group has had genuine arguments over someone pushing one tile too deep and stranding everybody.

Small box, quick teach, and it plays fast enough to run it back immediately after a bad round, which you will want to.

## 3. King of Tokyo

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/king-of-tokyo.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01F46RPS4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Kaiju monsters smashing Tokyo, six dice per turn, keep-and-reroll Yahtzee style. Claws deal damage, hearts heal, numbers score points, and three of the same number scores big.

The probability lesson here sits inside a bigger game, which is what makes it interesting. You're not just chasing the best dice result, you're weighing it against position. Sitting in Tokyo scores points every turn but takes damage from everyone outside it, so the odds question becomes whether you can survive long enough for the points to matter, not just whether you rolled well.

Six dice with two rerolls gives you a genuinely wide spread of outcomes to plan around, and experienced players visibly shift strategy mid-roll once they see what's locked in versus what's still live. That's the same thinking as holding dice in Yahtzee, but wrapped in a theme kids actually ask for by name.

Fifteen minutes to teach, and it comfortably handles six players without anyone waiting long between turns, which is rarer than it should be at this player count.

## 4. Dragonwood

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/dragonwood.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00UB7OV7K/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Collect cards, then spend sets of them as dice to capture creatures. Each creature has a target number, and you choose your attack type: a run, a matching set, or a single colour, each giving a different number and size of dice to roll.

This is the most direct probability lesson on the list, and it doesn't feel like one while you're playing it. To beat a target of twelve you're deciding whether four dice averaging 3.5 gets you there more reliably than three dice against a lower target elsewhere on the board. Nobody at the table thinks of that as maths. They think of it as deciding whether to risk the fight.

The feedback loop is immediate too. Gamble on a long shot, come up short, lose the cards you spent. A few rounds of that teaches expected value faster than being told about it ever will, and we've watched it happen to actual kids at actual tables.

Dice still mean luck can bite a good decision, and it's worth saying that out loud before the first game. For some players that's the whole appeal. For others it's the one frustration worth flagging in advance.

## 5. Sushi Go!

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Sushi-Go_ocbhis.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00J57VU44/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Pick a card from your hand, pass the rest, repeat until the cards run out. Different sushi scores in different ways, sets of sashimi, pairs of tempura, dumplings that get better the more you stack.

No dice anywhere in the box, which is exactly why it earns a place here. The probability is about information, not randomness. You saw the full hand before you passed it on, so you know roughly what's still coming back to you and what your neighbour just pulled out. Deciding whether to hold out for a third sashimi, worth nothing at two, is a real expected-value call made on incomplete information.

That's a more honest kind of probability than a die roll, because you can actually improve at it. Repeated plays teach you to track what's likely still in the pool, and the difference between a new player and an experienced one shows up immediately in which cards they hold onto.

It's the cheapest, smallest game on this list, and it travels anywhere. The only catch is scoring needs an adult or a rules-confident player for the first game or two, since six or seven card types all score differently.

## 6. No Thanks!

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/No_Thanks_ipxgsz.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B013FAC4FK/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Cards numbered 3 to 35, one flipped at a time. On your turn, take the card and its pile of chips, or pay one of your own chips to pass it along. Cards score against you, but a run of consecutive numbers only counts the lowest one.

The whole game is a running probability estimate with a visible countdown. Nine cards get removed from the deck at the start, unseen, and thirty-three of the thirty-eight actually get played. That means you're constantly recalculating your odds of a specific number showing up as more cards flip, and a card sitting next to one you already own gets dramatically less scary the longer you can afford to wait it out.

It's the purest chip-management probability game on this list, and also the simplest to teach. One sentence covers the whole rule: take it, or pay to skip it. Everything interesting happens in the decision, not the rulebook.

Where it can drag is that it plays best with fewer chips in circulation than the box suggests for larger groups, since a table flush with chips loses the tension that makes the game work. We'd stick to five players or fewer for the sharpest version of it.

## 7. Coup

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Coup-1_mcfcul.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00GDI4HX4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Fifteen character cards, three of each, two dealt to each player face down. Claim any character's power on your turn whether you have it or not, and get challenged if someone doesn't believe you.

We've written about Coup as a [bluffing game](/best-bluffing-board-games/) before, and the bluffing is the headline, but underneath it is a running probability calculation that sharp players are doing constantly. Fifteen cards, three of each, and you can see your own two plus whatever's been revealed through failed challenges or kills. That tells you, at any moment, roughly how many of a given character are still unaccounted for and how likely a claim is to be real.

Calling someone out isn't a vibe check if you're doing it right. It's "there are three Dukes, I'm holding one, one's already been shown dead, so the odds this person actually has one just dropped." Most players never do that maths consciously, but the ones who consistently call correct bluffs usually are, even if they'd describe it as a read on the person instead.

That said, it's genuinely a social game first. The probability layer rewards attentive players without punishing anyone who's just there to bluff and laugh, which is a hard balance to hit and one of the reasons it's stayed in regular rotation for us.

## 8. Catan

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Catan-1.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DYK1ZH2D/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Two dice, rolled every turn by whoever's turn it is, and every hex tile with a matching number produces resources for anyone touching it. Build roads, settlements and cities from those resources to reach ten points first.

We've gone deep on [Catan strategy](/settlers-catan-strategies/) before, and the probability grounding underneath all of it is the dot distribution on the number tokens. Six and eight have the most dots, meaning they're statistically the most likely rolls with two dice, and two or twelve are near-useless because they need a specific pair of ones. That's not flavour text, it's the entire logic of where you place your first two settlements.

What makes Catan a genuine probability lesson rather than just a dice game is that the odds are visible on the board the whole time, printed right on the tiles as dots. New players place on whatever resources sound good. Experienced players are silently doing a bell curve in their head before they've placed a single piece, chasing the middle numbers and avoiding the edges.

It won't click for everyone, and a bad run of rolls can sour a game even for a player who placed correctly, which is worth setting expectations around before your first play. We cover that tension more in our [full Catan review](/catan-review/).

## 9. Yahtzee

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/yahtzee.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00000IWH6/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Five dice, three rolls per turn, keep whatever you want between rolls. Thirteen categories to fill across the game, each scoring differently, and you have to use every category exactly once.

The oldest game on this list by decades, and still one of the sharpest probability teaches because the decision repeats thirteen times a game with a clear, learnable structure each time. Which dice to hold after the first roll is a genuine probability question, not a gut call: going for a full house with two pairs already showing is a different bet than chasing a large straight with one gap in the middle, and the odds are different enough that it's worth actually thinking through rather than just re-rolling everything.

The category system adds a second layer most push-your-luck games don't have. Late in the game you're not just optimizing the roll in front of you, you're weighing it against which empty category is most likely to go unfilled otherwise, so a mediocre roll late on can be worth locking in over gambling for something better and risking a zero.

It's cheap, it's been in production since the 1950s, and it plays with a huge range of ages at once because the maths scales with how deep a player wants to think about it. A kid can play it as "which numbers matched," and an adult at the same table can be running actual probabilities in their head.

## Which One Should You Get?

**If you want the purest probability lesson with the least game around it:** Zombie Dice. It's one decision, repeated, and you can watch someone's grasp of it improve within a single session.

**For a group that likes a bit of theme and monster-smashing with the maths:** King of Tokyo. The dice logic is the same skeleton as Yahtzee, dressed up in something a reluctant player will actually ask to play again.

**For card-counting over dice-rolling:** No Thanks! or Sushi Go!, depending on whether your table prefers a tense single mechanic or a lighter, faster one.

**For a game that hides the lesson inside something else entirely:** Coup. Nobody sits down to learn probability from a bluffing game, and that's exactly why it works.

**For the one everybody already owns:** Catan. The lesson has been sitting on the board the whole time, printed as dots on the tiles, and most players never notice until someone points it out.
