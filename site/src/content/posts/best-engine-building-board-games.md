There's a specific turn in an engine builder where the game stops being work and starts being fun.

You've spent forty minutes taking small, boring actions. Two coins here, one card there. Then you play something, and it triggers the thing you set up six turns ago, which triggers the thing you set up three turns before that, and suddenly you're doing four things on a turn where everyone else is doing one. That cascade is the whole genre. Everything before it is you paying for it.

Kaitlyn calls this "the turn where Ryan goes quiet." She's not wrong. I once spent a full two minutes resolving a single Terraforming Mars turn while three people waited, and I'd do it again.

This is our all-time list, not a 2026 list. Engine builders age unusually well. Dominion is from 2008 and still the cleanest version of the idea anyone has shipped, so a recency cutoff would have thrown out most of the good ones for no reason.

## What Counts as an Engine Builder?

An engine building game is one where the things you acquire make your future turns better, rather than just scoring points. You're not collecting victory points early. You're building the machine that collects them later.

That's a mechanic, not a genre, which is why this list overlaps with a few others on the site. Deck builders are engine builders where the engine happens to be your deck. Plenty of worker placement games have an engine bolted underneath. We included games where building the machine is the *main event* and left out games where it's a side dish.

Engines come in three rough shapes, and knowing which one you like saves you money:

- **Tableau engines** — you lay cards in front of you and they combo off each other. Wingspan, Everdell, Terraforming Mars.
- **Deck engines** — the cards you buy get shuffled into your deck and come back around. Dominion.
- **Economic engines** — you're raising your production *rate* rather than chaining triggers. Splendor, Scythe.

Tableau engines give you the most visible payoff, because the combo is sitting right there on the table. Economic engines are subtler and usually shorter.

**One warning before the list.** Engine builders have a known failure mode: you build a beautiful machine, admire it, and lose. The game ends on a timer that does not care whether you're finished. Knowing when to stop building and start cashing in is the actual skill in every game here, and it's the thing new players get wrong.

## 1. Wingspan ([Full Review Here](/wingspan-review/))

[![Wingspan](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/wingspan.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The friendliest engine builder made, and the one we hand to people who've never heard the phrase "engine builder."

You're attracting birds to three habitats. Each bird you play makes the row it sits in stronger, so when you take that row's action later in the game, you're triggering everything you already placed there. Take the food action in a row with four birds and you're not taking one action, you're taking five. The engine is *visible*. It's a line of cards left to right, and you can see it getting longer.

That visibility is why it works on beginners. In a lot of engine builders you have to trust that the machine is coming together. In Wingspan you watch it happen.

**The honest caveat:** it's close to multiplayer solitaire. You're barely interacting with anyone, and a few people in our group find that a bit lonely. Everyone else finds it relaxing, which is the same fact viewed from a different chair. Know which kind of player you're buying for.

The solo automa is one of the best in the hobby, too, and it plays almost identically to the multiplayer game rather than being an afterthought.

## 2. Terraforming Mars

[![Terraforming Mars](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/terraforming-mars.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01GSYA4K2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If Wingspan is the gentle version, this is the genre at full power. Corporations competing to make Mars habitable: raising the temperature, adding oxygen, dropping oceans on the map.

The card play is the reason it's here and the reason people forgive everything else. You draft project cards, and the tableau you assemble by the end can chain into things that feel broken. Your production goes up on five tracks at once, cards discount other cards, and a turn that took ten seconds in the first generation takes two minutes in the last. It's the cleanest example of the early-slow, late-explosive arc that defines the whole category.

**It is not a pretty game.** The production is famously plain, the player boards are flat cardboard that everything slides around on, and the table footprint is enormous. Nobody who loves it cares. If component quality is what sells your group, this is a hard sell, and a player mat upgrade is close to mandatory.

Two hours is realistic, and longer with five. Best at three.

## 3. Dominion ([Full Review Here](/dominion-review/))

[![Dominion](https://img.hexagamers.com/Dominion_z98xnd.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LYLIS2U/?tag=hexagamers-20)

My first impression was fear — no board, tons of different cards, what did I get myself into? That passed after one play, and seventeen years later this is still the purest engine in the hobby.

Here the engine is your deck. You start with ten deliberately terrible cards, buy better ones from a shared market, and they get shuffled in to come around again. Which means every purchase is a bet on a future shuffle. The first six turns are genuinely unsatisfying, and then your first real card comes back around and the thing starts breathing.

The move that separates good players from new ones is **trashing**. New players buy more cards. Good players remove the bad ones, because a deck of eight great cards beats a deck of twenty decent ones. That single idea is the entire skill curve, and it took our group about five games to figure out.

Ten kingdom cards from a big box every game, so setups almost never repeat. Thirty minutes, teaches in ten. It's the one to learn the genre on.

The catch: the expansions are excellent and there are a lot of them. Budget accordingly. More on how the mechanic works in our [guide to deck building games](/guide-to-understanding-deck-building-games/).

## 4. Everdell ([Full Review Here](/everdell-review/))

[![Everdell](https://img.hexagamers.com/Everdell_x5mxlz.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09ZWQTTNR/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The prettiest game most people own, and there's a genuinely clever puzzle underneath the woodland critters and the giant cardboard tree.

You're building a city of fifteen cards using workers and two resources tracks, and the trick is that cards get cheaper when you already have the right card in play, because construction cards let you play their matching critter for free. Chaining those pairs is where the engine lives. Play the Farm, get the Husband and Wife discount, and suddenly you're placing cards you shouldn't be able to afford yet.

**The seasons are the clock.** You move through four seasons, and each transition gives you more workers but also brings the end closer. Advancing too early wastes turns you'd already paid for; advancing too late leaves resources on the table. That decision is the game.

Honest note: the tree is decorative. It holds cards, it looks incredible, and it does nothing mechanically. Worth knowing before you pay for the deluxe version. It still gets more "what is *that*" reactions than anything else on our shelf, which is worth something on its own.

If you're weighing this against the obvious alternative, we broke it down in [Wingspan vs Everdell](/wingspan-vs-everdell/).

## 5. Splendor ([Full Review Here](/splendor-review/))

[![Splendor](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/splendor.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00IZEUFIA/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The smallest, fastest engine on this list, and the one we've taught to the most people.

You take gem chips and spend them on cards. Every card you buy gives you a permanent discount on future cards, so the diamond card you bought on turn three means every diamond cost for the rest of the game is one cheaper. By the end you're buying cards worth seven gems while paying nothing. That's it. That's the game.

It's the purest **economic** engine here: you're not chaining triggers, you're lowering your costs until they hit zero. Thirty minutes, rules in five, and the decisions never quite get boring because someone is always about to take the card you were saving for.

**Where it's mean:** reserving a card takes it off the table and gives you a gold wildcard. Taking the card someone has visibly been building toward is completely legal and extremely satisfying. Our group does it constantly.

The chips are heavy poker-style tokens and they're the reason this game feels more expensive than it is. Great price for what you get, and it travels.

## 6. Scythe

[![Scythe](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/scythe.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01IPUGYK6/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The box shows mechs and an alternate-history 1920s Europe, so everyone assumes it's a war game. It is not. It's an efficiency engine that occasionally has a fight in it.

Your player mat has four action columns, and you can't take the same column twice in a row. So every turn is picking the best of the *other* three, and the whole game is about upgrading those actions until each one gives you more than it costs. Combat exists, most games see two or three fights total, and the mechs are mostly there to control territory and look good doing it.

The asymmetry is the real draw. Every faction has its own mat, its own starting resources, its own mech abilities, and then the mats get randomly paired with the factions on setup. So the same faction plays differently game to game. It's one of the highest-variety setups in the hobby.

The production is exceptional. Jakub Rozalski's art, thick player mats, actual metal coins in some editions. If Terraforming Mars lost your group on looks, this is the one that wins them back.

Downside: the rules explanation is long, and a first game is mostly spent working out what your own mat does. Budget a full evening for the teach and the play.

## 7. Dune: Imperium – Uprising

[![Dune: Imperium – Uprising](https://img.hexagamers.com/Dune_-_Imperium_Uprising_numiud.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CF3MVMKH/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The best argument that two mechanics can be better than one. Every turn you play a card from your hand to send a worker somewhere, so your deck determines which spaces on the board you're even *allowed* to use.

That fusion fixes the biggest problem with both parent genres. Pure deck builders can feel like solitaire; here you're fighting for board spaces. Pure worker placement can feel static; here your options change every shuffle. And because the cards you buy unlock spaces you couldn't reach before, the engine and the board grow together.

The other thing it gets right is **conflict**. Every round ends in a combat over rewards, and you commit troops without knowing what everyone else has held back. Losing a conflict you spent the whole round setting up is genuinely painful in the way that keeps people at the table.

Uprising is the version to buy over the original. It plays up to six with a two-player team mode, and the base game is tighter. Heaviest teach on this list after Terraforming Mars.

## 8. Concordia

[![Concordia](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/concordia.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HFDSQP6/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The quiet one. Concordia has been a designer's favourite for over a decade and almost nobody outside the hobby has heard of it.

You have a small hand of cards, each of which does one thing: move a colonist, build a house, produce goods. Play them all and you spend a turn taking them back. That's the entire engine: a hand you're constantly reshaping, where the discipline is deciding when to reset instead of squeezing one more play out of a bad hand.

There is also no luck in it anywhere. No dice, no random draw, and nothing hidden from anyone. If you lost, you were outplayed, which some people find clarifying and others find exhausting.

The scoring is the clever part. Your cards *are* your victory conditions, and each one scores based on something you built during the game, so buying a card is simultaneously buying an action and buying a scoring category. You're never quite sure who's winning until it's counted.

Plain-looking, no theme to speak of, and one of the most respected medium-heavy Euros ever made. If your group has graduated from [Ticket to Ride](/ticket-to-ride-europe-review/) and wants something with real teeth, this is the step up.

## 9. 7 Wonders ([Full Review Here](/7-wonders-review/))

[![7 Wonders](https://img.hexagamers.com/7-Wonders.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0043KJW5M/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Usually filed under card drafting, and it belongs there. But the reason drafting *matters* in this game is that you're building a tableau where the brown and grey resource cards you take in Age I are what let you afford anything in Age III.

Take no resources early and you spend the late game paying your neighbours for everything. It's an engine with a very short runway: three ages, six cards each, and the whole thing is over in half an hour.

It's on this list at all because it's the only engine builder we own that plays seven people. Everyone picks at the same time, so the player count barely moves the clock. Seven players, thirty minutes, and the decisions are still real ones. Nothing else in the genre manages that.

The trade-off for that speed is depth. You're building a small engine rather than a beautiful one, and experienced players will hit the ceiling of it fairly quickly. Fine by us. It does a job nothing else in the genre does.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0043KJW5M/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 10. Viticulture: Essential Edition

[![Viticulture: Essential Edition](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/viticulture.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B018GRSLK4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Running a Tuscan vineyard, which sounds like the most Euro premise imaginable and mostly is. Plant vines, harvest grapes, turn them into wine, fill orders.

The engine here is a **production chain**, and it's the reason this plays differently from everything else on the list. Nothing pays off immediately. Vines take a year to be worth harvesting, grapes have to age before they're good wine, and wine orders want specific vintages you have to plan for two seasons out. You're building a pipeline, and the satisfaction is watching something you planted in year one finally cash out in year five.

**The summer/winter split** is the structural twist. Your workers are divided across two seasons, so committing a worker to a summer action means not having it in winter. It's worker placement with a scheduling problem attached.

The Essential Edition is the one to get; it folds in the good expansion content and skips the fiddly original bits. It's also one of the better six-player heavy games, though it's tightest at three or four.

### Ark Nova

[![Ark Nova](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ark-nova.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09L6FCP9S/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Probably the best-designed engine builder of the last five years, and it's down here for one reason: it's a monster. You're building a modern zoo, playing animal and sponsor cards off five action tiles that slide across a track, so the action you want is always cheaper if you're willing to wait for it to drift into range. The card combos are ridiculous once they get going.

It's also two and a half hours, a card pool the size of a small library, and a first game that's mostly admin. It's genuinely great. It's also the game on this page most likely to get bought, played twice, and shelved. If your group already loves Terraforming Mars and wants the next mountain to climb, this is it. If they don't, start literally anywhere else.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09L6FCP9S/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

### Res Arcana

[![Res Arcana](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/res-arcana.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07PFRTSB2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The fastest big combo in the genre. You get a small hand of magic cards, and you spend the game converting essence between five types to power artifacts, dragons, and monuments that convert it faster. It's the same build-a-machine payoff as a two-hour game, compressed into about twenty minutes.

That speed is the whole pitch. There's almost no setup, turns are quick, and you can play three games in the time one round of Ark Nova takes. The trade-off is that the card pool is small, so experienced players start to recognise the strong openings. Worth hunting down if you love the cascade and hate the sitting.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07PFRTSB2/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

### Century: Spice Road

[![Century: Spice Road](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/century-spice-road.webp)](https://www.amazon.ca/dp/B071DXCT5L/?tag=hexagamers07-20)

An excellent light engine and one we'd hand to a family over half the games higher on this list. You're a spice trader upgrading turmeric into saffron into gold, collecting cards that let you make better trades, and cashing them in for scoring cards. The engine is your row of trade cards, and building an efficient conversion chain is deeply satisfying for a game this gentle.

It lost its ranked spot to Splendor for one reason only: Splendor does the same "get better at getting resources" job with fewer moving parts, and it's the cleaner teach. But Century has more texture, and if your group has already worn out Splendor, this is the obvious upgrade. The cubes and little bowls are lovely, too.

<a href="https://www.amazon.ca/dp/B071DXCT5L/?tag=hexagamers07-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon.ca</a>

### Gizmos

[![Gizmos](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/gizmos.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07T99S4YX/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The most literal engine builder here, because you're building an actual machine out of card parts. You draw coloured energy marbles from a physical dispenser and spend them on gizmos that trigger off each other, so the right build turns one action into a chain reaction of free builds and free energy. When your engine fires it *feels* like a machine going off.

It's light, it's quick, and the marble dispenser is genuinely fun to use, which does more for a family table than it sounds. It's off the ranked list because the ceiling is lower than the games above it, and the combos get familiar after a dozen plays. As a gateway *into* engine building, though, it might be the single best pick on this page.

## How to Pick One

- **Never played an engine builder?** [Splendor](/splendor-review/). Thirty minutes, five-minute teach, and the discount mechanic teaches you what an engine is without anyone having to explain it.
- **Want the genre's best all-rounder?** [Wingspan](/wingspan-review/). Beautiful, calm, and the engine is visible on the table.
- **Want to see how deep it goes?** Terraforming Mars, and clear the evening.
- **Your group hates multiplayer solitaire?** Dune: Imperium – Uprising. There's an actual fight every round.
- **Your group hates luck?** Concordia. There isn't any.
- **Playing with seven people?** [7 Wonders](/7-wonders-review/) is the only option here, and it's a good one.
- **Playing alone?** [Wingspan](/wingspan-review/) or Terraforming Mars. Both have solo modes somebody actually designed rather than bolted on at the end.

## Conclusion

Engine builders ask for patience up front and pay it all back at the end. That's why people tend to either love the genre or bounce straight off it. A group that gets restless in the first fifteen minutes of anything is going to hate this shelf, and there's no teaching around that.

Our advice is to start with [Splendor](/splendor-review/) or [Wingspan](/wingspan-review/) and watch whether that cascade turn actually lands for people. If it does, the rest of this list is waiting for you and you've got years of good evenings ahead. If it doesn't, you found out for thirty minutes and about forty bucks, which is a cheap answer to an expensive question.
