Japan is probably the most over-served theme in the hobby, and that's a compliment. Designers keep going back to it because it gives them two completely different visual languages to work with: the calm one, all gardens and tea and cherry blossoms, and the loud one, all warring clans and giant monsters stepping on Tokyo.

The problem for anyone shopping is that both of those show up in the same search results. You go looking for "Japanese board game," and you get a serene bamboo garden and a box full of samurai miniatures on the same page, with nothing to tell you which one your group will actually enjoy on a Thursday night.

So we sorted this list by *feel* rather than by mechanic. Calm games first, then the tense two-player duels, then the loud stuff.

## The Calm Ones

## 1. Takenoko ([Full Review Here](/takenoko-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Takenoko_psyu9h.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0049H9NVW/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A gardener grows bamboo. A panda eats the bamboo. You are both of those people, and the tension between those two jobs is the entire game.

You're placing garden plots, running irrigation to them, growing bamboo in three colours, and then walking the panda over to eat it — all to complete objective cards that want very specific arrangements. The weather die at the start of your turn shifts what you're allowed to do, which keeps the plan you made last turn from surviving contact with this one.

It plays gentle, and it looks gentle, but there's a real squeeze in it. Objectives are worth different amounts and you only score the ones you finish, so the game punishes anyone who spreads themselves across all three types instead of committing.

**The panda is the reason it gets to the table.** People who don't play board games will sit down for this one because there's a little painted panda figure, and that is a completely valid reason to own a game. It's our go-to for mixed groups where somebody's parents are visiting.

It is lighter than the box and the table presence suggest, though. Anyone hoping for a meaty [worker placement game](/best-worker-placement-board-games-reviews/) will find it thin after a few plays.

## 2. Tokaido

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/tokaido_vn2ido.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0757QD8FY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

You're walking the East Sea Road from Kyoto to Edo, and you're trying to have the nicest trip. That's the win condition. Not conquest, not efficiency — a nicer holiday than everyone else.

The clever bit is the turn order. Whoever is *furthest behind* on the road goes next, so hanging back lets you take more stops, and racing ahead means you skip things. Every move is a choice between grabbing a stop now or lingering to grab three cheap ones while everyone else walks past you. It's a whole strategy game hidden in a mechanic you can explain in one sentence.

Stops give you hot springs, meals at the inns, souvenirs to collect in sets, panoramas you build up across the journey, and encounters with people on the road.

**It is a beautiful object.** The art by Xavier Gueniffey Durin is the reason a lot of people buy it, and the deluxe edition with the little wooden travellers is the version we'd point you at if you can find it.

Interaction is almost nonexistent, and that's the thing to know before buying. You can block a stop by getting there first, and that is genuinely the extent of it. Groups who like messing with each other find this one very polite and very quiet.

## 3. Let's Go! To Japan

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/lets-go-to-japan.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CZBCPF53/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This one is doing something none of the others try: you're planning an actual trip. Tokyo and Kyoto, a week, and a card for every temple, restaurant, market and museum you could reasonably fit into it.

You draft activity cards and slot them into days on your itinerary, and the scoring rewards trips that make sense — clustering things in the same city, not zigzagging, actually resting. It's a tableau builder wearing a travel guide's clothes.

The cards are all real places, each with a short write-up on it. That sounds like a gimmick and turns out to be the best thing about the game. We've had two evenings where somebody stopped mid-turn to read a card out loud, and one where a friend started an actual notes file for a trip she's now taking.

Be warned that the theme is doing a lot of the work, and reviews are genuinely split on the puzzle underneath it. If nobody at your table cares about Japan, that puzzle probably isn't enough on its own. It plays well solo, which is worth knowing either way.

## The Tense Two-Player Ones

## 4. Hanamikoji

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hanamikoji_jmervm.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09YVM2658/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Twenty-one cards, seven geisha, four actions, fifteen minutes. This is the smallest game on the list and the one most likely to make somebody stare at the table in silence.

You're competing for the favour of seven geisha, and you win by holding four of them or by taking the ones worth eleven points between them. Each round you get four actions, and you use each one exactly once: hide a card face down, discard two cards, split three cards and let your opponent pick one, or split four cards into two pairs and let your opponent pick a pair.

Those last two actions are the whole game, because you are handing your opponent the choice. You have to build offers where both options are survivable for you and neither is great for them, while reading which geisha they've quietly been working toward. Getting it wrong is entirely your own doing, which is exactly why it stings.

It's the best fifteen-minute two-player game we own, and it fits in a coat pocket. Ours has been to three countries.

It is strictly two players, with no variants and no expansions. A new player will also lose the first couple of games badly while they work out what the four actions are actually for.

## 5. Onitama

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/onitama.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01C05HZB4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Chess, if chess took fifteen minutes and the pieces didn't have fixed moves.

A 5x5 board, five pieces each, and five movement cards on the table. On your turn you pick one of your two cards, move a piece the way that card shows, then *pass that card to your opponent* and take the one from the middle. Win by capturing the enemy master or walking your own master into their temple.

That card-passing rule is the design. Every move you make hands your opponent the exact tool you just used. You cannot make a strong move without immediately arming the person across from you, and holding a card back because you don't want them to have it is a real and constant cost.

There are sixteen cards in the box and five in play, so the setup changes completely every game and the openings you learn one night are worthless the next. For a box this small that's an absurd amount of variety.

It is an abstract, though. The Japanese theme is paint, albeit beautiful paint, and the deluxe editions with the wooden pieces are lovely. There's no story here, so if your group needs theme to stay engaged this won't do it.

## The Light and Loud Ones

## 6. King of Tokyo

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/king-of-tokyo.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01F46RPS4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Giant monsters fighting over Tokyo, resolved with Yahtzee dice. It has no business working as well as it does.

You roll six dice up to three times, keeping what you want between rolls. Claws attack, hearts heal, numbers score, energy buys cards with powers on them. Whoever is standing in Tokyo hits *everyone else at once* but can't heal. That's the tension the whole game runs on. Staying scores fast and gets you killed, and stepping out feels like admitting something.

First to twenty points wins, or last monster alive.

It's also a good game to be losing at. Elimination exists, but a game runs half an hour and the person who dies first is usually laughing about it.

At five or six players your turn comes around slowly and the whole thing drags. It's sharpest at four. We prefer it to King of New York, too, which piled on complexity the concept never needed.

## 7. Sushi Go! and Sushi Go Party!

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Sushi-Go_ocbhis.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00J57VU44/?tag=hexagamers-20)

[Card drafting](/best-card-drafting-board-games/) stripped down to its friendliest possible form. You get a hand, pick one card, pass the rest, repeat until the cards run out. Three rounds, then you score.

Every card scores differently and the rules are printed on the cards themselves, so nobody is checking the rulebook after the first minute. Sashimi needs three or it's worth nothing. Maki rolls only pay the person with the most. Dumplings scale up sharply, so the fifth one is worth far more than the first. Wasabi triples the next nigiri you play, which means you have to gamble that a good nigiri comes back around to you.

Buy Sushi Go Party! rather than the original. Same game, bigger box, a menu you assemble each session, and it goes up to eight players. The original is cheaper and pocket-sized, but Party is the better game and the one that survives a big table.

There is real luck in what gets passed to you, and it's lighter than people expect from anything labelled drafting. Treat it as a warm-up rather than the main event.

## 8. Machi Koro 2

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/machi-koro-2.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09B6R9TL2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

You're the mayor of a small town, rolling dice to make your buildings pay out, spending the money on more buildings that pay out on more numbers. Build three landmarks and you win.

The hook is that other people's buildings trigger on *your* roll. Someone owns the café, you roll a three, you pay them. So you're never fully in control of your own economy, and the round where a rival's engine starts firing on numbers you keep rolling is genuinely funny for everyone but you.

Get the 2 rather than the original. The first Machi Koro had a fixed shop, so every game converged on the same handful of good cards. The sequel deals a rotating market of ten instead, which keeps what's available moving and changes the landmarks you're chasing. It fixed the one thing that was wrong with a game people already liked.

The dice are the dice, though. Somebody will roll badly for three straight turns and simply not have a town. It's short enough that this won't ruin an evening, but it will decide a game.

## 9. Tokyo Highway

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/tokyo-highway.webp)](https://www.boardgamebliss.com/products/tokyo-highway)

The best-looking game on this page, and the only one where you can lose by breathing wrong.

You're building Tokyo's stacked expressway network out of little wooden pillars and thin sticks. Each turn you stack a pillar, lay a road from your last one, and the new pillar has to be exactly one level higher or lower than the previous. Cross over or duck under an opponent's road that nobody has crossed yet, and you get to drop one of your tiny cars on it. First to place all your cars wins.

**The build is the game.** Every road you lay is a physical thing balanced on a stick, and the structure gets taller and more precarious as the table fills up. Knocking something over carries a penalty, so there's a genuine tremor in your hand by the second half. It's the rare dexterity game that also asks you to plan three moves ahead, because where you put a pillar now determines whether you can reach anything useful later.

It also builds its own centrepiece. By the end you've got a tangled grey sculpture of overpasses sitting on the table, and people walking past a game night will stop and ask about it. Nothing else on this list does that.

Availability is the catch. It comes and goes at North American retailers and the price swings hard when stock is thin, so it's worth watching rather than panic-buying at whatever a reseller is asking. Also note it's a [dexterity game](/best-dexterity-board-games/) in the truest sense: anyone with unsteady hands is at a real disadvantage, and it's a bad fit for a table with young kids leaning on it.

## 10. Rising Sun

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/rising-sun.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076PDV9KR/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The heaviest thing on this page by a wide margin, and the only one that's really about war. Feudal Japan, the kami have returned, and three to five clans are carving up the map.

What makes it more than a miniatures game is the negotiation. Before each season you can form an *alliance* with another player, and allies share the benefits of the actions each of them takes. Alliances are formal and public, and they break. The moment a partner turns on you in the last season is the reason people remember this game. There's also a bidding phase for the kami's blessings, so even the fights you don't fight cost you something.

The production is both the selling point and the trap. The miniatures are enormous and gorgeous. They're also why the box costs what it costs, and the game underneath would work fine with cardboard.

Three players minimum, and it's a two-hour commitment with a real teach in front of it. Games built on betrayal need a group that can take a knife in the back and still be friends afterward. Ours can. Not every group can, and that's worth knowing before you spend this much.

## A Few We Left Off

**Hanabi** is a superb [cooperative game](/best-cooperative-board-games/) about fireworks, and it's Japanese in name and setting, but the design is French and the theme is a thin coat over a pure logic puzzle. It's on our [Asia list](/cultural-themed-board-games-asia/) and it earns its spot there.

**Lanterns** gets called a Japanese game constantly. It's set in Imperial China. Great game, wrong list.

**Go and shogi** are the real answer to "Japanese board game" and we're not qualified to rank them next to a panda game. They're both worth your time and neither needs our recommendation.

**Kanagawa, Lotus, and Yamatai** are all still good and all still on the [Asia list](/cultural-themed-board-games-asia/). They lost their spots here to games that do a similar job with fewer rules, not because anything went wrong with them.

## How to Pick One

- **Never played a modern board game?** [Takenoko](/takenoko-review/). The panda does the recruiting for you.
- **Want the calmest possible evening?** Tokaido. Nobody attacks anybody.
- **Two players, fifteen minutes, want to think hard?** Hanamikoji. It's the best value on this page.
- **Playing with kids or a big loud group?** King of Tokyo, or Sushi Go Party! if you want something quieter.
- **You're actually planning a trip to Japan?** Let's Go! To Japan, and take notes.
- **Want something people will stop and stare at?** Tokyo Highway, if you can find it in stock.
- **Want the big expensive centrepiece game?** Rising Sun, assuming three-plus players and a group that likes betrayal.
- **Want something for a [couple's game night](/best-board-games-for-couples/)?** Onitama or Hanamikoji. Two players only, fifteen minutes, and neither one is a pushover.

## Conclusion

The reason this theme produces so many good games isn't mysterious. It gives designers a *mood* to build around, and mood is the thing most games are missing. Tokaido would be a forgettable set collector without the road. Takenoko would be tile-laying without the panda.

If you're buying one and you don't know your group well, get [Takenoko](/takenoko-review/) or Sushi Go Party!. Both are cheap, both teach in five minutes, and neither will sit unplayed. If you already know your group is up for it, Hanamikoji is the one we'd defend hardest, and it costs less than a round of drinks.
