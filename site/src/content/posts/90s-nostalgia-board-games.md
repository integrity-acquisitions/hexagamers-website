Everybody has one. The game with the plastic contraption in the middle, the one where the box lid was falling apart by 1997, the one you played in a basement with the good carpet. You might not remember the rules. You definitely remember the sound it made.

The 1990s were a strange and wonderful era for the toy aisle. Games came with electronics, spring-loaded traps, marble launchers, and voices. Design quality was... let's say inconsistent. But the *experience* of them burned in permanently in a way that a lot of better-designed games never manage.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](#90s-nostalgia-board-games-comparison-table)

This list is about those games. If you want the other half of the decade — the German imports that quietly invented modern board gaming while all this was happening — we covered those in [Best Board Games of the 90s](/best-board-games-of-the-90s/). Both are true. They just lived on different shelves.

### A Note on Dates

We were strict about this: **every game here was released or substantially redesigned between 1990 and 1999.** That rules out a lot of games people file under "90s" out of habit. Mall Madness is 1988. HeroQuest is 1989. Girl Talk is 1988. Fireball Island is 1986. Jenga, Twister, Pictionary, Scattergories, Taboo, Trivial Pursuit — all 80s or earlier. You played them in the 90s. They just weren't *from* the 90s. Most of those have a home in our [80s nostalgia list](/80s-nostalgia-board-games/) instead.

We've also been honest about what you can still buy. Most of these are easier to get hold of than you'd expect — a couple have been quietly reissued in the last few years. One is genuinely gone, and no amount of nostalgia brings back a discontinued electronic phone handset.

## 1. 13 Dead End Drive (1993)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/13-dead-end-drive.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000FE190G/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The best game on this list, and it's not particularly close.

Aunt Agatha has died, the will is contested, and the twelve of you circling her mansion are all secretly hoping to be the last one standing. Each player holds character cards in secret. You move characters around the board — including ones that aren't yours, as cover — and when a character lands on the wrong square, you spring a trap. The bookcase tips over. The chandelier drops. The suit of armour falls. Someone is eliminated and you try very hard not to look pleased about it.

**The traps are the whole point, and they're genuinely great toys.** They're spring-loaded plastic mechanisms built into the board, and setting them off never stops being satisfying. Thirty years on, this is the thing people describe when they describe this game — not the strategy, the *clunk*.

There's a real hidden-identity game underneath the gimmick, too. You're bluffing about which character is yours the entire time, moving your own character into danger to sell the lie. It's a genuine mechanic, not just theming.

**The bad news:** it's long out of print, and the sequel (1995's *1313 Dead End Drive*) is gone too. Used copies exist, but the plastic traps get brittle and pieces go missing, so "complete in box" carries a premium. Check the listing carefully before you buy.

If you find one intact at a reasonable price, grab it. Nothing modern really replicates this.

## 2. Jumanji (1995)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/jumanji.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07CH9JCFL/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Released alongside the Robin Williams film, and the box alone is a time machine.

The centrepiece is the decoder — a little plastic window with a red lens. You read a rhyming riddle, slide it into the decoder, and the answer appears. As a nine-year-old this felt like actual magic. As an adult you can see it's a red-filter trick used in about four hundred toys. It's still fun.

Gameplay is a race around four paths while jungle disasters accumulate on the "Doomsday Grid" in the centre. Fill the grid before someone reaches the middle and everyone loses together, which gives it a lightly cooperative edge in an otherwise competitive race.

**Honestly? The game underneath is thin.** You roll, you move, you solve a riddle under time pressure, you hope. There's very little decision-making. It's an experience piece, and it knows it.

But it's an experience piece that *works* on kids, and the theming does a lot of heavy lifting. Modern reprints of the original 1995 design are readily available, and there's a 30th Anniversary edition floating around too. If you're buying it for a kid rather than for yourself, they will not care that the strategy is shallow.

## 3. Cranium (1998)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/cranium.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D6MFPGT7/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The last great party game of the decade, and the one on this list that's aged the best by a wide margin.

Cranium's insight was that most party games only reward one kind of brain. Trivia games reward the person who reads a lot. Pictionary rewards the person who can draw. Charades rewards the extrovert. So Cranium just put all of them in one box — you draw, you act, you hum, you sculpt in clay, you spell words backwards, you answer trivia. Everybody at the table is brilliant at something and useless at something else.

**Sculptorades are the reason this game is remembered.** Watching a grown adult try to sculpt "vacuum cleaner" out of a lump of purple clay while their partner shouts increasingly desperate guesses is the single funniest thing in any game from the 90s. We once had someone spend the full timer building what turned out to be a very detailed "hamburger" while their teammate guessed every appliance in the house.

It plays in teams, wants at least four players, and genuinely improves with six or eight. It's the one game here we'd still actively recommend for a modern adult game night, not just for nostalgia.

The Cranium Classic edition is the current version and is basically the original with updated cards. The 25th Anniversary edition from Funko is also around with 800 new questions.

**One warning:** the clay dries out. Every copy of this game that has ever existed has at least one rock-hard tub of Cranium Clay in it. Buy a cheap tub of Play-Doh and thank us later.

## 4. Electronic Dream Phone (1991)

There's no current edition of this one, so the only way in is the used market — and that's the story of this game.

You're trying to work out which of the boys pictured on the board has a crush on you. You dial numbers on a chunky pink cordless phone, and a recorded voice gives you a clue. "He's not wearing a hat." Cross off everyone wearing a hat. It's Guess Who? with a deduction grid and a much better gimmick.

**The phone was the entire experience.** It was oversized, it was pink, it made a real dial tone, and getting to hold it was half the reason anyone wanted to play. The deduction underneath is legitimately fine — it's a proper elimination puzzle, and you can play it badly or well.

It has also aged in ways that are worth naming. The whole premise is a group of girls competing over which boy likes them, and the boys are all interchangeable haircuts with names like "Bruce." It's very much a product of 1991.

**Current status: gone.** Milton Bradley re-released it in 1996 and 2009 and there's been nothing since. Used copies turn up regularly on eBay, but they're 30-year-old consumer electronics — the handset frequently doesn't work, the batteries corroded in place decades ago, and a Dream Phone with a dead phone is just a board with pictures of boys on it. Buy only if the listing explicitly confirms the electronics are tested and working, and budget for the possibility that they aren't.

## 5. Crossfire (1994 version)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/crossfire.webp)](https://cardsandcoasters.com/products/crossfire-board-game)

The commercial. That's what everyone remembers. The vaguely post-apocalyptic setting, the aggressive rock soundtrack, the shouted tagline. It ran constantly in the early 90s and it sold an enormous number of these.

Worth being precise here: **Crossfire was originally a 1971 Ideal game.** What made it a 90s icon was the Milton Bradley run and specifically that ad campaign, plus a redesign that gave the board a dome shape so the ball bearings moved better. So the game is older than the nostalgia — but the version you remember genuinely is from this decade.

The game itself is two players, ten minutes, and dead simple. You each have a spring-loaded gun that fires small metal balls, and you're trying to shove a puck into your opponent's goal. First to three wins. There is no strategy. There is only firing as fast as your hands allow while metal balls go everywhere.

**And that's fine.** It's a physical toy that generates noise and adrenaline. It was never trying to be anything else.

Hasbro picked the rights back up in 2016 and has done occasional reissues since — the current version uses a sloped board instead of the domed one, and it tracks your score down the side. Availability comes and goes in waves, so if you see one in stock somewhere it's worth moving on it rather than assuming it'll be there next month. Just know that whichever version you find, you will be picking ball bearings out of the carpet for weeks.

## 6. Don't Wake Daddy (1992)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/dont-wake-daddy.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DVSRFB25/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A game with one idea, executed perfectly for its audience.

You're kids sneaking to the fridge for a midnight snack. Daddy is asleep in a plastic bed in the middle of the board. You move along the track, and every so often you have to press the alarm clock the number of times shown on your space. Somewhere in that sequence, Daddy springs bolt upright and screams.

**You know it's coming. That's the genius of it.** The entire game is built around a jump scare that everybody at the table is actively anticipating, and it works every single time on a six-year-old. It works on a fair number of adults too, if we're being honest.

There's no strategy whatsoever — you roll, you move, you press the clock, you flinch. As a game for grown-ups it's nothing. As a game to play with a four-year-old it's close to perfect, because the tension is completely readable and the payoff is instant.

Currently published by Goliath and easy to find. The modern version has a slightly different Daddy design than the one you remember, which somehow feels like a betrayal, but the mechanism is the same.

## 7. Crocodile Dentist (1990)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/crocodile-dentist.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00NQQTZBA/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Barely a board game. Absolutely belongs on this list.

There's a plastic crocodile with an open mouth. You press down its teeth one at a time. One tooth is the sore tooth. When you press it, the jaw snaps shut on your finger. That's the game.

**It's the purest possible version of the 90s toy-game formula:** one physical mechanism, one moment of anticipation, zero decisions. You're not playing so much as taking turns volunteering for a small, entirely safe scare.

What's remarkable is how well it works. The snap is fast enough to be startling and gentle enough that nobody gets hurt, and the anticipation of pressing a tooth you're fairly sure is fine is genuinely tense. Kids will play this for an hour.

It's cheap, it's still in print through Hasbro, and it's a solid pick for the four-to-six age bracket. Two to four players, though honestly it works fine with any number of people willing to stick a finger in a crocodile's mouth.

## 8. The Grape Escape (1992)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/grape-escape.webp)](https://www.amazon.ca/dp/B0B72RSV66/?tag=hexagamers07-20)

The strangest game of the decade, and the one that gets the biggest reaction when we bring it up.

You made grape people. Out of modelling clay. Using a plastic press. Then you walked them through an obstacle course designed specifically to destroy them — a rolling pin that flattened them, a spiked press, a wall that squashed them flat. Get your grape person to the end intact and you win.

**It is a game about manufacturing small clay creatures for the express purpose of watching them get mangled**, and it was marketed to children, and nobody at the time thought this was odd.

The clay was always the weak point. It dried out, it got ground into the mechanisms, it stained the carpet, and once it was gone the original Parker Brothers copies were effectively done. That's why so few intact 1992 sets survive.

**Here's the good news, and it genuinely surprised us:** Hasbro brought this back. The current edition ships with Play-Doh compound instead of the original clay, which is a sensible fix since you can replace Play-Doh for a couple of dollars forever. Same premise, same squishing, same obstacle course.

It's listed on amazon.ca rather than the US store, so Canadian readers have the easier path here. Ages 5 and up, 2–4 players, and it will absolutely make a mess. That was always the point.

<a href="https://www.amazon.ca/dp/B0B72RSV66/?tag=hexagamers07-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon.ca</a>

## So What's Actually Worth Buying?

Only one game here competes on merit rather than memory, and it's **Cranium**. Put it in front of six adults who haven't played it since school and it holds up completely.

For a young kid, **Crocodile Dentist** and **Don't Wake Daddy** are cheap, in print, and do exactly what they promise. Neither will survive contact with anyone over ten, which is fine, because neither is for them. **The Grape Escape** is the wildcard pick here — it's back in print with Play-Doh, and it's still the most gloriously strange thing on this list.

The one actually worth hunting down is **13 Dead End Drive**. The traps still work, the bluffing underneath is real, and nothing since has quite managed the same trick. Just check the listing photos for missing pieces before you commit.

And if you want to know what else was happening in board games while all of this was going on, the same decade quietly produced Catan, El Grande, and Tigris & Euphrates. We wrote about those in [Best Board Games of the 90s](/best-board-games-of-the-90s/) — it's a completely different world, and honestly worth a look even if you came here purely for the nostalgia.
