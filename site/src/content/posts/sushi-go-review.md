## Overview

A pick-and-pass card game where everyone chooses a card at the same time and then hands the rest of their hand to the person beside them. You're collecting sets of sushi over three rounds: two tempura for five points, three sashimi for ten, the most maki rolls for six. The cards you don't take go straight to your opponent, so every pick is a decision about your own scoring and a decision about what you're willing to give away.

## Ryan's Review

### Likes
- Simple on the surface with real layers underneath; the depth sneaks up on you
- The best introduction to card drafting there is, and it makes 7 Wonders click later
- Genuinely accessible for all ages, including kids who can't do the scoring yet
- The artwork is cute and well done, and the theme carries the whole thing
- The hand you're dealt is different every round, so you're never running the same plan
- Fast once everyone knows the rules

### Dislikes
- Not a deep game, so it can wear out if it's the only thing your group wants to play
- Easy to over-play to the point of burnout, which says more about how often it hits the table than the game
- Who you're sitting beside changes your game more than your own decisions sometimes
- Once you want more players or more variety, you're buying Sushi Go Party instead

## Getting It to the Table

I love this game, and I'll admit the tin does it no favours on a shelf. It looks like something from the checkout counter at a bookstore. Cartoon sushi with faces on it. Nothing about the packaging suggests there's a real drafting game inside.

Then you play a round and realize you've been making genuinely hard decisions for fifteen minutes without noticing.

That's the trick of Sushi Go!. The rules are almost nothing (take a card, pass the rest, repeat) and the difficulty is entirely in what you choose. We've handed this to people who don't play board games and to kids who can't add up the scores yet, and both groups end up engaged for different reasons.

## Thoughts

**The rules are simple. The decisions are not.** Take the card that maximizes your score, or the best option available. That's the whole instruction. What makes it hard is that the hand keeps moving. The eight cards in front of you right now will be in front of someone else in a second, so a card you pass on doesn't disappear. It becomes somebody's points.

**Sets are where the tension lives.** A single tempura is worth nothing. Two are worth five. Sashimi is worse: one is nothing, two is still nothing, three is ten points. So the moment you take your first sashimi you've made a bet, and you'll spend the rest of the round hoping the other two come around before the hand runs dry. Committing to sashimi and finishing with two of them is the most deliberately painful thing in the game.

**Who you're sitting beside genuinely matters.** This is the part I didn't expect the first few times. Your neighbour is either building their own hand or playing defensively to stop you building yours, and those are very different games to sit next to. Somebody who's watching your tempura count and taking the second one out of spite will wreck a round that had nothing to do with them. It's the only game I can think of where seat position is a real strategic factor and nobody warns you about it.

**Passing is a decision, not a formality.** Once you've seen it, you can't unsee it: you're not just picking the best card for you, you're choosing what to hand the person next to you. Passing a hand you know contains the third sashimi to someone sitting on two is a small, deliberate act of betrayal. It's very funny at a table of friends and it's the moment the game stops feeling like a kids' game.

**The replayability is in the deal.** The hand you get at the start of every round is almost always different, so you never know what you'll be going for. You can't walk in with a plan. You pick up eight cards, see three dumplings and no nigiri, and adjust on the spot. That's a good kind of variety — it comes from the shuffle rather than from a pile of expansions.

**It's the gentlest possible on-ramp to drafting.** Card drafting is everywhere in bigger games, and 7 Wonders is the one people usually get taught first, which is a rough introduction if you've never passed a hand before. Sushi Go! is the same core idea with cartoon dumplings and no engine-building on top. Learn it here and 7 Wonders makes sense immediately.

**Accessible without being dumbed down.** Younger kids won't grasp the scoring, but they don't need to. They can look for cards with matching pictures and play perfectly happily while the adults do the arithmetic. That's a rare quality. Most games that work for eight-year-olds are boring for adults, and most games adults like leave kids behind.

**It isn't deep, and that catches up with you.** This is the honest limitation. Sushi Go! is light, and once your beginner friends latch onto it they'll want it every single time, which is how a game that everyone likes quietly becomes the only game you play. We've hit that wall. I don't want to play it again for a while, and that's pure saturation rather than anything wrong with the game. Rotate it with something heavier and it stays great for years.

**On price and which box to buy.** The tin is around twelve dollars, which is close to the best value in this hobby. But we really like Sushi Go Party, and it's worth knowing what it does: more players, a modular menu of card types instead of a fixed set, and a proper board. If you want the pocket game, buy the tin. If you want the version that handles a bigger group and changes shape between plays, buy Party. Our group reaches for Party more often. There's also a dice version, Sushi Roll, and we break all three down in [Sushi Go vs Sushi Go Party vs Sushi Roll](/sushi-go-vs-sushi-go-party-vs-sushi-roll/).

## Conclusion

Sushi Go! takes about two minutes to teach and hides a real drafting game under cartoon sushi, which is why it works for kids, for non-gamers, and for people who've played a hundred games. Buy the tin if you want something cheap and pocket-sized that gets people comfortable with drafting before you hand them something heavier. Buy Sushi Go Party if you regularly have more than five people or you want the variety. It's earned its spot in our collection either way, even if it's currently resting after being played into the ground.
