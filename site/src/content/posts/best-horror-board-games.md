Horror is a strange thing to ask a board game to do. A movie can cut to black at the worst possible second. A book can make you turn the page against your better judgment. A board game just sits there on the table, cardboard and plastic, waiting for you to decide how scared you want to be. The good ones find a workaround. They don't jump-scare you. They build dread through not knowing what's behind the next tile, or not knowing which of your friends just got possessed.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](#best-horror-board-games-comparison-table)

We went wide with this one on purpose. Some of these are heavy [co-op](/best-cooperative-board-games/) campaigns you'll sink a whole season into. A couple are quick enough to teach in ten minutes and still leave someone rattled, closer to our [social deduction](/best-social-deduction-board-games/) picks than a monster hunt. One of them is entirely [solo](/best-one-player-solo-board-games-with-reviews/), for the nights nobody else wants to play. Haunted houses, cosmic dread, slasher flicks, séances. The theme keeps changing shape, but every game here earns its spot because the horror actually shapes how you play, not just how the box looks on the shelf.

A note on what we mean by "horror" here: we're not counting pure zombie survival games, since [we already covered those separately](/best-zombie-board-games/). This list leans into gothic, cosmic, and psychological horror instead. Haunted mansions, Lovecraftian cults, slasher villains. The stuff that gets under your skin rather than just chasing you with a horde.

## Best Horror Board Games Reviews

## 1. Betrayal at House on the Hill ([Full Review Here](/betrayal-at-house-on-the-hill-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Betrayal-1.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B003HC9734/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Betrayal is the obvious starting point for this list because it basically invented the modern haunted-house board game. You and your group explore a mansion tile by tile, flipping over rooms as you go, picking up omens along the way. Nobody knows what's in the house until you're standing in it. Then, at some point determined by which omens got drawn, one player secretly turns and becomes the villain, and the game you were playing five minutes ago is not the game you're playing now.

We've had mixed feelings on this one at our table. The premise is genuinely great. The tile-flipping reveal keeps everyone leaning in, and the haunt system means no two games open the same story. But the difficulty swings hard depending on which of the 50 scenarios you land on, and we've had nights where the traitor side wins so fast it barely feels like a game. We wanted to love this one more than we did. We know plenty of people whose favorite game on the shelf is this exact box.

If your group leans social and doesn't mind a game that occasionally ends in fifteen minutes, this is still one of the most purely fun "oh no, it's you" reveals in the hobby.

## 2. Mansions of Madness (2nd Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/mansions-of-madness.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01J4NB6CO/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If Betrayal is horror as a party trick, Mansions of Madness is horror as a slow-burn campaign. This is a fully cooperative Lovecraftian investigation game, and the twist that sets it apart is the companion app. It runs the story, tracks the clock, and reveals monsters and plot twists without needing a human game master. That sounds like a small thing until you're mid-game and the app suddenly plays a sound cue at exactly the wrong moment.

Setup takes a while, and a full campaign scenario can eat two to three hours, so this isn't a warm-up game. It's a commit-the-whole-evening game. What you get for that time is genuinely tense investigation: searching rooms for clues, managing your sanity as much as your health, watching the story branch based on choices your group made an hour earlier.

The app integration is the best and worst thing about it. Best because it removes the need for anyone to read a rulebook of secret triggers out loud. Worst because if you don't have a tablet handy, you don't have a game.

## 3. Arkham Horror: The Card Game

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/arkham-horror-card-game.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0999D3P8S/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This one is a deckbuilder wearing a horror game's clothes, and it's one of the most respected cooperative card games out there for a reason. You and up to three friends build custom investigator decks, then work through a campaign where your choices, and your failures, actually carry forward into the next scenario. Cards get scarred, characters die, relationships change based on what happened two sessions ago.

The Revised Core Set gets you the Night of the Zealot campaign to start: three scenarios that teach the system without burying you. What sets it apart from other Lovecraftian games on this list is how personal it feels. You're not managing a shared board state so much as living with the consequences of your own deck's weaknesses.

It's a bigger investment than most games here, both in time to learn the rules and in money once you start chasing expansions. Go in expecting a long-term hobby, not a one-off purchase.

## 4. Cthulhu: Death May Die

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/cthulhu-death-may-die.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07XJDYR1T/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Where Arkham Horror is quiet and cerebral, Death May Die is loud. This is a fully cooperative dice-and-miniatures brawler where your investigators are racing to summon an Elder God themselves, on purpose, so they can kill it before it fully arrives. Combat is chunky and fast, and sanity loss isn't just a stat to manage. It actively makes your character more powerful and more likely to die screaming, which is a genuinely clever way to make madness feel mechanical instead of decorative.

The production is a big part of the appeal. The miniatures are large and dramatic (the Elder God figures in particular are a table centerpiece), and CMON clearly leaned into spectacle over subtlety. If you want your horror game to feel more like a boss fight than a slow investigation, this is the pick.

It runs heavier on rules overhead than Horrified but lighter on decision fatigue than Arkham Horror. A solid middle ground for a group that wants co-op horror without a multi-session campaign commitment.

## 5. Horrified: Universal Monsters

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/horrified-universal-monsters.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07QQHTD4Q/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Horrified is the easiest entry point on this list, and that's a compliment. You and your team work together across a town map to defeat classic movie monsters (Dracula, the Mummy, Frankenstein's Monster, the Invisible Man) before the terror track runs out. Each monster brings its own win condition, so the puzzle changes completely depending on which ones you're facing that game.

This is the one we'd hand to a family or a mixed group that includes someone who's never played a "real" board game before. The rules teach in about ten minutes, the theme is fun rather than genuinely disturbing, and the co-op structure means nobody's sitting out waiting for their turn to matter.

It won't scare anyone. What it will do is get a table of very different skill levels actually working together, and the monster combinations give it more replay value than a licensed theme game usually earns.

## 6. Mysterium

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Mysterium_x7x39l.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B013TJ5P80/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Mysterium swaps out monsters for mood. One player takes on the role of a ghost trying to communicate a murder (the suspect, the location, the weapon) using nothing but surreal, dreamlike illustrated cards. Everyone else has to interpret what the ghost is showing them, which is exactly as maddening and as fun as it sounds when the "clue" is a painting of a candle melting into a clock.

There's no real threat in this game. No traitor, no monster to fight. The horror here is atmospheric: the séance framing, the ghost unable to speak, the growing frustration of not understanding something that feels like it should be obvious. It plays up to seven, which makes it one of the few games on this list that scales well for a bigger group.

We'd put this on the table for a night when people want something spooky-adjacent but not actually tense. It's a great "in between" pick if half your group wants horror and half wants something lighter.

## 7. Final Girl

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/final-girl-core-box.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09N8R6296/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Every other game on this list assumes you have people to play with. Final Girl doesn't, and that makes it worth calling out on its own. You play the lone survivor of a slasher movie, managing a deck of actions while the killer stalks around a location trying to close in before you can escape or fight back. The core box is required, but it doesn't do anything on its own. You also need at least one Feature Film box, each of which swaps in a different killer and setting, from a haunted carnival to a summer camp.

The design nails the slasher-movie feel better than almost anything else in the hobby. You're managing tension, not just resources. Every card flip could be the moment the killer finds you. It's genuinely difficult, in a way that feels earned rather than unfair, and losing still tells a satisfying story.

This is the pick for solo game nights, or for the person in your group who always wants to play something after everyone else has gone home.

## 8. One Night Ultimate Werewolf ([Full Review Here](/one-night-ultimate-werewolf-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/One_night_ultimate_werewolf_pvnehl.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HS7GG5G/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Not every horror game needs a monster miniature. One Night Ultimate Werewolf is social deduction with a horror skin. Everyone's a villager except for a hidden werewolf or two, roles get secretly swapped in the night phase, and the entire game is over in ten minutes of accusations and bluffing.

The whole game lives in that one daytime discussion phase, where everyone's trying to piece together who did what during the "night" using nothing but partial information and other people's body language. It's fast enough to play five times in a row, and different every time because roles reshuffle each round.

This won't give anyone chills, but the paranoia of trying to catch a werewolf hiding in plain sight scratches a similar itch to the bigger co-op games on this list, at a fraction of the setup time.

## 9. Dead of Winter: A Crossroads Game

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Dead-of-Winter-1024x1014.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HFKITJC/?tag=hexagamers-20)

We're closing with one crossover pick from our [zombie board games list](/best-zombie-board-games/), because Dead of Winter earns its place here too. The zombies are almost beside the point. This is a colony-management game where your group is trying to survive the winter together, except one of you might secretly be working against everyone else. The real horror isn't the undead outside the gate. It's not knowing which of your friends wants the colony to fail.

The Crossroads cards are the standout mechanic: story events that trigger mid-turn and force a decision nobody saw coming, often with a moral weight that a standard co-op game never bothers with. Do you let a starving stranger into the colony, knowing it might be a trap? That kind of tension is closer to a survival-horror story than a zombie miniatures skirmish.

If your group already owns it from our zombie list, it's worth remembering it belongs on this shelf too. If you don't, it's one of the better traitor mechanics in the genre.

## Conclusion

If you only buy one game off this list, start with Horrified or Betrayal at House on the Hill. Both teach fast and don't demand a whole season of your group's time. If you're chasing something closer to real dread, Mansions of Madness and Arkham Horror reward the extra setup with actual atmosphere, and Final Girl is there for the night nobody else wants to play.

We kept the zombie games off this one on purpose.
