## Overview

Flip 7: With a Vengeance takes the bones of the original Flip 7 (flip cards, don't duplicate a number, bank before you bust) and adds a deck full of ways to mess with everyone else's hand. You still know the basics within a minute if you've played the original. What's different is how much you have to pay attention to the table, not just your own cards.

## Ryan's Review

### Likes
- Same easy pickup as the original: if you already know Flip 7, you know this one in minutes
- Numbers now go up to 13 instead of 12, which raises the risk and the payoff
- Lucky 13, Unlucky 7, and the zero card each swing a hand hard in one direction
- Action cards got meaner: Flip Four, swapping, stealing, and forcing discards make it interactive
- Multiplier and modifier cards let you tank someone else's score instead of just building your own
- Rewards paying attention to the whole table, not just your own cards

### Dislikes
- A lot less control over your own score depending on who you're playing with
- If the table decides to gang up on one player, it can stop being fun for them
- The added chaos means bigger point swings, which some groups won't want

## Getting It to the Table

We already had Flip 7 in the rotation, so this one was an easy pickup. Same idea, new box, and enough new cards on the sleeve to make it worth a look. First game in, though, we made a rookie mistake: we didn't read the multiplier cards closely enough and kept assuming they applied to our own hand. They don't. You hand them to someone else. Once we figured that out, the whole table changed. People got competitive fast.

## Thoughts

**The number deck goes higher, and it matters.** Flip 7 With a Vengeance runs the deck up to 13 instead of capping at 12. That's a small change on paper, but it means bigger scores are possible and busting on a high number stings more. Every flip past 10 feels like a real decision instead of a formality.

**Lucky 13, Unlucky 7, and the zero card change how a hand plays out.** Lucky 13 lets you collect a second 13 without busting, which is a nice cushion when you're pushing for extra points. Unlucky 7 forces you to discard everything else in front of you and keep just that one card, brutal if you'd built up a strong hand. The zero card is its own trap: you score nothing on it unless you flip seven different numbers, so it either quietly kills your round or forces you to keep pushing when you'd rather stop. All three flip a hand from "steady collecting" to "all or nothing" in a single card, and that's exactly the kind of swing that keeps a slow, cautious table honest.

**The action cards do the heavy lifting.** Flip Three from the original becomes Flip Four here, and it's exactly as reckless as it sounds: four cards forced on you at once, with no say in when it stops. Swap lets you trade a card with another player, which is a genuinely useful way to manage your own risk instead of just hoping for good luck. The freeze card becomes Take One More and Freeze, so instead of simply locking someone in, you might be handing them one more shot at a bust, or one more shot at a big score. It's a small rule twist, but it adds a layer of "do I want to help or hurt this person" that the original never asked.

**Stealing and discarding make your hand a moving target.** Beyond the freeze twist, you can steal a card straight off another player's tableau, or force someone to discard one of theirs. Combined with swapping, your hand stops being something you just build. It's something you have to defend. That's a real shift from the original, where your only opponent was the deck.

**The multiplier cards are where the "vengeance" part shows up.** Instead of adding points to your own hand, these let you hand a penalty to someone else: knock them down or cut their score in half. We missed this completely on our first play and kept them to ourselves, which flattened the whole point of the mechanic. Once we started actually giving them away, the table got loud fast. People took it personally, in a good way. Small rivalries formed over nothing more than a bad flip someone else caused on purpose.

**It's a louder, more invested version of the original.** Because the point spread can swing so much harder here, people lean in more. You're watching what your neighbor collects, not just your own cards, and the game rewards that attention. It also means the gap between first and last place can get wide fast, which pushes people behind to take bigger risks late. That's exactly when the game gets fun to watch.

**Where it can go wrong.** If your group tends to pick on the same person round after round, this game gives them the tools to actually do it, and that's not fun for whoever's on the receiving end. To be fair, that dynamic exists in the original too, some tables just enjoy needling one player more than others, but the extra interaction here gives it sharper teeth.

## Conclusion

If you liked the original Flip 7, this is the version that turns the volume up. Same quick teach, same push-your-luck core, but now everyone at the table has a hand in your business. I'd take this one over the base game most nights, purely because of how much more invested people get. It's not for a table that wants a calm, heads-down warm-up game, but for a group that likes a little chaos and doesn't mind getting picked on once in a while, it earns a spot in the rotation.
