Three boxes, the same cartoon sushi on the front, and a shop shelf that gives you no help at all. One is a little tin, one is a big square box, and one is full of dice. They're all by Phil Walker-Harding, they all use the same scoring ideas, and the packaging does almost nothing to explain why you'd pick one over another.

The short version: **Sushi Go Party is the best game of the three and the one we reach for most.** But it's the wrong purchase for a lot of people, and the tin is genuinely better in a few specific situations. Sushi Roll is the odd one out and it's the one most people should skip unless a particular thing appeals to them.

## They're All the Same Game Underneath

Every one of these is a drafting game played over three rounds. You take one thing, pass or rotate the rest, and collect sets that score in specific ways. Two tempura are worth five points. Three sashimi are worth ten. Whoever has the most maki rolls takes six. Puddings are counted at the very end and the person with the fewest gets punished.

If you know one, you know all three within about a minute of sitting down. What changes between the boxes is how many people can play, how much variety you get between games, and whether you're picking cards or dice.

## Sushi Go! — The Tin

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Sushi-Go_ocbhis.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00J57VU44/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The original is 108 cards in a metal tin, and the card set never changes. Every game has the same twelve card types in the same proportions, which sounds like a weakness and mostly isn't. The variety comes from the hand you're dealt rather than from the box, and that's different enough every round to keep it interesting.

What the tin does better than either of the others is portability and price. It's around twelve dollars and it fits in a jacket pocket. That's the entire argument, and it's a strong one. This is the version that goes camping, lives in a backpack, and comes out at a restaurant while you wait for food.

The limitation is the player count. Five is the ceiling and it's a real ceiling, not a soft one. If your group is regularly six or more people, the tin can't do it and no amount of wanting it to will help.

## Sushi Go Party! — The Big Box

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/sushi-go-party-edition_xq83ml.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01CETNKE2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Party is the original rebuilt as a modular game. There are 22 menu tiles, and before each game you slot eight of them into a board: one roll type, three appetizers, two specials, and one dessert. That's your card set for the night, and it changes what the game actually rewards.

This is the real difference, and it's bigger than "more cards." Playing with edamame and miso soup is a different game from playing with temaki and tofu, because the scoring shapes change. Some menus reward hoarding one type, others punish it. Building the menu takes a minute and it's genuinely part of the fun.

It also handles two to eight players, which is the practical reason it's the one we play most. A game that covers everything from a quiet two-player evening to a full table of eight is worth owning even if you already have the tin.

Two honest costs. It's roughly double the price, and it takes shelf space and table space that the tin doesn't. The board with the score track is nice, no more scribbling on paper, but it's the thing that turns a pocket game into a boxed one. If portability is why you wanted Sushi Go!, Party undoes that.

## Sushi Roll — The Dice Game

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/sushi-roll.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07PLFLR54/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Sushi Roll swaps cards for dice. Everyone has a conveyor belt in front of them, you roll your dice onto it, take one, then slide the whole belt to the player on your left and take from the belt that arrives. Same three rounds, same set scoring, different feel.

The interesting change is that you can see what's coming. In the card game you have no idea what's in the hand heading your way. Here the belts are face up on the table, so you can watch a belt loaded with sashimi travelling toward you and plan for it. There are also re-roll and swap tokens, which add a small amount of control that the card version doesn't have.

The catch is the one thing the original got right without trying. Sushi Go! and Party are simultaneous. Everyone picks at once, so five players take the same time as two. Sushi Roll is sequential. Players take turns choosing from their belt, and at five players that means real downtime in a game whose whole appeal was that it never had any.

It's a good game. It's just solving a problem the card versions didn't have, and paying for it with waiting. At two or three players, where the downtime doesn't bite and you can read every belt, it's the most tactical of the three.

## Which One Should You Buy?

**Buy Sushi Go! if** you want the cheap pocket version, you mostly play with four or fewer people, or you're buying it as a gift for someone who's never drafted before. It's also the right pick if the game needs to travel: camping, hotels, a bag you carry every day.

**Buy Sushi Go Party! if** you play with five or more people even occasionally, or you expect this to hit the table often enough that a fixed card set would wear thin. It's the better game and the one we play most. If you can only own one and space isn't tight, own this.

**Buy Sushi Roll if** you already like the original and want a different texture, you mostly play at two or three, or your group prefers dice to cards. It's the least essential of the three and we'd never recommend it as a first purchase.

**Don't buy two of them expecting different evenings.** They overlap heavily. Party contains everything the tin does and more, so owning both only makes sense if you want a travel copy alongside the group copy. That's a legitimate reason, and it's exactly why we have more than one of these in the house.

## Conclusion

Party is the best version and the one to buy if you're only buying one. The tin wins on price and pocketability and is the better gift. Sushi Roll is for people who already know they like this game and want it in dice form.
