History is one of the most reused themes in the hobby, but a lot of games only borrow it for flavor. A cardboard Rome or a painted Viking longship on the box doesn't mean the game actually asks you to think like a builder, a trader, or a general from that era. We went looking for the games that do.

This list sticks to games where a real historical period drives the mechanics, not just the artwork. That rules out pure conflict wargames (we've already covered those in our [best tabletop war games list](/best-tabletop-war-games/)) in favor of the builders, traders, and civilization games where history shapes what you're actually doing on your turn: laying trade routes along the Mediterranean, financing canals in Birmingham, or racing rivals to the last artifact in Ra's Egypt.

## What Counts as a Historical Board Game Here

We're using "historical" in the narrower, more honest sense: a specific real-world time and place that the mechanics are actually built around. Rome, the Industrial Revolution, Mesopotamia, Viking-age Scandinavia, not a vague fantasy kingdom with a medieval coat of paint. Some of these games take real liberties with the history (nobody is claiming Puerto Rico's colonial plantation economy is presented with much nuance), but the setting isn't decoration. It's the engine.

We also skipped anything that's primarily a battle simulation. If you want to refight Gettysburg or D-Day, our [war games list](/best-tabletop-war-games/) is the better starting point. This one is for the builders and traders: the games where the fun is watching an empire, a city, or a trade network grow under your hands.

You'll notice some overlap with our [best Euro board games](/best-euro-board-games/) list, and that's not an accident. A lot of the best heavy Euros happen to be set in real history. That list is sorted by weight and mechanical complexity; this one is sorted by setting, so a few titles earn a spot on both for different reasons.

## 1. 7 Wonders ([Full Review Here](/7-wonders-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/7-Wonders.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0043KJW5M/?tag=hexagamers-20)

7 Wonders drops you into one of seven ancient civilizations and has you build it up across three ages, drafting cards for resources, military strength, science, and the wonder itself. Everyone plays at the same time, which is the whole trick of the game. You're not waiting through five other turns to see what's left for you.

It works as a genuine gateway into ancient-world theming because it never slows down to explain history to you. You just feel the arc of a civilization: scrappy resource-scrambling in Age I, military posturing and specialization in Age II, a mad science-and-wonder sprint in Age III. The pyramids, the colossus, the lighthouse: you're racing to actually complete one of the seven ancient wonders before the game ends, which gives the theme a real hook instead of a coat of paint.

At 30 minutes for up to seven players, it's also the fastest game on this list by a wide margin. If the idea of Ancient Rome or Babylon sounds fun but Through the Ages sounds like a slog, this is where you start.

## 2. Through the Ages: A New Story of Civilization

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/through-the-ages.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B071CZT9D9/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If 7 Wonders is the appetizer, Through the Ages is the whole meal, plus dessert, plus a second dinner later that night. You're steering a civilization from antiquity through the modern age, balancing science, culture, military, and government across a genuinely long game. This is the one that goes on BGG's top-ten-of-all-time lists, and it earns the spot by making every decision matter for the rest of the game.

The tension here comes from having to spread yourself thin on purpose. You can't max out your military, your science output, and your happiness track all at once. Every leader you pick, every wonder you build, every action card you take is a trade-off against three or four other things you also want. Leaving your army weak invites a rival's aggression card straight at you.

We'll say the honest part up front: this is not a light game, and it's not a quick one. Games run two to three hours even once everyone knows the rules, and the rulebook alone will scare off a casual group. This is a pick for players who already know they like heavy Euro strategy and want the deepest historical one on the shelf. If that's your table, it's one of the best games ever made, full stop.

## 3. Concordia

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/concordia.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HFDSQP6/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Concordia sets you up as a trading family in the Roman Empire, sending colonists out along real trade routes to found cities and gather goods. There's no combat here at all, which is unusual for a game with "empire" in its theme. This one is entirely about efficient expansion and building an engine out of your hand of role cards.

The card mechanic is the whole game. You have a small hand of Roman god cards (Mercator for selling, Architect for building, Prefect for moving colonists), and each turn you play one and get its ability, then eventually have to buy it back into your hand to use it again. Watching your engine tighten as your cards get more efficient is a satisfying kind of progress that doesn't rely on anyone else messing up.

It scales cleanly from two players up to five, which is more than we can say for most games on this list, and the real map of the Mediterranean and Roman provinces gives it a geography lesson's worth of flavor without ever feeling like homework. It's a strong pick if your group likes building efficient systems more than fighting over territory.

## 4. Brass: Birmingham

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/brass-birmingham.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1988884047/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Brass: Birmingham puts you in charge of an industrial empire during England's Industrial Revolution, running two distinct eras (the Canal Era and the Rail Era) where you build networks, develop industries, and sell goods for score. It's routinely near the top of BGG's all-time rankings, and it's not hard to see why once you've played it.

The double-scoring structure is what makes this one click. You score once at the end of the Canal Era and again at the end of the Rail Era, and both eras use the same board but ask for a completely different strategy. Investments that felt smart early can be worthless later once the canals get torn up, so the game constantly forces you to rethink your plan instead of coasting on an opening lead.

We'll be direct about this one: it's dense. The economic engine of loans, coal, iron, and beer resources takes real effort to learn, and a first game with new players runs long while everyone figures out what's actually happening. It rewards that effort completely once it clicks, but go in knowing you're signing up for a genuine strategy game, not a light history romp.

## 5. Puerto Rico

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/puerto-rico.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01L4H38OC/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Puerto Rico is one of the games that basically invented the modern Euro strategy genre, and it's held up remarkably well for a design from 2002. You're a colonial plantation owner producing goods (corn, sugar, coffee, tobacco), shipping them to Europe for money and points, and building up your island's infrastructure.

The role-selection mechanic is the reason people still play it decades later. Each round, one player picks a role (Builder, Trader, Captain, and so on) that everyone gets to use, but the person who picked it gets a bonus. That means you're constantly reading the table: which role does your opponent need most, and can you pick it first and deny it to them, or does picking it help them more than you?

We'll say plainly that the theme here is a product of its era: it presents colonial plantation economics without much critical framing, and that's worth knowing going in. Mechanically, though, it's still tight and sharp, and if your group likes reading opponents' needs as much as building their own engine, this earns its reputation.

## 6. The Castles of Burgundy

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/castles-of-burgundy.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07R5ZH6FQ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The Castles of Burgundy sets you up as a medieval French noble expanding your holdings in the Duchy of Burgundy, building out a personal hex-tile map of castles, mines, and villages. It's a dice game at heart, but not in the way that word usually means. The dice determine which tiles you're allowed to take and place, not whether your plan succeeds or fails.

That's the clever part. You roll two dice each turn, and the numbers correspond to spots on your personal board and the shared tile market. A roll of 3-5 might let you grab a tile from space 3 and place it on space 5 of your board, but if that spot's already full, you need a different plan. It's puzzle-solving under a light layer of randomness, and good players find ways to make almost any roll useful.

It's a quieter game than most of the others here. There's minimal direct interaction, and the tension comes from racing the clock and your own board space, not from fighting your neighbor. That makes it a strong pick for a group that wants a medieval theme without the elbows-out competitiveness of something like Puerto Rico.

## 7. Tigris & Euphrates

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/tigris-and-euphrates.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1616619104/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Tigris & Euphrates puts you back in ancient Mesopotamia, building kingdoms along the two rivers the game is named for. Reiner Knizia designed this one, and it shows: the scoring is deliberately, almost brutally balanced, forcing you to develop all four of your civilization's areas (agriculture, religion, trade, and leadership) instead of just picking your strongest and running with it.

The catastrophes are what people remember. When two kingdoms of different colors get connected by a tile placement, they collide, and whoever's weaker in that area loses everything they had built there. It's a genuinely tense moment. You can watch a rival's carefully built temple network get wiped out in a single tile placement, and that threat hangs over every turn.

This is not a forgiving game for new players. The rules are simple to explain but the strategic depth underneath them takes real repetition to see, and a first play can leave people confused about why they lost. Stick with it for a second game, though, and the appeal of a genuinely balanced, cutthroat Mesopotamian strategy game becomes obvious.

## 8. Raiders of the North Sea

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/raiders-of-the-north-sea.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BMWB1M78/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Raiders of the North Sea drops you into the Viking Age as a warrior building a crew, gathering provisions, and sailing off to raid villages for plunder, livestock, and glory with the Chieftain. It's a worker-placement game, but with a mean streak: you can place your worker on a spot someone else already occupied and bump their worker back home, taking the action for yourself while they get a small consolation prize.

That displacement mechanic is what sets this apart from most worker placement games, where you're usually just racing to claim a spot before someone else. Here you can outright steal one, which keeps the whole board tense right up until the last turn. Raiding villages specifically is the most satisfying part: you spend resources and workers to sail out, and the payoff of a big pillage run landing is one of the better "yes, that worked" moments in the genre.

At 60-80 minutes for up to four players, it's a manageable length for a mid-weight strategy game, and the Viking theme is baked into every mechanic rather than pasted over a generic Euro chassis. Good pick if your group likes worker placement but wants more teeth than something like Lords of Waterdeep.

## 9. Ra

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ra.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0C3BKP97C/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Ra is set in Ancient Egypt and built entirely around a bidding mechanic that plays out faster and meaner than it sounds. Tiles representing monuments, floods, civilizations, and gold get drawn into an auction track, and players bid their sun tokens to win the whole lot. You're not bidding on individual tiles; you're bidding on the pile as it currently stands.

The tension is in the timing. Do you bid now on a decent-but-not-great set, or wait and hope something better shows up before a Ra tile forces an auction anyway? Waiting too long can leave you empty-handed when the round ends abruptly, and bidding too early can burn a token you needed for something better two turns later. It's a short, sharp game of reading the table and knowing when to pull the trigger.

Another Reiner Knizia design, and like Tigris & Euphrates, it rewards repeated plays once you understand the rhythm of the auction track. At 30-60 minutes it's one of the shorter, lighter games here, which makes it a good entry point if the rest of this list feels intimidating but you still want real Ancient Egypt theming instead of a watered-down version.

## Conclusion

If you want one game that nails historical theming without demanding a whole evening, start with 7 Wonders. It's fast, it scales to a crowd, and the arc from scrappy ancient settlement to wonder-of-the-world finish is genuinely satisfying in 30 minutes. If your group already knows it likes heavy strategy and wants the deepest civilization game on the shelf, Through the Ages and Brass: Birmingham are where the real commitment pays off.

This list skews toward builders and traders on purpose. If what you actually want is refighting real battles, our [best tabletop war games](/best-tabletop-war-games/) list picks up where this one leaves off. And if ancient-world theming specifically is what hooked you, our [7 Wonders review](/7-wonders-review/) is a good next stop.

None of these are cheap games, and a few of them (Through the Ages especially) are a real investment of both money and learning time. We'd say try before you buy where you can. A local game night or a friend's collection is the best way to know if a 2-3 hour civilization game is actually your speed before you commit to the box.
