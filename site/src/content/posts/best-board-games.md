"What's the best board game?"

I get asked this a lot, and the honest answer is a question back: best for who? A game that lands perfectly with four friends who've been playing together for a decade will die on the table at a family Christmas. Catan is either the game that got you into the hobby or the game you're tired of, and which one depends almost entirely on when you first played it.

So this page answers it three ways. First, what actually makes a game good: the handful of dials that decide whether a game fits your table or doesn't. Then two top ten lists: the games our group genuinely plays the most, and the games the wider hobby considers the greats. Those lists are not the same, and the gap between them is more interesting than either one on its own. After that, a full breakdown by genre, so you can skip straight to the kind of game you're actually shopping for.

If you're here for a quick answer: buy [Ticket to Ride: Europe](/ticket-to-ride-europe-review/) if you're new, [Codenames](/codenames-review/) if you need a party game, and [Pandemic](/pandemic-review/) if your group would rather not compete. Everything below is the long version.

## What Makes a Board Game Great?

There's no universal answer, but there are about seven dials. Every game sets them somewhere, and knowing where *you* like them set will save you a lot of money.

### Luck vs. Skill

This is the big one, and people get weird about it.

The gaming snob position is that luck is a flaw, that a "real" game rewards skill and nothing else. Chess has zero luck. It's also a game where a beginner has no chance whatsoever against an experienced player, and that's not fun for the beginner.

Luck is what lets your ten-year-old beat you fair and square. It's what makes a comeback possible in the last round. It's the reason a game night stays social instead of turning into a tournament. Catan hands you dice and a robber, and yes, sometimes the dice decide the game. That's also why a table of mixed skill levels can all have a good time.

The other end has real value too. In [Hive](/hive-review/) there is no luck at all, no hidden information, nothing to blame. If you lost, you were outplayed. Some people find that clarifying and some find it exhausting.

Most good games sit in the middle: enough randomness to keep things unpredictable, enough decisions that the better player wins more often than not. Push-your-luck games like Deep Sea Adventure are the purest version of the tension. The dice decide, but *you* decided how far to push before rolling them.

**Ask yourself:** when you lose, do you want to feel outplayed, or do you want to be able to laugh and blame the dice? Both answers are correct. They just point to different shelves.

### Weight: How Heavy Do You Want It?

Weight is hobby shorthand for complexity, and it mostly translates to one practical number: how long the rules explanation takes.

- **Light (5–10 minutes to teach).** Codenames, Sushi Go!, Love Letter. You explain it, you play it, nobody's confused.
- **Medium (15–25 minutes).** Catan, Ticket to Ride, [Splendor](/splendor-review/), Carcassonne. One or two subsystems to hold in your head.
- **Heavy (30+ minutes, plus a reference sheet).** [Agricola](/agricola-review/), Gloomhaven, Twilight Imperium. Multiple interlocking systems and a first game that mostly teaches you how to play the second one.

The trap is buying above your table's weight. A heavy game that gets played twice is a worse purchase than a light game that gets played forty times. Be honest about who you're playing with, not the group you wish you had.

### Length

Related to weight, but not the same thing. Some heavy games are quick and some light games run long.

The brutal truth about long games: a three-hour game needs everyone to commit an evening in advance. That's a scheduling problem, not a game problem, and it's why so many expensive epics sit on shelves. A 20-minute game gets pulled out on a Tuesday because someone's already there.

Fillers (10–20 minutes) do more work than people credit. They start the night while you wait for the last person to show, and they end it when you've got energy for one more but not for another hour.

### Theme vs. Abstract

Does the theme do any work, or is it a sticker on a math problem?

[Azul](/azul-review/) is about laying tiles in a Portuguese palace. It's beautiful, and the theme is completely irrelevant. You could reskin it as anything. That's fine. Abstracts are clean, they age well, and nothing gets in the way of the decisions.

At the other end, in Robinson Crusoe or Dead of Winter, the theme *is* the experience. When you're rationing food and deciding who goes outside in the snow, the mechanics and the story are doing the same job. Those games generate stories you retell later.

Neither is better. But if your group needs a story to get invested, a gorgeous abstract will leave them cold. And if your group just wants a good puzzle, heavy theme is friction.

### Player Interaction: How Mean Is Your Group?

This one causes more arguments than anything else, and it's worth knowing about your group before you buy.

**Cutthroat / take-that** games let you directly hurt someone. [Coup](/coup-review/), Munchkin, most social deduction. Someone is going to lie to your face and enjoy it.

**Indirect competition** means you're fighting over shared resources but not attacking anyone. Ticket to Ride, where taking the route someone needed is technically an attack but doesn't feel like a betrayal.

**Multiplayer solitaire** is everyone building their own thing with minimal interference. [Wingspan](/wingspan-review/) mostly plays like this: pleasant, low conflict, and some people find it a little lonely.

**Cooperative** removes competition entirely. Everyone against the game.

I've watched a couple genuinely not speak on the drive home over a game of Coup. If your group takes it personally, buy co-ops. Nothing wrong with that.

### Replayability

Will you play it twice? Twenty times?

Variable setup is the cheapest way to get this. Catan's random island, Wingspan's card draw, Carcassonne's tile shuffle. Every game starts differently, so no memorized opening.

Campaign and legacy games go the other direction: a fixed sequence of scenarios where the game changes permanently as you play. Pandemic Legacy is one of the most beloved experiences in the hobby, and you play it about twelve times and then it's done. That's not a con if you know it going in.

Then there are games with a low ceiling that are perfectly fine at what they do. Nobody's playing Flip 7 for the fortieth time to master it. It's a filler, it does its job, and mastery isn't the point.

### Player Count: What It Actually Plays At

Boxes lie. Not deliberately, but "2–6 players" almost never means the game is equally good at 2 and at 6.

Catan says 3–4 and it means it. Two-player Catan is a different, worse game. Party games usually need bodies; Codenames at three is flat and at eight is great. And a lot of heavy strategy games technically support five, but adding a fifth adds twenty minutes of waiting for your turn.

Buy for the count you'll *actually* play at most often, which for most people is either two or four.

---

There's no single best board game. There's a best board game for your table, at the weight your group tolerates, in the length you can actually schedule. That's the whole thesis of this page, and the two lists below should be read with it in mind.

## 1. Codenames ([Full Review Here](/codenames-review/))

[![Codenames](https://img.hexagamers.com/Codenames.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FJMQB63N/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Nothing else on our shelf works with as many different groups. Two teams, a grid of 25 words, and one person per team giving single-word clues to link multiple cards at once. That's it. Non-gamers get it in two minutes, and it's still interesting to people with 300 games at home.

The magic is in being the Spymaster. Finding one word that connects "Ninja," "Bug," and "Beetle" without accidentally pointing at the assassin is genuinely difficult, and watching your team confidently pick the wrong card while you sit there silently is its own kind of pain. The post-clue debrief — where you find out what they *thought* you meant — is half the fun.

Scales from 2 to as many people as you can fit around a table. It's come to family gatherings, work parties, and game nights, and I've never seen it not work.

## 2. Ticket to Ride: Europe ([Full Review Here](/ticket-to-ride-europe-review/))

[![Ticket to Ride: Europe](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000809OAO/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the game we've recommended to more people than any other. You collect coloured train cards and spend them to claim routes across a map of Europe, trying to connect the cities on your secret destination tickets.

The rules take five minutes. The decisions do not get boring. Do you claim the route you need now, or gamble that nobody takes it while you build up a longer play? Every turn is one of three simple options and somehow that's enough.

The Europe edition is the one to buy over the original US version — tunnels, ferries, and train stations add just enough texture, and the stations give you a safety net when someone blocks the route you needed. Works at two, gets properly competitive at four or five.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000809OAO/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 3. The Resistance: Avalon ([Full Review Here](/the-resistance-avalon-review/))

[![The Resistance: Avalon](https://img.hexagamers.com/The-Resistance-Avalon.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B009SAAV0C/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The best social deduction game we've played, and the one that produces the most memorable arguments. Most of the table are loyal servants of Arthur; a few are secretly evil. You propose teams, vote on them, and the evil players quietly sabotage missions while pretending to be as confused as everyone else.

What lifts it above Werewolf is that nobody gets eliminated. You're in it for the whole game, arguing your case the entire time. And Merlin, the good player who knows who the villains are but has to hide that knowledge or lose, is one of the great role designs. Being Merlin is agonizing. You can see exactly what's happening and saying so too clearly gets you killed.

Needs five players minimum and is better at seven or more. If you've got the bodies, this is the pick.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B009SAAV0C/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 4. Azul ([Full Review Here](/azul-review/))

[![Azul](https://img.hexagamers.com/Azul_znrp2h.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B077MZ2MPW/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Heavy resin tiles that feel great in your hand, a board that looks like art when it's finished, and a mean little drafting puzzle hiding underneath.

You take all tiles of one colour from a factory display and the leftovers slide into the middle for the next player. That single rule creates the whole game — you're constantly choosing between what you need and what you're about to hand your opponent. Taking six tiles you can't use, purely so the person after you doesn't get them, is a completely valid and very satisfying move.

Overflow tiles cost you points, so greed is punished directly. It's abstract, the theme is decoration, and it doesn't matter at all. One of the few games that's genuinely beautiful on the table.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B077MZ2MPW/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 5. Agricola ([Full Review Here](/agricola-review/))

[![Agricola](https://img.hexagamers.com/Agricola.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01DREMT68/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The heaviest game on this list and the one I'd defend hardest. You're a farmer with two family members, a shack, and not nearly enough of anything. Every turn you place a worker on an action space, and whoever gets there first takes it — so the pressure comes from watching someone grab the exact thing you'd planned your next three turns around.

The harvest is what makes it. Every few rounds you have to feed your family, and if you can't, you take begging cards and lose points. That deadline turns every decision into a triage problem: expand now or you'll never catch up, but expand too fast and you starve.

It's punishing, it takes 90 minutes, and the first game is mostly about learning what you did wrong. If your group has the appetite for that, few games reward it better.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01DREMT68/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 6. Coup ([Full Review Here](/coup-review/))

[![Coup](https://img.hexagamers.com/Coup-1_mcfcul.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00GDI4HX4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Fifteen cards, two per player, and a game that runs about five minutes. You claim to be characters you may or may not actually have, and anyone can call your bluff — if they're wrong they lose a card, if they're right you do.

The genius is that you can bluff any role from the start. There's no build-up, no waiting for the right moment. You just say "I'm the Duke" on turn one and dare somebody to check. We once had all six players claim Duke in a single round, which is mathematically impossible and extremely funny.

Sometimes you have to Coup your mum even though she made you dinner. It's that kind of game. Fast enough that losing doesn't sting, and you'll always play three more.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00GDI4HX4/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 7. Pandemic ([Full Review Here](/pandemic-review/))

[![Pandemic](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00A2HD40E/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The co-op that taught most people what co-ops are. Four diseases spreading across a world map, everyone playing a specialist with one useful ability, and a deck that keeps making things worse on a timer.

It earns its spot because it's the game we hand to people who don't like losing to their friends. Nobody's a target, everyone's problem-solving together, and the tension of an outbreak chain is genuinely stressful in a good way.

Its real flaw is the quarterback problem — one experienced player can end up directing everyone else's turns, and then four people are watching one person play. Worth naming out loud before you start. Ryan rates it 4.2, a bit lower than the hobby consensus, mostly for that reason.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00A2HD40E/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 8. Dominion ([Full Review Here](/dominion-review/))

[![Dominion](https://img.hexagamers.com/Dominion_z98xnd.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LYLIS2U/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The game that invented deck building. My first impression was fear — no board, tons of different cards, what did I get myself into? That passed after one play.

You start with a deck of ten terrible cards and buy better ones from a shared market, which get shuffled into your deck to come around again. Watching a pile of coppers turn into an engine that draws half your deck in one turn is the whole appeal, and it's still satisfying twenty games in.

The kingdom cards change every game (you pick ten from the box) so it never sets up the same way twice. That variety is why it's outlived a decade of imitators.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LYLIS2U/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 9. Takenoko ([Full Review Here](/takenoko-review/))

[![Takenoko](https://img.hexagamers.com/Takenoko_psyu9h.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0049H9NVW/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A gardener and a very hungry panda on a bamboo garden. It looks like a children's game and plays like a tidy medium-weight puzzle, which is a combination that gets it to the table with almost anybody.

You're completing objective cards: grow bamboo to certain heights, arrange plots in patterns, feed the panda specific colours. And the panda eating the bamboo you just grew is the running frustration that gives the game its personality.

It's gentle. Nobody's getting attacked, the components are charming, and it's the one I reach for when the group wants something with decisions but not conflict.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0049H9NVW/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 10. Catan ([Full Review Here](/catan-review/))

[![Catan](https://img.hexagamers.com/Catan-1.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DYK1ZH2D/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Here's an honest one. Catan is the most-read review on this entire site, and Ryan rates it 3.8, the lowest score in this top ten.

It's here because it matters. Catan is the game that pulled a generation out of Monopoly and showed them what a modern board game could be. Trading resources with the person you're about to block, the settlement race, the robber ruining somebody's night — it invented the vocabulary a lot of us still use.

But the dice can genuinely decide it. Roll badly on a number you built your whole position around and you spend an hour with nothing to do. Newer games solve that problem better. Catan earns its place through influence and through how well it still works on people who've never played a hobby game, not because it's the best-designed game on the shelf.

Play it. Just know that if it doesn't click, the hobby has a lot more to offer.

## 1. Pandemic Legacy: Season 1

[![Pandemic Legacy: Season 1](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-legacy-season-1.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00TQ5SEAI/?tag=hexagamers-20)

For years this sat at the very top of the rankings, and it's still the most talked-about co-op ever made. It's [Pandemic](/pandemic-review/) stretched across a twelve-to-twenty-four game campaign where the board changes permanently — you write on it, tear up cards, and open sealed boxes as the story escalates.

The catch is that you play it once. That's the deal, and most people who've done it say it was worth the price for that single run. Play with the same group from start to finish.

## 2. Gloomhaven

[![Gloomhaven](https://img.hexagamers.com/Gloomhaven_tvaycr.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H4HZ1JCJ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A twenty-pound box containing a hundred-scenario dungeon crawl campaign. You build a character, play cards to fight through tactical combat scenarios, unlock new classes, and gradually reshape a persistent world.

It is enormous — the kind of purchase that becomes your group's project for a year. If that sounds like commitment rather than fun, **Gloomhaven: Jaws of the Lion** is the smaller entry point and teaches you the system as you go.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H4HZ1JCJ/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 3. Terraforming Mars

[![Terraforming Mars](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/terraforming-mars.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01GSYA4K2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Corporations competing to make Mars habitable — raising temperature, adding oxygen, placing oceans. It's an engine builder where the cards are the whole game, and the tableau you assemble by the end can chain into absurd combos.

The production is famously plain and the table takes up a lot of room. Nobody cares, because the card play is that good.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01GSYA4K2/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 4. Wingspan ([Full Review Here](/wingspan-review/))

[![Wingspan](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/wingspan.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The game that proved a beautiful, calm, bird-themed engine builder could be a mainstream hit. You attract birds to three habitats, and each one you play makes the row it sits in better — so your later turns cascade off the work you did early.

Won the Kennerspiel des Jahres in 2019 and brought a lot of people into medium-weight games. Low conflict, gorgeous cards, and a genuinely clever engine underneath the nature documentary.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 5. Root ([Full Review Here](/root-review/))

[![Root](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/root.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F454YF3/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Adorable woodland animals fighting a brutal asymmetric war. Every faction plays by completely different rules — the Marquise builds an industrial economy, the Eyrie is locked into a rigid decree that punishes mistakes, the Vagabond wanders around making friends and enemies.

That asymmetry is the draw and the barrier. Teaching it means teaching four different games at once, and a table where one player understands their faction and the others don't goes badly. Get past that and there's nothing else quite like it.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F454YF3/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 6. Twilight Struggle

[![Twilight Struggle](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/twilight-struggle.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007XDRP1E/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Two players, the entire Cold War, and a card deck that constantly forces you to help your opponent. Play a card for its operations value and you trigger its historical event — which is frequently good for the other side. That tension is the whole design.

It held the number one spot on the global rankings for years. Strictly two-player, strictly for people who want a long, tense, deeply historical duel.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007XDRP1E/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## 7. Scythe

[![Scythe](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/scythe.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01IPUGYK6/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Alternate-history 1920s Europe with mechs, gorgeous Jakub Rozalski art, and an engine-building core that's far more peaceful than the box suggests. Combat exists but happens rarely — most of the game is efficiently upgrading your little economy.

Asymmetric factions and variable player mats mean the setup changes significantly every time. One of the best-produced games in the hobby.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01IPUGYK6/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

## Where We Disagree With The Internet

The two lists above barely overlap, and that's the honest part of this page.

**The consensus over-indexes on heavy games.** Rankings are voted on by people deep in the hobby, and those people own a lot of games and have dedicated groups. That skews the results toward 90-minute strategy games that a normal table will never schedule. Gloomhaven is a fantastic design. It is also a year-long commitment that most groups quietly abandon four scenarios in. Our list has more short games on it because short games are what actually get played.

**Party games get no respect in rankings and enormous respect at real tables.** Codenames is our most-played game by a distance and it doesn't sniff most all-time top tens. Neither does Coup. Ranking systems reward depth, and depth isn't what you need at 11pm with seven people and three drinks in.

**Betrayal at House on the Hill is the one we wanted to love and sold.** It's beloved by a lot of people and the concept is genuinely great — explore a haunted house together, then one player turns on everyone. We gave it many chances. The games ran long, turns dragged, the difficulty felt punishing, and even our wins felt unsatisfying. It's the only game on the site with a [full review](/betrayal-at-house-on-the-hill-review/) that we eventually got rid of. Maybe we were playing it wrong. It happens.

**And Catan is underrated by the hobby and overrated by everyone else.** Enthusiasts have moved on and call it dated. Meanwhile it remains the single best tool for showing a non-gamer that board games got good. Both things are true.

The takeaway: use consensus lists to find well-designed games, not to find *your* games. The second one is what the rest of this page is for.

## Best Board Games By Type

## Gateway Games

**What it is:** the games you hand someone who thinks board games means Monopoly. Simple enough to teach in ten minutes, deep enough that they realise there's a whole hobby here.

**How it plays:** a gateway game gives you two or three options on your turn and makes all of them interesting. There's usually one core decision repeated — claim a route, place a tile, buy a card — and the skill is in timing and priorities rather than memorizing rules. Crucially, you can't really lose on turn one. Bad decisions cost you points, not the whole game.

**Variations:** some gateways lean on negotiation and player interaction (Catan), some are almost solitaire with a shared board (Ticket to Ride), and some are pure pattern-matching (Kingdomino, Azul). If your beginner is competitive, the trading-and-blocking style works. If they're shy, give them something they can build quietly.

## Party Games

Games for a lot of people, most of whom aren't gamers, often at a table where the game isn't the only thing happening.

Turns are short or simultaneous, rules fit on a card, and the fun comes from what people *say* rather than what they do mechanically. A good party game is really a machine for generating conversation. Most of them barely have strategy, and that's the design working correctly.

There's a real split here. **Word and clue games** (Codenames, Just One, So Clover!) reward cleverness and work with almost anyone. **Performance games** (Telestrations, Time's Up!) require people willing to be a bit silly. **Trivia and betting games** (Wits and Wagers) let people who know nothing still compete. Know which one your group tolerates — handing a shy table a performance game is a bad night.

## Cooperative Games

**What it is:** everyone plays as a team against the game itself. You all win or you all lose.

**How it plays:** the game gets a turn too, and its turn is always bad for you. You spend your actions triaging — this fire is spreading, that disease is about to outbreak, and you have three actions and four problems. The conversation is the gameplay: everyone talking through the board state and deciding who does what.

**Variations:** the big divide is **communication**. Traditional co-ops (Pandemic, Forbidden Desert) let you talk freely, which is friendly but creates the quarterback problem — one confident player running everyone's turn. **Limited-communication co-ops** (Sky Team, The Mind, Hanabi) fix that by banning table talk, and the silence is where the tension comes from. There are also **traitor games** (Dead of Winter) where the team might contain someone working against you.

## Social Deduction Games

Somebody at the table is lying, and the game is figuring out who. The most emotionally intense genre in the hobby.

You're given a secret role. Most players are on the good team and know nothing; a few are on the bad team and know everything. The good players are trying to extract information from behaviour, and the bad players are trying to look exactly like confused good players. Almost all of the game happens in conversation — the mechanics just create reasons to talk.

This is the genre with the clearest sub-splits, and they play completely differently.

- **Hidden roles with bluffing:** [Avalon](/the-resistance-avalon-review/), Secret Hitler, [One Night Ultimate Werewolf](/one-night-ultimate-werewolf-review/). You have a secret team and you lie about it all game.
- **Bluffing without hidden roles:** [Coup](/coup-review/). Everyone knows the situation; you're lying about which cards you hold, and anyone can challenge at any time.
- **Deduction without bluffing:** [Love Letter](/love-letter-review/), Deception: Murder in Hong Kong. You're reading limited information and drawing conclusions, but nobody's putting on a performance.

That distinction matters for buying. Groups that hate lying to each other can still love Love Letter, and groups that love the theatre of it want Avalon.

## Deck Building Games

**What it is:** you start with a deck of weak cards and spend the game buying better ones, which get shuffled in and come back around. Your deck *is* your score and your engine at the same time.

**How it plays:** the first few turns are deliberately unsatisfying — you're playing coppers and estates and wondering what the point is. Then around turn six your first real purchase comes back around, and by the end you're drawing half your deck in a single turn. That arc from useless to powerful is the entire appeal.

**Variations:** **pure deck builders** (Dominion) have no board at all — just a market of cards. **Deck builders with a board** (Clank!, Star Realms with its combat) attach the engine to some other activity, so your cards drive movement or fighting. And **deck destruction** matters as much as building: knowing when to *thin* your deck by trashing weak cards is what separates good players from new ones.

**Our top picks:** [Dominion](/dominion-review/) is the original and still the cleanest expression of the idea — ten kingdom cards from a big box, different every game. It's the one to learn on.

## Worker Placement Games

You have a few workers and there are more useful things to do than workers to do them. Place one on an action space and it's yours — and nobody else can have it.

The whole genre runs on blocking. You look at the board, see the space you desperately need, look at the turn order, and calculate whether you can afford to take something else first. Somebody takes it. That specific frustration is the genre working as designed.

The split that matters is how badly the game punishes you. **Harsh worker placement** (Agricola, Caverna) adds survival pressure — you must feed your workers, and failing has real penalties. **Friendly worker placement** ([Lords of Waterdeep](/lords-of-waterdeep-review/), [Stone Age](/stone-age-review/)) removes the starvation clock and just lets you build. Newer designs often let you place on occupied spaces for an extra cost, which softens the blocking considerably.

## Card Drafting Games

**What it is:** you're handed a set of cards, pick one, and pass the rest to your neighbour. Repeat until they're gone.

**How it plays:** drafting is a game of reading what's in front of you and what you're about to give away. Take the card that's best for you, or take the card that would be devastating in the hands of the person to your left? Because everyone picks at the same time, drafting games play a lot of people quickly — the main reason the mechanic is everywhere.

**Variations:** **pass-the-hand drafting** ([7 Wonders](/7-wonders-review/), Sushi Go!) is the classic form. **Open drafting** ([Azul](/azul-review/), Splendor's card market) puts everything face up on the table and takes turns — same tension, no hidden information. And plenty of games bolt drafting onto something bigger, using it as the engine that feeds a longer build.

## Engine Building Games

You spend the early game building a machine and the late game watching it run. Each piece you add makes the next turn better.

The defining feeling is the turn where it clicks — where the card you played six turns ago triggers the one you played three turns ago and suddenly you're taking four actions instead of one. Early turns are slow and feel unproductive, which is the deal you're making. The skill is knowing when to stop building and start scoring, because the game ends whether your engine is finished or not.

Engines come in a few shapes. **Tableau builders** ([Wingspan](/wingspan-review/), [Everdell](/everdell-review/), Terraforming Mars) have you laying cards in front of you that combo together. **Deck builders** are technically engine builders where the engine is your deck. And **economic engines** (Scythe, Splendor) build production rates rather than card combos — you're increasing how much you get per turn rather than how many things trigger.

## Two-Player Games

**What it is:** games designed for exactly two, not games that technically support two.

**How it plays:** with only one opponent there's nowhere to hide. Every move you make is directly aimed at one person, and they know it. Good two-player designs lean into that — tight, tense, and usually shorter than multiplayer games because there's no downtime waiting for four other people.

**Variations:** **abstract duels** ([Hive](/hive-review/), chess-likes) are pure skill with no luck and no hidden information. **Card-driven duels** (7 Wonders Duel, Twilight Struggle) add hand management and hidden plans. And **two-player co-ops** (Sky Team, Pandemic at two) turn the format cooperative, which is the reason a lot of couples buy games at all.

## Solo Games

Games you play by yourself, either designed that way or with a proper solo mode bolted on.

Without an opponent, the game needs to push back some other way — usually a deck of cards or a rules-driven automa that takes its own turns. The good ones feel like a puzzle with a clock. The bad ones feel like solitaire with extra steps.

Solo modes solve the missing-opponent problem in different ways. **Beat-your-score** games (Sprawlopolis, most puzzle solos) just want you to optimize. **Automa-driven** solos (Wingspan, Scythe) simulate an opponent with a card deck. And **campaign solos** (Gloomhaven, Robinson Crusoe) give you a long-form story to work through over weeks.

## Bluffing Games

**What it is:** games where lying convincingly is the primary skill. Overlaps with social deduction, but bluffing games often have no hidden teams — just hidden information you're misrepresenting.

**How it plays:** you make a claim, and the table decides whether to believe you. The tension is entirely in the moment of challenge. Good bluffing games make lying *necessary* — if you only ever tell the truth you'll lose, so everyone has to get comfortable with it.

**Variations:** **claim-and-challenge** ([Coup](/coup-review/), Liar's Dice) has you asserting something anyone can call out. **Bidding bluffs** (Skull) commit you before the reveal. And **hidden-role bluffs** ([Avalon](/the-resistance-avalon-review/)) tie it to a team game.

## Family Games

Games that work with a genuinely mixed table — kids, parents, and grandparents all playing the same game without anyone being bored or lost.

The design problem is making a game a nine-year-old and a forty-year-old can both enjoy. Usually solved with simple rules and a decision space that rewards thinking without punishing not thinking. Short turns matter a lot, because kids don't wait well.

Some family games are just light strategy games ([Azul](/azul-review/), Kingdomino). Others use dexterity or speed to level the field, since a fast kid genuinely beats a slow adult. And a few handicap naturally — [Ghost Blitz](/ghost-blitz-review/) is a reaction game where being an adult helps you not at all.

## Dexterity Games

**What it is:** games where you physically do something (flick, stack, balance, or drop) and your hands decide the outcome.

**How it plays:** no amount of strategy saves you if your flick goes wide. That's the appeal: they're loud, everyone watches every turn, and there's no downtime because failure is entertaining. They also completely bypass the experience gap, which makes them great with mixed groups.

**Variations:** **flicking** (Crokinole, Flick 'em Up), **stacking and balance** (Rhino Hero, Junk Art, Suspend), and **speed and reaction** (Ice Cool, [Ghost Blitz](/ghost-blitz-review/)). Crokinole in particular is less a party game than a genuine lifelong skill game.

## Micro and Pocket Games

Complete games in tiny boxes. Usually under twenty cards, usually under fifteen minutes.

The constraint forces designers to be ruthless. Every card has to matter, so micro games often have a higher decision-per-minute rate than games ten times their size. They're what you bring camping, to a restaurant, or on a plane.

The category splits by what's in the box. **Card-only micros** ([Love Letter](/love-letter-review/), No Thanks!, Scout) fit in a pocket. **Tiny box games with components** (Deep Sea Adventure, Tiny Epic anything) squeeze real board game systems into a small footprint. And **solo micros** (Sprawlopolis) are eighteen cards and a genuine puzzle.

## War Games

**What it is:** games about military conflict, from beer-and-pretzels skirmishes to multi-hour historical simulations.

**How it plays:** most war games are about position and timing rather than dice. You're managing supply, terrain, and where your opponent *can't* be next turn. The heavier end asks you to learn a system, not just a ruleset — first plays are about understanding the shape of the conflict.

**Variations:** **card-driven** (Twilight Struggle) uses a deck to drive events. **Area control** (Root, Scythe) dresses conflict in lighter clothes. **Hex-and-counter** (Memoir '44, Commands & Colors) is the classic map-and-units form. And **epic** (Twilight Imperium, War of the Ring) means clearing your calendar.

## Zombie and Horror Games

Games where the theme is survival against something that wants to eat you. The genre where atmosphere does the heavy lifting.

Usually a resource squeeze dressed as a story — never enough ammo, food, or actions, and the threat escalating every round. The best ones use the theme to justify genuinely hard decisions about who gets left behind.

The theme gets used four different ways: **pure co-op survival** (Zombicide), **traitor games** (Dead of Winter, where one survivor may be working against the group), **push-your-luck** (Zombie Dice), and **kid-friendly horror** (Zombie Kidz Evolution, which is a legacy game for six-year-olds and genuinely good).

## How to Choose Your Next Game

Short version, based on the dials at the top of this page:

- **Buying your first real board game?** [Ticket to Ride: Europe](/ticket-to-ride-europe-review/). Start at [gateway games](/best-gateway-board-games-for-beginners-with-reviews/).
- **Big group, mixed crowd, drinks involved?** [Codenames](/codenames-review/). Then [party games](/best-party-board-games-with-reviews/).
- **Your group takes competition personally?** Go [cooperative](/best-cooperative-board-games/) and never look back.
- **Your group loves lying to each other?** [Social deduction](/best-social-deduction-board-games/), starting with [Avalon](/the-resistance-avalon-review/).
- **Just the two of you?** [Two player games](/best-two-player-board-games/) — and buy games designed for two, not games that tolerate it.
- **Playing by yourself?** [Solo games](/best-one-player-solo-board-games-with-reviews/) are a real category now, not an afterthought.
- **Want the puzzle without the conflict?** [Engine builders](/best-engine-building-board-games/) — [Wingspan](/wingspan-review/) or [Everdell](/everdell-review/).
- **Ready for something heavy?** [Worker placement](/best-worker-placement-board-games-reviews/), starting with [Lords of Waterdeep](/lords-of-waterdeep-review/) and working up to [Agricola](/agricola-review/).
- **Need something for the cabin or a flight?** [Micro games](/best-micro-games/).

The most common expensive mistake is buying above your table's weight — a beautiful heavy game that gets played twice. The second most common is buying for the group you wish you had. Buy for the people who actually show up.

## Conclusion

There isn't a best board game, and anyone who gives you a straight answer is telling you about themselves rather than about games. What there is: a best game for four adults who want to talk while they play, a best game for two people on a Tuesday, a best game for a table that includes a seven-year-old.

If you take one thing from this page, take the dials — weight, length, and how mean your group is. Get those three right and almost anything you buy will land. Get them wrong and even a masterpiece sits in the closet.

Our shelf says the games that survive are the ones that are easy to say yes to. That's why [Codenames](/codenames-review/) has more plays than anything else we own, and why the heavy stuff we love comes out four times a year. Both kinds are worth having. Just be honest about the ratio.
