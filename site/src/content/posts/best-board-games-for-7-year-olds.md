Seven is the age where kids graduate out of the kids' section. They've outgrown colour-matching and roll-and-move, they can sit still for half an hour, and they can handle losing a game without the evening ending. But most of them still aren't fluent readers, and a wall of card text will stop a game dead.

That gap is what this list is for. Everything here asks a seven-year-old to make a real decision, not just spin a spinner and move, while keeping the reading load low enough that they can play without an adult reading every card aloud.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](/best-board-games-for-7-year-olds#table/)

We had two hard rules picking these. First, there has to be an actual choice on your turn. A game where the dice decide everything teaches a kid nothing and bores the adult playing with them. Second, a seven-year-old has to be able to play it mostly on their own steam. A few of these need help adding up the score at the end, and we've flagged those, but none of them require a parent narrating the rules the whole way through.

Worth saying plainly: several games here are boxed 8+. Publisher ages skew conservative, and the age on the box is about who can play *unassisted*, not who can enjoy it. Every one of these is widely played by sevens. Where a game genuinely runs hot for a sensitive kid, we've said so in the entry rather than burying it.

If your seven-year-old has a younger sibling elbowing into games night, our [best board games for kindergarten](/best-board-games-for-kindergarten/) list steps things down. Going the other way, most of these graduate naturally into our [best family board games](/best-family-board-games/) picks.

## 1. Sushi Go!

[![Sushi Go!](https://img.hexagamers.com/Sushi-Go_ocbhis.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00J57VU44/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Sushi Go! is the cleanest introduction to card drafting we've found, and card drafting is one of the genuinely great board game mechanics. You get a hand of cards, pick one, and pass the rest to the player beside you. Everyone reveals at once, and the hand keeps circling until it's gone.

The reason it works so well at seven is that the whole game is pictures. Dumplings, sashimi, a little tempura. Kids read the icons instantly and never once ask what a card does. But underneath that, they're making a real drafting decision: take the card that helps me, or take the card that would have helped the person I'm passing to. Watching a seven-year-old figure that out for the first time is genuinely great.

The one thing to know is that scoring happens at the end and involves some adding, plus a couple of sets that score in odd ways. Expect to tally it for them the first few games. That's the only adult intervention it needs.

It's cheap, it's fifteen minutes, and it fits in a coat pocket. If you're buying one game off this list, this is the one with the best ratio of teaching effort to payoff. Sushi Go Party! is the bigger box if you want more variety and up to eight players.

## 2. Zombie Kidz Evolution

[![Zombie Kidz Evolution](https://img.hexagamers.com/Zombie_Kidz_Evolution_ncj82a.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07M5BFVP2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Boxed 7+, and it's the most on-the-nose age match on this list. Zombie Kidz is a cooperative game where you're kids defending a school from zombies, and each game takes about fifteen minutes.

Here's the part that makes it special. It's a legacy game, which means it changes permanently as you play. Win a few games and you unlock a sealed envelope with new rules inside, or peel a sticker onto the board that stays there forever. Kids lose their minds over this. The "can we play again" rate on this game is higher than anything else we've put in front of that age group, because there's always another envelope waiting.

Being cooperative also solves the losing problem. Everyone wins or loses together, so there's no meltdown over a sibling winning, and an adult can play alongside without it feeling like a handicap match.

The honest caveat: the campaign runs out. You'll get somewhere around a dozen or so plays before you've opened everything, and after that it's a decent-but-ordinary co-op game. For the price, we still think that arc is worth it, and Zombie Teenz Evolution picks up where it leaves off when they're ready.

## 3. Ticket to Ride ([Full Review Here](/ticket-to-ride-europe-review/))

[![Ticket to Ride](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F8PKN4B7/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Seven is the year Ticket to Ride starts working, and it's a big moment because this is a full-fat hobby game with no training wheels on it. Collect coloured train cards, spend matching sets to claim routes on the map, and connect the cities on your secret destination tickets.

There's essentially no reading. City names and colours, that's the entire literacy requirement. But the decision is real and it's a good one: claim a short route now and bank it, or hold out for the long route that scores far more but might get taken out from under you. Kids feel that tension immediately.

Two honest warnings. It runs long, up to an hour, which is right at the ceiling for this age, so expect some wriggling near the end. And getting your route blocked genuinely stings. Some seven-year-olds take that fine and some take it very badly, and you know which one you've got.

If yours is on the younger or more sensitive end, Ticket to Ride: First Journey is the 6+ version at about fifteen minutes. It's a good bridge, but it's thin enough that they'll want the real thing within a year, so don't buy it for a confident seven-year-old. Our full review covers the Europe map, which adds tunnels and stations and is our pick of the versions.

## 4. Kingdomino

[![Kingdomino](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/kingdomino.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01N3A4070/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Kingdomino won the Spiel des Jahres in 2017, which is the biggest prize in board games, and it plays in under twenty minutes. You're laying domino-shaped tiles to build a five-by-five kingdom, matching terrain to terrain.

The clever bit is how you pick tiles. The better the tile you take this round, the later you choose next round. That's it. One rule, and it creates a genuine strategic squeeze that a seven-year-old can feel without being able to articulate. Take the great tile and pay for it, or take the mediocre one and get first pick next time. There is no reading at all.

The catch is the scoring, which multiplies the size of each terrain area by the number of crowns in it. That's above grade-two maths, so you'll be scoring it for them for a while. It doesn't spoil anything — the kid still makes every decision, they just don't do the final arithmetic.

Some kids find it a bit dry next to the games with monsters and zombies on the box. But if yours likes puzzles, this is the most elegant game on the list.

## 5. ICECOOL

[![ICECOOL](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ice-cool.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01GDHGYHG/?tag=hexagamers-20)

ICECOOL is a flicking game, and it does something none of the others here can: it lets a seven-year-old beat an adult fair and square, on skill, with nobody going easy on them.

You flick weighted penguins around a school built out of the game's own nested boxes, chasing fish and dodging the hall monitor. The penguins are the whole trick. They're rounded and bottom-weighted, so with a bit of practice you can curve a shot around a corner or make one jump a wall. Kids figure out the curve shot fast and then want to show everyone.

It won the Kinderspiel des Jahres in 2017, the kids' version of that same big award, and the physical build is a real part of why. Setup is a minute of slotting boxes together and it looks great on the table.

This is dexterity, not strategy, so it doesn't scratch the same itch as Kingdomino or Ticket to Ride. Treat it as the game you pull out when you want energy rather than thinking. The boxes can warp a little over time if you store it badly, which is the only durability knock.

## 6. King of Tokyo

[![King of Tokyo](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/king-of-tokyo.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01F46RPS4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Giant monsters stomping Tokyo, resolved with Yahtzee-style dice. Roll six dice, keep the ones you want, reroll twice. Claws attack, hearts heal, numbers score points, and energy buys power-up cards.

The push-your-luck decision is the good part. You're two rerolls in, you've got a decent spread, and you have to decide whether to bank it or gamble the lot on one more roll. That's a real risk assessment, and it's the kind kids actually internalise because the consequence lands immediately.

Two things to weigh before buying. There's player elimination. Get knocked out and you're watching, though games are short enough that it's rarely a long wait. And it's directly confrontational: you attack the other players, and the other players attack you. For a lot of seven-year-olds that's the appeal. For a sensitive one it's the reason to pick Zombie Kidz instead.

The power cards also carry real text, which makes this the heaviest reading load on the list. A seven-year-old who reads a bit will manage with occasional help; one who doesn't will need you nearby.

## 7. Blokus

[![Blokus](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/blokus.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00FBWBM3G/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Blokus is boxed 7+ and it has exactly one rule: your pieces have to touch at the corners, never along the edges. You can teach it in under a minute, including to a grandparent who claims not to like board games.

Everyone has a set of Tetris-ish shapes and takes turns placing them, trying to fit as many as possible on the board before running out of legal spots. There is no reading, no luck, and no dice. It's pure spatial reasoning, which is a genuinely useful thing to be practising at seven, and the translucent plastic pieces feel good in the hand.

The flip side of no luck is that an adult playing properly will win every time. That's the one real drawback, and it's worth handling up front. Either play at partial strength or accept that the kid needs a few months to catch up. Losing here is also unambiguous, with no dice to blame it on.

It's a good one for a kid who likes puzzles more than stories, and it's the game on this list most likely to still be in the cupboard in ten years.

## 8. Dragonwood

[![Dragonwood](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/dragonwood.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00UB7OV7K/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Dragonwood is the closest thing here to a first adventure game. You collect cards in your hand, then spend sets of them to earn dice, then roll those dice against a target number to capture goblins, ogres and eventually dragons.

It teaches three things at once without announcing any of them. Playing a run of cards is sequencing, playing matching colours is set collection, and deciding whether your five dice are enough to attempt the dragon is a probability judgement. That last one is the real lesson — kids learn quickly that a big monster needs a big hand, and that gambling on a weak roll usually ends badly.

The dice rolling gives it a swinginess that keeps it fun and stops it feeling like homework. It also means a seven-year-old can beat you without any help, which matters more than it should.

The creature cards do have text on them, so it's a step up in reading from Sushi Go. And because so much rides on the dice, the strategy is thinner than Kingdomino's. It's the more exciting game, not the deeper one.

## 9. Ghost Fightin' Treasure Hunters

[![Ghost Fightin' Treasure Hunters](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ghost-fightin-treasure-hunters.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CPKG6RK8/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A cooperative haunted-house game that won the Kinderspiel des Jahres in 2014. Your team moves through a mansion hauling treasure out the front door while ghosts keep spawning behind you, and if the house fills up with ghosts, everyone loses.

What makes it good for seven is the constant tension between two jobs. Someone has to be fighting ghosts back and someone has to be carrying treasure, and treasure slows you down. That forces an actual team conversation every turn: who's grabbing, who's covering, who's coming back to help. It's the most talking-out-loud strategy of anything on this list.

It's longer than most here, up to about forty-five minutes, so it's better as the main event than a filler.

The thing to watch is quarterbacking. Co-op games let a confident adult quietly run everyone's turn, and that hollows the game out for the kid. Sit on your hands, ask questions instead of giving instructions, and let them make the wrong call sometimes. The Anniversary Edition is the one currently in print and supports up to five players.

## 10. Hey, That's My Fish!

[![Hey, That's My Fish!](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/heys-my-fish.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0CGVZ4843/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Small box, twenty minutes, and a lesson that lands harder than anything else on this list. Your penguins slide across ice tiles collecting fish, and every tile you leave is removed from the board. The ice you're standing on is disappearing as you eat.

Kids get stranded on their own private island of ice about once per game before it clicks, and once it clicks they never forget it. Suddenly they're thinking two moves ahead about escape routes rather than just grabbing the nearest three-fish tile. For a first lesson in consequences, it's hard to beat, and there's no reading at all.

It's cheap and it packs down tiny, which makes it a good restaurant or travel game.

Fair warning that it can play mean. Cutting someone off from the rest of the board is a legitimate and very effective move, and at four players it happens a lot. Some tables love that and some don't. It's also the most abstract game here — penguins and fish, but no story to speak of.

## Conclusion

If you want one game and you want it to work tonight, buy Sushi Go! It's cheap, it teaches in three minutes, and the drafting decision underneath it is the real thing. If you want the game your kid begs to play again, Zombie Kidz Evolution and its stack of sealed envelopes wins that contest outright. And if you're ready to hand a seven-year-old a proper hobby game with no concessions made, Ticket to Ride is the one that finally fits.

This age is a great one to buy for, because almost nothing you get now becomes obsolete in a year. Most of these will still be getting played at ten. When they're ready for the next step, our [best family board games](/best-family-board-games/) list is where this one leads, and the [best cooperative board games](/best-cooperative-board-games/) picks are worth a look if the team-versus-the-board format is what's landing at your table.
