Battleship looks like a guessing game. It mostly isn't. We pull it out on nights when everyone's tired and just wants something dumb and easy, and even then, some people in the group win way more than they should if this were pure luck. There's a real layer of strategy under that coin-flip surface, on both sides of the board: how you hide your fleet, and how you hunt for theirs.

This isn't a rules refresher. Those are printed right on the inside of the box lid and take about ninety seconds to read. This is what to actually do once the pegs start going in.

## Where you put your fleet matters more than you think

Most people place their ships the way they'd arrange furniture, wherever looks tidy. That's a mistake. A decent opponent isn't guessing randomly, so predictable placement gets found fast.

Spread your ships out instead of lining them up. Clustering your fleet in one corner, or running every ship in neat parallel rows, means one lucky hit gives away the general neighborhood of two or three ships at once. Put some near the edges and some more central, so a hit on one ship tells your opponent almost nothing about where the rest are.

Edges and corners feel safer than they are. There's an instinct to tuck ships along the border because it feels like it removes a direction the opponent can search from. Experienced players know that instinct exists too, so they search edges just as hard, sometimes harder. Put at least one ship dead center, where the intuition says not to.

Watch for your own symmetry habits. If you put your 4-cell Battleship vertically on the left side, don't balance it out by putting your 3-cell Submarine vertically on the right. That's a pattern, and if you play the same person more than once, they'll start reading it. Mix horizontal and vertical placement instead of defaulting to one.

Leave gaps between ships when you can. Two ships touching, or sitting just one cell apart, means a single hit can chain straight into the next ship's discovery, since the first thing anyone does after a hit is probe the cells around it.

Your smallest ship, the 2-cell Destroyer, is genuinely hard to hide and hard to hunt either way. Don't overthink its placement. Save that instinct for the bigger ships, which are easier to stumble into by accident.

## The checkerboard search pattern

If you're still hunting cell by cell in a plain grid, top-left to bottom-right, you're leaving free wins on the table. The single biggest upgrade to your search is switching to a checkerboard pattern.

Here's why it works: every ship in Battleship is at least 2 cells long, and no matter how a ship sits, horizontal or vertical, it has to occupy at least one cell of each color if you picture the board like a checkerboard. So instead of firing at all 100 cells to guarantee coverage, firing at just the cells of one color, roughly half the board, guarantees you'll eventually hit every ship at least once.

In practice: picture the board like a chessboard, and only fire where the row and column numbers add up to an even total (or all odd, pick one and stick with it). Skip everything else during your search phase. You're not trying to sink anything yet, just find it.

The Destroyer is the only wrinkle. At 2 cells, standard checkerboard spacing is already tight enough to guarantee you can't miss it, so you don't need anything fancier than the basic pattern for the base game.

## What to do the moment you get a hit

A lot of players get sloppy right here. They get a hit, get excited, and fire at some random neighboring cell.

Go cross-shaped first: the instant you get a hit, fire at all four adjacent cells, up, down, left, and right, before doing anything else. One of them will connect, unless you happened to hit the very end of the ship. Don't guess a direction; test all four.

Once your second shot connects, switch to linear. Two hits in a row tells you the ship's orientation, so stop probing in a cross and follow the line both directions until it's sunk or you run off the edge of the board.

Finish what you start. It's tempting to bounce to a new lead the moment something else looks promising, but a partially hit ship is one your opponent knows you're onto, and every turn you leave it exposed is a turn wasted. Sink it, then go back to searching.

Don't fully write off the area around a ship you just sank. The setup rules most people play with keep ships from touching, but that zone just outside a sunk ship's footprint is still marginally more interesting early on than a cold patch of board you haven't touched at all.

## Thinking in probabilities

Checkerboard search covers most of the advantage on its own. The next step up, and how genuinely strong players actually think, is weighting cells by how many ways a remaining ship could occupy them.

For any ship still afloat, the center of the board has more valid placements running through it than the edges do. A corner cell might only fit a handful of orientations; a central cell could be part of dozens. That's why favoring the middle, once you're past the opening search, tends to pay off.

Update as ships go down. Once the Destroyer is sunk, stop reserving any mental weight for a 2-length ship and recalculate around whatever's left. Since the remaining ships are longer, this naturally pushes your shots toward more open space.

You don't need to map out full probability density for a normal game night. That's closer to something you'd bother coding a bot to do. The practical version is just: favor the center over the edges, favor open space over corners, and switch to linear follow-up as soon as you land a hit.

## Mistakes that sink your own game

- Firing in a straight scan pattern, row by row, instead of checkerboard
- Abandoning a hit too early, before confirming the ship's full length and direction
- Clustering your own fleet, a habit almost everyone has to unlearn on purpose
- Undervaluing the center, both when placing ships too cautiously and when searching
- Placing the same way every game against the same opponent, until they notice

## Our take

Battleship comes out at our game nights more as a nostalgia pick than a serious one, and it's a little funny how fast that changes once someone actually plays it with intent instead of vibes. Checkerboard search alone is enough to turn a 50/50 coin-flip game into something you can meaningfully get better at, and once your opponent starts losing more than they should, they usually want to know why.

It's a five-minute game with a real skill ceiling underneath, which isn't a common combination. Worth pulling back out of the closet.
