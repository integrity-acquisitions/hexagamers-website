Everyone's played Risk once. Fewer people have played it *again* — a lot of tables set the box down after one 4-hour session and never open it a second time. That's not really Risk's fault so much as it's the fault of playing the base game with six people and no house rules, but it's given the whole franchise a reputation problem.

What most people don't realize is how many times Hasbro and its predecessors have gone back and actually fixed the things that make classic Risk drag. New victory conditions, turn limits, asymmetric factions, entirely different combat systems — some of these versions barely resemble the game your uncle owns. A few of them are genuinely some of the best strategy games on the market, Risk name or not.

We went through the version that regularly tops BoardGameGeek's Risk rankings, the ones enthusiast blogs keep coming back to, and the licensed editions that are actually worth the shelf space versus the ones that are reskins for the sake of a reskin. The list below is ranked by what actually holds up at the table, not by nostalgia.

One thing worth saying up front: the "best" Risk almost never means classic Risk. If you want the purest version of the conquest fantasy without the parts that make people quit halfway through, several entries here do that better than the original.

## How Many Versions of Risk Are There?

Dozens. Hasbro has licensed Risk into everything from Star Wars to The Walking Dead to Metal Gear Solid, and that's on top of the mechanically distinct editions — Legacy, 2210 A.D., Europe, Godstorm — that change how the game is actually played rather than just what's printed on the board.

Most of those licensed versions use the standard ruleset with new artwork and renamed territories. That's fine if the theme is one you love, but it means the map is the only thing that's different. The versions worth seeking out are the ones that changed something structural: turn limits, new win conditions, unit types that actually matter, or (in Legacy's case) a board that permanently changes shape as you play it.

That's the filter we used here. Every entry on this list earns its spot by doing something the classic board doesn't.

## 1. Risk Legacy

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-legacy.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/Hasbro-Gaming-Immersive-Narrative-Miniature/dp/B08TPGT1QL)

Risk Legacy is the reason "legacy game" is a category now. You play a campaign of up to 15 games with the same group, and every session leaves a permanent mark on the board — you put stickers on territories you've won big in, you name continents, you tear up cards you're never allowed to use again. Somewhere around game five, the board stops looking anything like the Risk you grew up with.

There are sealed envelopes and boxes you're told not to open until specific conditions are met. We won't spoil what's in them, but the anticipation of "we're not allowed to open that yet" does more for table energy than most board games manage in an entire session.

**This is not a game you play once and shelve.** The entire design assumes a group willing to commit to a campaign, which is a real barrier — if your group can't reliably get the same five people back to the table, the payoff of a scarred, permanently-changed board never arrives. But for a group that commits, nothing else on this list creates the kind of shared history Legacy does.

After the campaign ends, the board is still fully playable — just permanently altered, missions and rule changes intact. That's a genuinely clever piece of design: the thing you'd expect to become useless junk after 15 plays is actually the most interesting Risk board you'll ever own.

## 2. Risk 2210 A.D.

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-2210-ad.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/Renegade-Game-Studios-D-Underwater/dp/B0D2J2RTQG)

Risk 2210 A.D. moves the map 200 years into the future and adds two entirely new battlegrounds: the moon and the ocean floor. You're not just fighting for continents anymore — moon territories generate energy, ocean territories are safer to build up quietly, and Earth is still where most of the fighting happens.

**The hard 5-turn limit fixes the single biggest problem with classic Risk.** No game runs long because one player is too stubborn to lose gracefully. You know exactly how many turns you have, which means every move in turn one is already being weighed against a turn-five endgame.

Commander units and command cards add a layer classic Risk never had — special one-time abilities that let you nuke a territory, teleport troops, or grant a temporary combat bonus. These show up rarely enough that using one at the right moment feels like a real decision, not a gimmick.

The miniatures are the other reason people bring this one up unprompted. Moon bases, underwater domes, and Earth troops are all sculpted differently, and the box comes with well over 400 pieces. It's not a cheap game, but the components alone make it feel like an upgrade over the original rather than a variant of it.

## 3. Risk: Europe

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-europe.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/Winning-Moves-Games-Risk-Europe/dp/B081LLJ8YY)

Risk: Europe throws out area-control-by-numbers and replaces it with unit ranks. Archers fire before your infantry gets to swing. Knights hit harder than peasants. Casualties get removed rank by rank, which means combat actually rewards planning your army composition instead of just stacking the most dice.

Each of the seven kingdoms plays differently — some start with stronger economies, some start with better troops, some are positioned to grab easy early territory. That asymmetry alone puts this ahead of most Risk variants, which usually give every player an identical starting hand and let the dice sort it out.

**Siege weapons change how you think about defense.** Fortified territories aren't just harder to take — they require the right tools to crack, which slows down runaway leaders in a way classic Risk's dice-vs-armies math never does. You can't just throw more troops at a well-defended kingdom and expect the math to work in your favor eventually.

It's a heavier, longer game than most people expect from something with "Risk" in the title — closer to a historical war game than a family night filler. Worth it if your group already likes strategy games with teeth, less worth it if you're trying to ease someone into the genre.

## 4. Risk Classic

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-classic.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/ACD-Hasbro-Games-Risk-Refresh/dp/B07PXD6QVN)

The one everyone already knows. Classic Risk earns a spot here not because it's the best version — it clearly isn't, or the rest of this list wouldn't exist — but because it's still the reference point, and because the 2016 refresh quietly fixed a few of the complaints people have carried for decades.

The refreshed edition includes four ways to play: the original Secret Mission variant, straight Classic conquest, a Capital mode where losing your capital ends your game instantly, and a proper 2-player mode with a neutral "wild" army. That last one matters more than it sounds — two-player Risk used to require a house rule just to be playable, and now it doesn't.

**Mission cards fix the biggest complaint about the base game.** Instead of "wipe out the entire board," each player gets a secret objective — control two specific continents, eliminate one specific color, hold 18 territories. Games end faster and there's a bluffing layer to figuring out what your opponents are actually working toward.

Even fixed up, Risk Classic still runs long with six players and can still turn into a slow bleed for whoever gets eliminated first. If you own only one Risk, this is probably still it — but know going in that half the versions below solve at least one problem this one hasn't.

## 5. Risk: Godstorm

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-godstorm.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/Renegade-Game-Studios-Godstorm-Cultures/dp/B0D7MS1DZS)

Godstorm sets the fight in a mythologized ancient Mediterranean — Greeks, Celts, Egyptians, Babylonians, and Norse factions, each with their own god cards and epoch-based bonuses. Every round advances the world through a new epoch, and each epoch unlocks a fresh set of divine powers you can call in.

**The five-epoch structure gives Godstorm the same turn-limit fix 2210 A.D. has, dressed in different clothes.** You always know how many rounds are left, which means the mid-game desperation moves classic Risk lacks are baked into how the game paces itself.

Underworld battles are the standout mechanic — losing units don't just vanish, they can end up contested in a separate underworld track that adds a second layer of conflict most players don't see coming their first game. It's a genuinely clever twist that most licensed Risk variants never attempt.

The original 2004 Avalon Hill printing has been out of print for years and mostly shows up secondhand, but Renegade Games Studios brought it back with over 380 sculpted miniatures. Worth tracking down if ancient-mythology theming is more your speed than sci-fi or licensed IP.

## 6. Risk: Star Wars Edition

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-star-wars-edition.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/Hasbro-Risk-Star-Wars-2014/dp/B07H35FZDW)

This edition drops the classic map entirely in favor of a board shaped like a TIE fighter, recreating the space battle above the second Death Star from Return of the Jedi. You're the Rebels or the Empire, fighting across three separate zones — the Death Star's surface, the space battle, and the shield generator on Endor — that all resolve simultaneously.

**Simultaneous play across three fronts is the real innovation here**, and it's one classic Risk never touches. You're not waiting through four other players' turns before you get to act again. Everyone's making moves in the same round, which keeps the pace fast and the table engaged in a way six-player classic Risk struggles with.

It only supports 2 or 4 players, split into two teams if you've got four, which is a real limitation if your group runs bigger than that. But for exactly the players it's designed for, a full game wraps in well under two hours.

The theming is executed well — the board, the pieces, the phase structure all genuinely evoke the film's climax rather than just slapping Star Wars stickers on the classic map. If your group has Star Wars fans who've never gotten into war games, this is a legitimately good entry point.

## 7. Risk: The Lord of the Rings Trilogy Edition

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-lord-of-the-rings-trilogy-edition.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/Hasbro-Gaming-Risk-Trilogy-Strategy/dp/B08MVX27WW)

This one maps the entire trilogy onto Middle-earth instead of picking a single film like most licensed Risk editions do. Gondor, Mordor, Rohan, and the rest of the familiar geography replace the standard continents, and Adventure cards pull directly from moments across all three movies rather than just one.

**The One Ring mechanic is what separates this from a straight reskin.** Whoever controls it gets meaningful bonuses, but holding it also makes you the target everyone else is coordinating against — which mirrors the source material's central tension in a way that actually affects strategy, not just flavor text.

Secret missions return here too, layered on top of the standard conquest win condition, so you've got the option to play a shorter, objective-based game instead of grinding to full map control. That flexibility matters more than it sounds like on paper — full-map Risk with the LOTR-sized board can run long.

**Tolkien fans get more mileage here than out of the Star Wars edition**, simply because the trilogy scope gives the design more material to draw from. If your group already owns a Risk and is choosing a themed second copy, this is one of the stronger picks specifically because of how much of the source material actually made it into the mechanics.

## 8. Risk: Game of Thrones (Skirmish Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-game-of-thrones-skirmish-edition.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/Winning-Moves-Games-Thrones-Board/dp/B011QFAFNY)

Risk: Game of Thrones drops the classic single continuous map in favor of a Westeros board built around the show's major houses, with a separate skirmish-style combat resolution that plays faster than standard Risk's dice-off. Four houses, four starting positions, and a board that's noticeably smaller in scope than classic Risk's full-world map.

**The smaller, more contained map is the biggest structural change, and it's a good one.** Fewer territories means fewer turns spent shuffling small groups of troops around doing nothing, and it keeps games closer to the 60–90 minute range instead of classic Risk's multi-hour sprawl.

The show tie-in is executed with real production value — sculpted house sigils, a board that actually looks like a fantasy war map instead of a licensed reskin of the original. For a group of Thrones fans who also like a bit of strategy, this lands better than most "put a logo on Risk" licensed editions.

It's a niche pick if nobody at your table watched the show — the faction names and territory references won't mean much out of context. But as a themed conquest game built specifically around its source material rather than just borrowing its name, it's one of the better-executed licensed versions on this list.

## 9. Risk Junior

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/risk-junior.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/Hasbro-Gaming-E6936-Risk-Junior/dp/B07VYTX21W)

Risk Junior swaps continents and armies for a pirate-themed treasure hunt aimed at kids five and up. There's no elimination, no multi-hour grind, and no reading required — just simple movement, dice, and a map of a single island split into a manageable number of regions.

**A full game wraps in about 30 minutes**, which is close to the actual attention span of the age group it's built for. That alone makes it a smarter first Risk for a family than handing a six-year-old the classic board and hoping for the best.

It's not a game for anyone who already plays strategy games — there's no depth here beyond "roll, move, take the region." That's the entire point. This exists to teach the shape of Risk's conquest fantasy without any of the friction that makes the adult version a hard sell for young kids.

If your household has a mixed-age game night and someone's been begging to play "the Risk with the pirates," this is the actual purchase. Don't buy the classic board expecting a kid to sit through it.

## 10. Risk Express

Designed by Reiner Knizia and released as part of Hasbro's short-lived Express line, Risk Express takes the conquest fantasy and compresses it into a card-and-dice game that plays in about 30 minutes. There's no board — you're rolling dice against territory cards laid out on the table, trying to claim enough of them before your opponents do.

**It's the fastest way to get the feeling of Risk without committing an evening to it.** No setup beyond shuffling a deck, no elimination, no hour three where someone's clearly lost but still has to sit there. If you like the dice-versus-territory tension of Risk but not the time investment, this is exactly that tension distilled.

Knizia's fingerprints are obvious if you've played his other dice games — there's a push-your-luck rhythm to deciding whether to commit more dice to a contested card or bank what you've already got.

**This one's out of print and only shows up secondhand now**, which is the main strike against it. Worth grabbing if you find a used copy for a reasonable price; not worth chasing eBay listings at collector markups for a 30-minute filler.

## Conclusion

If your group has never finished a full campaign game together and you're willing to commit: **Risk Legacy**. Nothing else on this list creates the kind of shared history a scarred, permanently-changed board does.

For a group that wants the strategic depth of Risk without the multi-hour runtime and elimination problems: **Risk 2210 A.D.** or **Risk: Godstorm** — both fix the pacing with a hard turn limit, just with different theming.

For a themed pickup that's actually built around its source material instead of a reskin: **Risk: The Lord of the Rings Trilogy Edition**. For families with younger kids who want a taste of the conquest fantasy without the multi-hour commitment: **Risk Junior**.

Classic Risk isn't going anywhere, and there's a reason it's the one everyone's played. Just know that almost every version on this list fixes something about it — so if the original left a bad taste, the fix might already exist on this list.
