War games have a reputation problem. Say "World War 2 board game" to someone who's only ever played Catan and they picture a card table buried under hex maps, a rulebook the size of a phone book, and four hours before anyone actually attacks anything. Some of that reputation is earned. Some of it isn't.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](#best-ww2-board-games-comparison-table)

The truth is the genre runs the full range. On one end you've got games you can teach in ten minutes and finish before the pizza gets cold. On the other end you've got simulations detailed enough that historians use them to argue about supply lines. We tried to cover that whole spectrum here, because "best WW2 board game" means something different to the person who wants a fun Friday night than it does to the person who owns three different games about Stalingrad.

We're using "WW2 board game" in the traditional wargame sense: games where you're actually maneuvering units, managing combat, and fighting over territory, not just games with a WW2 skin slapped on a Euro mechanic. If you're coming from lighter strategy games, start at the top of this list. If you already know what "zone of control" means, skip to the bottom.

## What Makes a Good WW2 Board Game?

We looked for games that get the tension of the era right without needing a weekend to finish. That meant weighing how fast you can learn it, how long a session actually runs, and whether the historical detail adds to the experience or just adds rules overhead. A game that nails 1944 Normandy but takes three sessions to resolve isn't going to see the table very often, so pacing mattered as much as accuracy.

We also made a point of covering both theaters. Most "best WW2 games" lists lean hard into the European front because that's where the most famous titles live. The Pacific war gets its own entry here too, since it plays completely differently: naval movement and carrier strikes instead of tank columns and river crossings.

## 1. Memoir '44

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/memoir-44.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0002TV2NS/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If you're new to war games entirely, start here. Memoir '44 puts you in command of an army on a specific D-Day battlefield, using a simple hex grid and command cards that tell you which sections of the battlefield you're allowed to order around that turn.

That command card restriction is the whole game. You can see the perfect move sitting right there on the board, and you might not have a card that lets you make it. It forces you to plan two moves ahead instead of one, and it keeps games from turning into a slow march where the better player just wins on math.

Combat is dice-based and fast: roll a handful, count the hits, remove figures. There's no combat table to cross-reference and no modifier to calculate in your head. You can teach someone the entire game in the time it takes to set up the terrain tiles.

The scenario books are the game's real depth. Each historical battle has its own map, its own starting positions, and its own victory conditions, so you're not replaying the same fight with different colored plastic every time. We've had newer players ask genuine questions about the actual historical battle after playing a scenario. That's not something a lot of games manage.

**We'd start every new war-gamer here.** It's the entry point that made an entire generation of players realize this genre could be fast and fun instead of homework.

## 2. Axis & Allies: 1942 Second Edition

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/axis-and-allies-1942.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BZ9NJKQB/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the one most people picture when they hear "WW2 board game," and there's a reason it's stayed in print for decades. Each player commands a major power (the US, UK, Germany, Japan, or the Soviet Union) and fights the entire war at once, across a world map divided into territories you attack, defend, and try to hold.

The economics are the real game underneath the combat. Every territory you control generates income, which buys the units you'll need for next turn's fighting. Lose ground and your production shrinks with it, so a bad early turn can spiral fast if you're not careful. Watching an ally's economy collapse because they overextended is its own kind of tension, even when it's not your turn.

Combat runs on dice and attack/defense values printed right on the unit pieces, so there's no lookup table to fumble through mid-game. The learning curve is less about the rules and more about strategy. Knowing when to reinforce a front versus when to cut losses takes a few plays to click.

This is a long game. A full five-player session can run most of an evening, and that's the honest tradeoff for commanding an entire world war. **Play it with people who've committed to the whole session, not people eyeing the door at hour two.**

## 3. Undaunted: Normandy

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/undaunted-normandy.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1472834704/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Undaunted: Normandy takes a mechanic you'd normally associate with fantasy deck builders and points it at squad-level WW2 combat, and somehow the combination works better than it has any right to. Each player runs a deck representing their squad. Playing a card activates that unit: move it, have it suppress an enemy, fire on a target. Lose the unit, and its card gets pulled from your deck permanently.

That last part changes how you play. Losing a card isn't just losing a piece on the board. It's losing an option for the rest of the game. Protecting your stronger units stops being an abstract priority and becomes something you're actively managing hand to hand.

The fog-of-war element deserves a mention: you don't always know exactly where an enemy unit is until you're close enough to spot it, which keeps early turns tense in a way a fully visible board doesn't. Combat resolves with a simple dice pool against a target number, so a full scenario clears in under an hour.

Setup and teardown are both quick, which matters more than people expect for a war game. A lot of the genre asks you to commit an entire afternoon before you even see if you like it. This one doesn't. **It's the easiest recommendation on this list for someone who wants tactical WW2 combat without a four-hour buy-in.**

## 4. Quartermaster General

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/quartermaster-general.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07RZ9PRL6/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Quartermaster General strips the WW2 grand strategy game down to cards and a shared map, and somehow keeps the "commanding an entire war" feeling intact. Each player takes an Allied or Axis power, and every turn comes down to a hand of cards: some build up your military strength, some trigger attacks, some are historical events that bend the rules for a turn.

What separates this from the classic Axis & Allies-style games is speed. There's no unit-by-unit combat resolution eating up the clock. Most of the war plays out through the cards you choose to commit and when. A full game clears in under two hours with five or six players around the table, which is not a sentence you can say about most global-scale WW2 games.

The team dynamic adds a layer that solo-power games don't have. You're not just fighting the other side, you're coordinating with your own alliance about who attacks where and who reinforces whom, and that negotiation is honestly where a lot of the fun lives. We've seen an Axis table lose a war because two players couldn't agree on priorities, just as often as we've seen them lose to good Allied play.

If you like the scale of Axis & Allies but don't have three hours to spend proving it, **this is the version that respects your evening.**

## 5. Axis & Allies: D-Day

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/axis-allies-d-day.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000KICNBQ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

D-Day takes the Axis & Allies system and narrows it down to a single, focused fight: the Normandy invasion. Where the global versions ask you to think about five fronts at once, this one hands you a beach, a handful of objectives, and a much shorter list of things to worry about.

That focus makes it a genuinely good second game for someone who liked Memoir '44 but wants more strategic weight without jumping straight to a full world-war title. You're managing landing craft timing, beachhead reinforcement, and the push inland, all compressed into a game that wraps up in an evening instead of eating your whole weekend.

The historical framing is tighter too. Instead of an abstract global economy, you're recreating one of the most consequential days of the war, and the map reflects it: specific beaches, specific objectives, specific defensive positions the Germans actually held. Players who care about the history tend to get more out of this one than the broader Axis & Allies entries simply because the story is more concrete.

**A strong pick if you want the Axis & Allies feel without committing to a full evening every single time you play.**

## 6. Combat Commander: Europe

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/combat-commander-europe.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0G2J8BFW7/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is where the list starts getting serious. Combat Commander: Europe is a card-driven tactical war game. You're commanding a small unit in a specific historical engagement, and instead of a fixed sequence of moves, you're reacting to a hand of cards that dictate what's actually possible on a given turn.

The card system is the whole appeal for players who've outgrown dice-and-hex simplicity. Cards trigger ambushes, force morale checks, and let you interrupt an opponent's move. The fog of war isn't simulated with hidden information so much as it's simulated with genuine uncertainty about what tools you'll have available when it matters. No two games play out the same way even on an identical scenario.

Be honest with yourself about the learning curve here. This isn't a weekend-one game. The rulebook assumes some familiarity with tactical war game conventions, and a first play will probably involve a lot of rule-checking. It rewards that investment, but it does ask for it upfront.

**This is the game you graduate to once Memoir '44 and Undaunted start feeling too simple.** Don't start here. Start here eventually.

## 7. Conflict of Heroes: Awakening the Bear

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/conflict-of-heroes-awakening-the-bear.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07HBCPKFK/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Conflict of Heroes puts you on the Eastern Front, which is genuinely underrepresented in this genre given how much of the actual war was decided there. It's a squad-level tactical game. You're moving individual units across a hex map, managing line of sight, cover, and suppression, in engagements small enough that every single unit's fate matters.

The action-point system is more restrictive than most tactical games in the genre, and that's deliberate. Each unit gets one action per turn, which sounds limiting until you realize how much tension it creates. You can see exactly what you'd want to do with a unit and simply not have the actions left to do it. It's a similar restriction philosophy to Memoir '44's command cards, just applied at a much more granular, unforgiving level.

Terrain matters here in a way a lot of lighter games gloss over. Cover, elevation, and line of sight genuinely change what's possible on a given turn, and reading the map correctly is as much a skill as managing your units.

This one asks more of you than Memoir '44 or Undaunted, but it pays it back with a level of tactical texture those games don't attempt. **Recommended once you want the Eastern Front specifically, not just "WW2" in the abstract.**

## 8. Axis & Allies: Europe 1940 (Second Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/axis-allies-europe-1940.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BZ9CFX7H/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If the base Axis & Allies game felt too compressed, this is the answer. Europe 1940 zooms into the European theater specifically and adds enough extra detail (more unit types, research options, a more granular map) that a full game can run five or six hours with a full table.

That extra granularity is the entire draw for players who've already worn out the base game. Naval interdiction, strategic bombing, and the ability to actually starve an opponent's production by cutting supply lines all show up here in ways the faster versions don't have room for. It plays less like "WW2 the board game" and more like a genuine simulation of the European conflict's economic and military pressures.

We won't pretend this is a casual pick. It demands a full table of committed players and a full day, realistically, if you want to see it through to a real conclusion rather than calling it early. **This is the game for the group that already loves Axis & Allies and wants the deepest version of it, not an introduction for anyone new to the genre.**

## 9. Empire of the Sun

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/empire-of-the-sun.webp)](https://www.gmtgames.com/p-1114-empire-of-the-sun-5th-printing.aspx)

Every game on this list so far covers the European theater. Empire of the Sun is the one to reach for if you want the Pacific war instead. It's widely considered the definitive strategic-level treatment of that conflict, running from Pearl Harbor through the final push on the Japanese home islands.

The Pacific war plays completely differently from anything happening in Europe, and this game captures why. It's naval and air-driven rather than a grinding land campaign: carrier strikes, amphibious landings, and the logistics of projecting force across an ocean instead of across a border. Card-driven turns let historical events reshape your options the way they did for the actual commanders, which gives the whole thing a different rhythm than the tank-and-infantry games dominating the rest of this list.

This is a two-player, sit-down-and-commit kind of game. It's not a filler, and it's not forgiving of a distracted opponent. A full campaign game genuinely runs the better part of a day. But if you've played through the European side of WW2 gaming and want to see the other half of the war, **this is the one serious players point to first.**

## Conclusion

If you're picking just one game off this list, start with Memoir '44. It's the fastest way to find out whether WW2 gaming is for you before you invest in anything heavier. If you already know you like the genre and want the classic feeling of commanding a whole side of the war, Axis & Allies: 1942 Second Edition is still the standard everyone else gets compared to. And if your table wants tactical grit over big-picture strategy, Undaunted: Normandy and Combat Commander: Europe cover that end of the spectrum without needing a full weekend.

We kept this list to nine because we'd rather send you to games we can actually stand behind than pad it out to a round number.
