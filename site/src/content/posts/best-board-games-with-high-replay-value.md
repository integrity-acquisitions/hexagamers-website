There's a specific kind of buyer's remorse that only board gamers get. You spend $70, you play the thing four times over two enthusiastic weeks, and then it sits on the shelf for a year because everyone at the table already knows how it goes. The game wasn't bad. It just ran out of road.

I've bought that game more than once. So has everyone in our group. The question that actually matters when you're deciding what to spend money on isn't "is this good?" Plenty of games are good on play one. It's whether the thing still has something to show you on play twenty.

This list is the games that do. Ten of them, picked for longevity rather than for how they review out of the box.

## What Makes a Game Actually Replayable

"Great replay value" gets stamped on the back of almost every board game box ever printed, which makes it useless as a signal. Here's what we actually looked for.

**A setup that changes in ways that matter.** Random tiles alone don't do it. Catan shuffles its board every game and it's still recognisably the same game each time, because the strategic questions barely move. What counts is when a different setup asks you a different question. Dominion's ten-card kingdom is the clearest example. A board with Chapel on it and a board without are two genuinely different games.

**Hidden information, or another player to react to.** This is the one most "replayable" games miss. Bitewing Games ran a piece on exactly this and landed on the same conclusion: the games that go stale tend to be the low-interaction ones. If you're solving a puzzle alone at your seat, you'll eventually solve it. If you're solving it against four people who are also adapting, the target keeps moving.

**No dominant line.** Some games have a best opening, and once your group finds it, the game is over as a contest. The ones that last have several viable paths whose value flips depending on what's available and who else is going for it.

**Depth that outlives about twenty plays.** This is the honest threshold. A lot of very good games are genuinely fresh for ten or twelve plays and then start repeating. That's not a failure. It's just a different purchase. But it's not what this list is for.

One thing worth saying up front: we've deliberately left out legacy and campaign games. More on that below.

## 1. Terraforming Mars

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/terraforming-mars.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01GSYA4K2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 1-5 · **Time:** 120 minutes

The box holds 208 project cards and 17 corporations, and you'll see maybe fifteen of those cards in a given game. That ratio is the whole pitch. Two players can sit down to the same board and end up playing two completely unrelated economic puzzles because one drew into heat production and the other drew into microbes.

**The corporation you start with reframes everything.** Not in a small way. Some corporations want you spending aggressively from turn one; others want you sitting on money while everyone else races. You don't get to pick a strategy and then execute it. You get dealt a hand and a company, and you work out what's possible from there.

The caveat, and it's a real one: this game is long and it looks like a spreadsheet. The production board is fiddly, the cubes slide when someone bumps the table, and a five-player game will eat two and a half hours. If your group's tolerance tops out at ninety minutes, this is the wrong pick no matter how well it holds up.

But it holds up. It's the game in our circle most likely to get a "we should play that again next week" the moment it ends.

## 2. Dominion ([Full Review Here](/dominion-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Dominion_z98xnd.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01LYLIS2U/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 2-4 · **Time:** 30 minutes

Dominion is the cleanest answer to this entire question, and the maths backs it up. The base box has 26 kingdom cards and you use ten per game, which works out to 5,311,735 distinct setups. Add one large expansion and that figure runs past fifteen billion.

Numbers like that are easy to wave around, but here the variety is real because the ten cards don't decorate the game. They *are* the game. A kingdom with trashing in it plays nothing like one without. Put Chapel on the table and everyone's first thought is deck-thinning; leave it off and the same players build something totally different.

**It's also the fastest game on this list to set up and finish.** Thirty minutes, no board, and you can play three back-to-back with three different kingdoms in the time one game of Terraforming Mars takes.

Where it shows its age: the base set's card art is plain, and a couple of the original ten are weak enough that experienced players stop picking them. The Second Edition swapped out the worst offenders, which is the version worth buying.

## 3. Spirit Island

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/spirit-island.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MUHP51S/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 1-4 · **Time:** 90-120 minutes

Most co-ops solve out. Someone works out the efficient line, the group settles into it, and the game becomes a procedure. Spirit Island dodges this by stacking three independent dials on top of each other: eight spirits with genuinely different power sets, seven adversaries once you count expansions, and multiple difficulty levels inside each adversary.

**Every spirit is close to a separate game.** Lightning's Swift Strike wants speed and early aggression. River Surges in Sunlight wants patience and board control. Playing one after mastering the other means relearning your instincts, not just adjusting a few decisions.

Then the adversary layer changes what the *island* does to you, so a spirit you know well behaves differently against Brandenburg than against England. The combinations multiply rather than add.

Honest warning: this is a heavy game and the first play is rough. The iconography is dense, the powers take a while to parse, and there's a decent chance your table loses badly and doesn't understand why. It's worth pushing through, but don't hand this to people who wanted a relaxing evening.

## 4. Root ([Full Review Here](/root-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/root.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F454YF3/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 2-4 · **Time:** 60-90 minutes

Root takes asymmetry further than almost anything else on a mainstream shelf. The factions don't share a rules set. The Marquise builds and defends, the Eyrie is locked into an escalating decree that can collapse on itself, the Woodland Alliance farms sympathy off other players' aggression, and the Vagabond wanders around making friends and enemies on a completely separate scoring track.

**Learning a new faction is closer to learning a new game than adjusting a strategy.** That's the replayability engine. Four factions in the base box means four different games before you've bought anything, and at three players you're leaving one out each time, which changes the board politics on its own.

The art also does a lot of quiet work here. It looks like a woodland picture book, which gets it to the table with people who'd never sit down to a war game. Which is, structurally, exactly what it is.

The friction worth naming: Root is unforgiving about table balance. If one player doesn't understand that the leader has to be attacked, the leader wins uncontested. It needs a group willing to police the board, and it's noticeably worse with two players than with four.

## 5. Wingspan ([Full Review Here](/wingspan-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/wingspan.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07YQ641NQ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 1-5 · **Time:** 40-70 minutes

170 unique bird cards, and each one carries its own ability. You'll draw a small fraction per game, which means the engine you end up building is dictated by what showed up rather than by a plan you brought with you.

**The round-end goals are the underrated part.** They rotate, and they quietly determine whether this particular game rewards going wide across habitats or stacking one. Same birds, different goal tiles, different game.

Wingspan also earns a spot here for a less obvious reason. It's the game on this list most likely to actually get played. It's beautiful, it teaches in ten minutes, and non-gamers say yes to it. A game with theoretically infinite depth that nobody wants to play has a replay value of zero.

The knock: player interaction is light. You're mostly building your own tableau, and Bitewing's argument about low-interaction games going stale does apply here to a degree. It has more longevity than most engine builders, not more than Root.

## 6. Race for the Galaxy

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/race-for-the-galaxy.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000YLAOEW/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 2-4 · **Time:** 30-60 minutes

Race for the Galaxy gets brought up constantly in replay-value discussions, usually by people with several hundred plays logged. It's a tableau builder where everyone secretly picks a phase each round, only the chosen phases happen, and you get a bonus for the one you called.

**That selection mechanism is what keeps it alive.** You're not only optimising your own tableau. You're guessing what everyone else will call, because their choices hand you free actions. Play against a table that always picks Produce and you play one game. Play against people who read you and it becomes something else entirely.

Games run 30 to 60 minutes, which is remarkable for the amount of decision-making packed in, and it's the cheapest heavy game here by a distance.

Now the part everyone warns about, correctly: the iconography. The cards use a symbolic language instead of text, and the first two or three games are genuinely unpleasant while you're flipping to the reference sheet every turn. It clicks around game four and then never bothers you again. Plenty of people bounce off before that point, and that's a fair reason to skip it.

## 7. 7 Wonders ([Full Review Here](/7-wonders-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/7-Wonders.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0043KJW5M/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 2-7 · **Time:** 30 minutes

Thirty minutes, up to seven players, and the length barely moves as you add people because everyone drafts simultaneously. On the shelf-life question that matters more than it sounds: a game that scales to seven and still finishes in half an hour gets played in situations where nothing else on this list fits.

**The variety here comes from the table, not the box.** Each wonder board gives you a different set of build bonuses, but the real churn is that you're passing cards to your neighbour. What you deny them matters as much as what you take. Same cards, different seating, different game.

It rewards knowing the deck, too. Early on you take what looks strong; later you learn to count what's already gone and cut someone off from a science set they've been building for two ages.

Worth flagging: 7 Wonders needs bodies. At three players it's noticeably flatter, and the two-player variant is a bolted-on compromise. If you're usually playing as a pair, [7 Wonders Duel](/best-two-player-board-games/) is the version designed for that, and it's a better game at that count.

## 8. Cascadia

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/cascadia.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B093H8RGXX/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 1-4 · **Time:** 30-45 minutes

Cascadia won the 2022 Spiel des Jahres, and the jury specifically called out the modular scoring cards as the reason it keeps giving. Each of the five animals has multiple scoring cards, and you swap one in per animal per game. Bears might score for pairs this session and for groups of three next time.

**That single swap reshapes the whole puzzle.** You're placing habitat tiles and wildlife tokens either way, but which placements are worth anything changes completely. It's a small design lever doing a lot of work.

It's also the gentlest game on this list by some margin. Rules take five minutes, games take forty, and nobody attacks anybody. That makes it the one I'd hand to a family who liked Ticket to Ride and wanted the next thing.

Fair criticism: interaction is nearly nonexistent. You're competing for tiles from a shared pool and that's it. It's a solitaire puzzle played next to other people. For some tables that's the appeal. If you want games where players push each other around, look at Root instead.

## 9. Innovation

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/innovation.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MT5MYK4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 2-4 · **Time:** 30-75 minutes

105 cards across ten ages, and essentially every card breaks a rule. Carl Chudyk designs games where the cards *are* the rules engine, and Innovation is the purest version of that idea. One card lets you steal a pile; another rewrites how scoring works; another ends the game early if the conditions line up.

**You cannot plan an Innovation game in advance.** The board state gets rebuilt underneath you constantly, and what you're really doing is spotting a combination two cards before anyone else does. Games swing hard and fast.

That's also the complaint. It's chaotic, it can feel unfair, and a well-timed card can undo twenty minutes of careful play. If your group wants a game where the better player reliably wins, this will annoy them. If your group enjoys a game that keeps producing situations nobody's seen before, few things compete.

It's a small box of cards at a low price, which makes it an easy risk to take. This is the pick on the list I'd most expect to divide a table.

## 10. Codenames ([Full Review Here](/codenames-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Codenames.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FJMQB63N/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Players:** 2-8+ · **Time:** 15 minutes

400 word cards and a 5×5 grid produces more layouts than anyone will ever exhaust, but that undersells why Codenames belongs here. The variety isn't in the cards. It's in the people.

**The same grid played with different people is a different game.** A clue that connects three words instantly for your partner is opaque to someone else. Your group builds a shared vocabulary over time (inside jokes, associations that only work at your table) and the game gets *better* the more you play it with the same crowd, which is close to the opposite of how replayability usually works.

Fifteen minutes, plays with almost any number, teaches in two minutes. It's the most-played game in a lot of collections for a reason.

The limitation is real though: it needs at least four to work properly, and the two-player Duet version is a different game rather than a substitute. It also lands badly with people who freeze under social pressure. Being spymaster in front of six expectant faces isn't relaxing for everyone.

## A Few We Left Off

**Legacy and campaign games, all of them.** Gloomhaven has 95 scenarios and Pandemic Legacy runs 24 sessions, and both are superb. But they're finite by design. You play the content and then it's done, and what you're left with is a box with stickers on it. That's an enormous amount of gaming for the money, just not the same product as a game that regenerates. If that's what you're after, our [best cooperative board games](/best-cooperative-board-games/) list covers that ground properly.

**Catan.** It shuffles its board every game, which is the thing people point at, but the strategic questions barely change between setups. It's a great gateway game and we've [written plenty about it](/catan-review/). It isn't a twenty-plays game.

**Calico and Pendulum.** Both get recommended for replayability and both have been criticised for the opposite. Bitewing's write-up flagged that once you find the efficient line in Calico, play three feels like play two. Low interaction is usually the culprit.

**7 Wonders Duel** narrowly missed. It's excellent, but Keith Law's criticism at Paste, that it varies less game to game than its reputation suggests, is a fair hit, and the base 7 Wonders edged it out.

## Conclusion

The honest summary: Dominion is the one I'd point most people at. It's cheap, it's half an hour, and the maths on its variety is absurd in a way you can actually feel at the table rather than just read on the box.

But the real lesson from putting this list together is that replayability comes from other players more often than from components. The games here that last longest (Root, 7 Wonders, Codenames) are the ones where the thing you're adapting to is a person, not a shuffle. Randomness gives you a different setup. People give you a different game.
