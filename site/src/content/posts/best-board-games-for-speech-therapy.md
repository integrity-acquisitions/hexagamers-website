A few months back, one of our friends who works as an SLP in an elementary school mentioned she was raiding the clearance shelf at Target for the same games we buy for game night. Not because they're marketed as therapy tools. Most of them aren't. It's because a good game gives a kid a reason to say a word ten times in a row without it feeling like a drill.

That's really the whole trick. A worksheet asks a kid to repeat "rabbit" five times and everyone in the room, including the kid, knows what's happening. A round of Candy Land asks the same thing, except now there's a fairy, a castle, and a chance to beat their sibling to it. The repetition is the same. The buy-in isn't.

A quick caveat before we get into it: we're board game reviewers, not speech-language pathologists. Nobody on our team has clinical training, and this article shouldn't be read as therapy advice. What we did instead is dig through what actual SLPs write about which games work and why, then cross-referenced that against games we already know well from our own shelf. The list below is our honest read of that research, not a clinical recommendation.

The list leans toward games built for young kids and early elementary, since that's where most speech therapy happens, but a few of these work just as well with teens and adults working on pragmatic language or word retrieval. If you're shopping for that same young age range more broadly, our [best board games for kindergarten](/best-board-games-for-kindergarten/) list is a good companion to this one.

## What We Looked For

**Quick turns.** A therapy session is 20 to 30 minutes. A game with a five-minute turn is a game you never finish, so everything here moves fast.

**A built-in reason to talk.** Roll-and-move games work because there's dead time between turns, which is perfect for slotting in a sound drill. Guessing games work because the entire mechanic is asking and answering questions. We stayed away from anything where a kid can win by pointing or grabbing.

**Room to adapt.** None of these games were designed by an SLP (well, almost none, more on that below). The value is that a therapist or a parent can bolt a goal onto them without breaking how they're played. Attach a target word to a game piece. Make a player describe their card before they play it. The games flex.

**Actually fun.** A kid who feels like they're doing homework checks out. Every game on this list is one we'd hand a five-year-old for a birthday, therapy goals aside.

## 1. Candy Land

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/candy-land.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00000DMF5/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The oldest trick in the book, and it's still the best one. There's no reading, no real decision-making, and no way to lose so badly a kid melts down over it. You draw a card, you move, that's the whole game.

That simplicity is exactly why it's a speech therapy staple. Every single card flip is a turn, and every turn is a slot for a target sound. Working on final /k/? Every color card becomes a chance to say "pick," "stick," or whatever word list you're running that week. Some therapists tape target words directly onto the board squares so the game generates its own drill list as the pieces move.

It also does more than articulation. The color-matching mechanic is a built-in vocabulary check for kids still nailing down color names, and the two "special" squares (the licorice space, the gooey gumdrop) give you a natural spot to ask a WH-question: "what happened?" "why do you have to go back?"

We wouldn't put this in front of an eight-year-old for a fun family game night, but for a four- or five-year-old in an early session, it's close to perfect. Cheap, replaces itself if a piece goes missing, and every parent already owns one.

## 2. Guess Who?

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/guess-who.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BZ8PRZ6T/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This one earns its spot for a different reason than Candy Land. It's not about repetitions, it's about the shape of the language itself. To play Guess Who correctly, you have to form a real yes/no question, out loud, over and over: "Does your person have glasses?" "Is your person wearing a hat?"

For a kid who's working on question formulation, that's the entire session in a box. You can't shortcut it by pointing. You have to build the sentence, and if the sentence is malformed the game literally doesn't work: your partner won't know what you're asking.

It also quietly drills deductive reasoning and descriptive vocabulary (hair color, accessories, facial features), which makes it a favorite for slightly older kids, six and up, who've outgrown Candy Land but aren't ready for anything with real strategy. Two players only, which is a limitation in a group setting, but ideal for one-on-one sessions.

## 3. Jenga

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/jenga.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07CW84Q8B/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Jenga shouldn't work as a therapy tool on paper. It's a physics game, not a language game. But it's one of the most-cited tools we found, and once you see why, it's obvious: the tower forces a pause before every single move. That pause is free real estate.

The classic use is writing prompts directly on the blocks in dry-erase marker: a "get to know you" question, a target word, a category to name three items from. Pull the block, do the task, place it on top. It works with articulation drills just as well as it works with pragmatic language goals like taking turns gracefully and handling the tower falling without a meltdown (which, itself, is a real skill some kids need practice with).

It scales in a way most of these games don't. A four-year-old can play a stripped-down version with a helper. A group of teenagers can run a full pragmatic-language session off the same set of wooden blocks. Very few games in this list flex that far.

## 4. Hedbanz

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/hedbanz.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MYV9MM9/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Hedbanz is Guess Who with the volume turned up. Everyone's playing at once, there's a timer, and the card is strapped to your own forehead so you're the only one who doesn't know what you are. Kids find that setup genuinely funny, which matters more than it sounds like it should. A game that's actually funny gets replayed without anyone having to be talked into it.

Mechanically it's doing the same question-formulation work as Guess Who, but it adds category reasoning on top ("am I an animal?" "do I live in water?") which makes it a stronger fit for kids working on categorization and semantic organization, not just yes/no questions. The pace also rewards kids who need practice thinking on their feet instead of turn-taking slowly around a table.

Ages 7 and up on the box, though a lot of therapists run a simplified version earlier by letting an adult ask the questions and having the child just answer.

## 5. Rory's Story Cubes

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/rorys-story-cubes.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07P3MB9H8/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Nine dice, each face a tiny picture: a key, a lightning bolt, a question mark. You roll them all and build a story that touches every image somehow. There's no board, no win condition, no losing. It's the closest thing on this list to a pure narrative-language tool.

That makes it the go-to pick for sequencing and storytelling goals specifically. A kid who can name nouns fine but struggles to string them into a narrative with a beginning, middle, and end gets forced into exactly that structure by the dice. Therapists also use it for transition words like "first," "then," and "after that," since the roll naturally produces a string of unrelated images that need connecting.

It's also genuinely portable. Small tin, no setup, works in a hallway or a waiting room as well as it works at a table. If you only have room in a bag for one thing that isn't a full board game, this is usually it.

## 6. Connect 4

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/connect-4.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00D8STBHY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Connect 4 doesn't look like a language game at all, and mostly it isn't. It's a two-player strategy game about getting four pieces in a row. What it's actually good for is stealth-drilling articulation without the session feeling like a drill, because every single disc drop is a turn, and turns come fast.

We've seen it used for prepositions too, which is a nice unexpected fit: describing where you're dropping a piece ("on top of," "next to," "in between") gives a kid live practice with spatial language while they're focused on beating their opponent, not on the vocabulary. That's the sweet spot: the language work happens as a side effect of a game they actually want to win.

It's cheap, it's everywhere, and it survives being thrown in a bag for years. Not a deep game, but it doesn't need to be for what it's doing here.

## 7. Zingo

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/zingo.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01N94ELDH/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Zingo is Bingo with a dispenser that spits out tiles one at a time, and it's one of the few games on this list that was actually built with early language development in mind rather than adapted for it. Every tile shows a picture and a word, so kids are reading (or pattern-matching, if they're not reading yet) and saying the word out loud to claim it.

The fast, repeated word-naming is exactly what makes it useful for articulation carryover. Kids call out the same handful of target words dozens of times across a session without noticing the repetition, because they're racing to fill their card before anyone else. Categories and vocabulary building come along for free since the tile sets are grouped thematically (animals, food, everyday objects).

Ages 4 and up, 2 to 6 players, so it's one of the better options here for group or classroom settings rather than strictly one-on-one.

## 8. Pop the Pig

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pop-the-pig.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00SOTQMWY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If you've never seen this one, it's exactly what it sounds like: feed the pig burgers, press his head down, and eventually (nobody knows exactly when) his belly pops and the burgers go flying. Kids lose their minds over it, in the good way.

The appeal for younger kids specifically is that the "task" before each turn is tiny. Press the pig's head once, or twice, or however many times the die says, so there's a real turn happening every few seconds. That's a lot of reps in a short session. It also works well for basic concepts (colors on the burgers, counting the presses) and functional requesting ("my turn," "give me the pig") for kids who are earlier in their language development than most of the other games on this list are built for.

It's a toy as much as it's a game, which is exactly why it belongs here. For a three- or four-year-old who isn't ready for a board and cards yet, this is often the entry point.

## 9. Sorry!

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/sorry.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B076HK9H7Z/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Sorry! is the outlier on this list: a real board game with real strategy, aimed at kids who've outgrown roll-and-move simplicity but aren't ready for anything heavier. We're including it because the skill it targets isn't articulation or vocabulary at all. It's pragmatic language: how you handle getting sent back to Start when you were one space from home.

That's a bigger deal than it sounds. A lot of the social work in speech therapy for school-age kids isn't about producing sounds correctly, it's about handling frustration, losing gracefully, and not derailing a game because something unfair just happened to them. Sorry! manufactures that moment on purpose, repeatedly, in a low-stakes setting where an adult can coach through it in real time.

It runs longer than everything else on this list, 30 to 45 minutes, so it's more of an end-of-session or full-session game than a quick drill tool. Worth having in the rotation specifically for the kids who need that particular practice.

## Conclusion

None of these games were built to replace a real treatment plan, and any SLP will tell you that. What they're good for is making the repetition disappear into something a kid wants to keep doing — which, if you've ever tried to get a five-year-old through fifty reps of the same word, is most of the battle. Candy Land and Pop the Pig are the easiest starting points if you're building a kit from scratch; Jenga and Rory's Story Cubes are the ones that'll still be useful once that same kid is seven.

Again, we're not SLPs. If you're a parent working from this list, run it by your kid's actual therapist first, they'll know what fits the specific goals in play. This list came out of reading what practicing speech-language pathologists have written about their own game closets, not out of clinical experience of our own. The sources we leaned on most: [Speech Therapy Store's board game roundup](https://www.speechtherapystore.com/speech-therapy-board-games/), [SimplePractice's list of games for speech therapy](https://www.simplepractice.com/blog/9-best-speech-therapy-games/), and [Communication Community's picks](https://www.communicationcommunity.com/best-board-games-for-speech-therapy-and-home/), along with a handful of individual SLP blog posts on specific games.
