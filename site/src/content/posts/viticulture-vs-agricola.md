Both games give you a small plot of land and a couple of workers, then hand you a calendar that's too short. Both are worker placement, so the space you need is always the one your neighbour just took. And they share a designer, sort of. Uwe Rosenberg made Agricola, and when Stonemaier put together Viticulture: Essential Edition, Rosenberg is the one who picked which pieces of the *Tuscany* expansion made it into the box.

So people search "Viticulture vs Agricola" expecting two versions of the same game. We've played a lot of both, and they aren't. Agricola is the game that became one of our group's favourites after Kaitlyn got it for her birthday. Viticulture is the one I reach for when someone at the table has never played a worker placement game. Why comes down to one thing: what happens when you fall behind.

**Buy Viticulture if** you want a medium-weight strategy game that newer players will actually enjoy, you regularly have five or six people, or you want the theme to carry some of the teaching. **Buy Agricola if** you love a tight puzzle, your group plays at one to four, and you want a game that keeps finding new ways to squeeze you after dozens of plays.

## The Core Loop, Side by Side

In Agricola you're a farming family. Over 14 rounds you place your people to gather wood and clay, plough fields, fence pastures, collect animals and grow your family. Six times during the game a harvest comes, and every family member needs food. Come up short and you take a begging card, which is worth minus three points at the end.

In Viticulture you've inherited a run-down vineyard. Each year you plant vines, harvest grapes, crush them into wine, let it age and fill wine orders for victory points. The first player to 20 points triggers the end of the year, and the game ends there.

Both games run on the same basic rhythm. Place a worker, take the action, get blocked, adjust. Where they split is what the game does to you when you get it wrong.

## Hunger vs Tempo

**Agricola punishes mistakes. Viticulture just makes you slower.** This is the whole comparison, and everything else follows from it.

In Agricola the harvest is a debt that comes due whether you're ready or not. Every grown family member eats two food, and the harvests get closer together as the game goes on. Early on you have four rounds to prepare; by the end you get one. You'll spend a surprising number of turns taking actions you don't want, because the alternative is begging. Our Agricola review calls the game stressful at all times, and that's a compliment. The squeeze is the point.

Viticulture has no feeding. Nobody starves. If you have a bad year, you end it with fewer points than you wanted and you try again next spring. The pressure comes from the calendar instead. Everyone is racing to 20 points, and a vine you plant now won't turn into points for a couple of years. Fall behind and you lose the race. Nothing on your board goes wrong.

That difference changes who enjoys each game. A new player in Agricola can make one bad decision in round three and spend the next hour digging out of it. A new player in Viticulture has a slow first year and then starts catching up. I'd happily teach Viticulture to someone whose heaviest game so far is Catan. With Agricola, I'd have that conversation first.

## Where the Luck Lives

Neither game has dice. Both have cards, and the cards are where the arguments start.

**Agricola deals you your strategy.** At the start you get seven occupations and seven minor improvements, and those cards push you toward a plan. One game you're the pig breeder. Another, a card makes clay so cheap that you're the first to upgrade your house. It's great for replay value. It can also feel lopsided when someone's opening hand combos and yours doesn't. Plenty of groups draft the cards instead of dealing them, and that fixes most of it.

**Viticulture spreads its luck across the whole game.** Visitor cards come out throughout, and each one bends a rule in your favour. The Essential Edition's visitor deck is much better filtered than the original's, but in a close finish the right visitor at the right moment can still decide it. You also draw vine and order cards, so sometimes the order you need just doesn't show up.

If randomness at the end of a close game drives your group mad, Agricola with drafting is the safer pick. The luck in Agricola comes at the start, where you can plan around it.

## Turn Order and Blocking

Both games are about taking the space someone else needs. Agricola is harsher about it. Each action space holds one worker, full stop. When the one space that gives you reeds is gone, it's gone, and at four players that happens a lot.

Viticulture softens this in two ways. Its action spaces open up more slots as the player count goes up, and the first slot pays a small bonus. And you get the Grande worker, an oversized meeple who can go on any space even when it's taken. You get exactly one per year, and deciding when to spend it is a proper decision.

Then there's the **wake-up chart**, which is still my favourite idea in either box. Each spring you choose your turn order for the year. Going first gets you the best spaces. Going later gets you a bonus card, coin or temporary worker. Agricola hands turn order to whoever takes the starting player space. Viticulture turns it into its own choice every year.

## Scoring: A Little of Everything vs a Race

Agricola scores across a long list of categories: fields, pastures, grain, vegetables, each kind of animal, rooms, family members and more. Most categories cap at four points and cost you a point if you have none. So the game quietly pushes you toward a balanced farm, and a good score sits somewhere in the 40s.

Viticulture is a straight race on a points track. Filling wine orders scores the most, and orders also add to your residual income, a small payout you collect every year. Specialising is fine here as long as it gets you to 20 first.

This is also why the two games feel different at the end. The last rounds of Agricola are a scramble to plug holes so you don't lose points. The last year of Viticulture is a sprint to fill one more order.

## Player Count

**Agricola (revised edition) is 1–4 players** in the base box. A separate expansion takes it to six, but that's another purchase. It's very good at two. Agricola made our [best two-player board games](/best-two-player-board-games/) list, and the solo mode is one of the better ones out there.

**Viticulture: Essential Edition is 2–6 players**, plus a 1-player mode using the included Automa deck (which is why some listings say 1–6). Four is the sweet spot. It works at two, though the board feels open. At six the downtime stretches it well past the 90 minutes on the box.

If your game night is regularly five or six people, that settles it. Viticulture.

## Teaching and Table Time

Neither is a light game. Agricola has more rules, more pieces and a longer setup, and our review's biggest complaint is that the box has no organiser, so setup and cleanup drag. Games run about 30 minutes per player, so a four-player game is a two-hour night.

Viticulture is quicker to teach because the theme does some of the work. Grapes go in the crush pad, wine goes in the cellar, older wine is worth more. People get it. The fiddly bit is the wine-order maths, which usually takes a new player a year of play to click. The box insert actually holds everything, too.

## Components and Price

Both have lovely wooden pieces. Agricola's animals are shaped like the animals they are, and after a while you stop noticing how nice that is until you play a game that uses cubes. Viticulture has glass tokens for grapes and wine that feel good in hand, and it's the better-organised box.

## Which One Should You Buy?

**Buy Viticulture if:** you want a strategy game you can bring to newer players without anyone feeling crushed. It's also the pick if your group is often five or six people, or if you'd rather lose a race than get punished for a mistake. It's the friendlier game and the better-looking box, and it's what I'd give as a gift.

**Buy Agricola if:** your group enjoys pressure and wants a puzzle that bites back. It suits you if you mostly play at two to four, or solo, and you like a game where the cards dealt at the start send you down a different road each time. It's the deeper game and the one we rate higher. It also asks more of the table.

**Buy both if:** you have a regular strategy group. They look alike from the outside, but they sit in different slots. Viticulture is the easier game for a mixed crowd. Agricola is for the nights when everyone's up for being squeezed. If you already own [Caverna](/agricola-vs-caverna-which-game-should-you-buy/), you have the looser Rosenberg farm game covered, and Viticulture adds more variety to your shelf than Agricola would.

Still can't decide? Think about the last time your group played something hard and someone had a bad game. If they laughed it off and asked for a rematch, get Agricola. If they went quiet, get Viticulture.
