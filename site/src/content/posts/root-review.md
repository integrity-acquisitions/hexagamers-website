## Overview

Four factions. One forest. Zero shared rules. In Root, the Marquise de Cat spreads her industrial empire across the woodland, the Eyrie Dynasties struggle to keep their birds in formation, the Woodland Alliance slowly turns the clearing folk against everyone, and the Vagabond wanders the map doing quests and picking fights for personal gain. The catch: every faction scores differently, moves differently, and loses differently. You're technically playing the same game — but the experience at the table feels nothing alike depending on which faction you pick up.

## Ryan's Review

### Likes

- True asymmetry — every faction has a genuinely different game loop, not just different stats
- Storybook-quality art that makes the table look incredible
- The politics shift constantly; an early leader rarely stays there
- High replay value — learning each faction is basically learning a new game
- Once the group clicks with it, the debrief after is almost as good as the game itself

### Dislikes

- Steep learning curve — teaching four different rulesets at once to new players is a real ask
- Balance feels off in low player counts; some factions are hard to play well without a full table
- The Eyrie's programming mechanic can punish beginners badly before they understand the trap they're building
- Rulebook is dense and assumes a lot of board game literacy

## Getting It to the Table

A friend brought Root to game night and we spent twenty minutes just reading faction summaries before the first card was played. My reaction was somewhere between impressed and overwhelmed. Four factions, four sets of rules, one board — I kept waiting for someone to admit we'd bitten off more than we could chew.

We didn't. We muddled through a learning game and by round three I was genuinely nervous about the Woodland Alliance. By round five I was losing to them. It was one of those sessions where the game clicked late and then suddenly made complete sense — like the rules assembled themselves mid-play.

## Thoughts

**The asymmetry is the whole thing.** Most games with different factions mean different starting positions or slightly different abilities. Root doesn't do that. The Marquise scores by building workshops and sawmills. The Eyrie score by completing a program of actions that gets harder to maintain every turn. The Woodland Alliance scores by inciting sympathy across clearings, which requires none of what the other factions are doing. Playing Root once as one faction and then switching to another is a legitimately different experience. That's rare.

**The Marquise de Cat is the game's anchor.** New players often start here because the rules are the closest to a traditional area control game. You place warriors, build structures, and score when you use those structures' abilities. It's still more complex than it sounds, but it gives first-timers something familiar to hold onto while everyone else figures out their own situation.

**The Eyrie is where the game starts biting back.** You're building a program at the start of each round — move, recruit, battle, build — and then you have to execute every action in that program or your government collapses and you lose most of your board position. The tension is you'll keep adding more actions each turn to score points, and eventually the program becomes impossible to complete. Managing that spiral is one of the most interesting constraints in any game I've played. But if you don't understand why it works that way, it just feels cruel.

**The Woodland Alliance is patient in a way that feels unfair.** They spread sympathy tokens across the map and don't score points until later in the game — but when they do, they come fast. You spend the first half of the game watching them do seemingly nothing, and the second half scrambling to stop them. Some players find this unfair. I find it accurate to guerrilla warfare, which is exactly the theme. When they flip a clearing to revolt and place a base, other players get genuinely annoyed. That's design working as intended.

**The Vagabond is everyone's wildcard.** One warrior, a backpack of items, and a relationship track with every other faction. They can help or hurt anyone. Good Vagabond play means reading the table and becoming useful — or dangerous — at the right moment. In our group, whoever plays Vagabond becomes the swing vote for who wins, which makes them a target as often as an ally.

**The components are genuinely beautiful.** Kyle Ferrin's art is something between a children's picture book and a woodblock print — soft, detailed, warm. The cats look like cats. The birds look regal and doomed. Even the board, just a forest clearing with numbered regions, is pleasant to look at. Leder Games put real craft into the physical object and it shows at the table.

**One honest complaint about new player games:** if your table has two players who have played Root before and two who haven't, the experienced players will have a real edge in the first half. Not just from knowing the rules — from knowing which factions to pressure early and when to let the beginner Alliance breathe. We've had to pull our punches in learning games, which isn't ideal. Root rewards familiarity in a way that makes early games uneven.

**Price is fair for what you get.** The base game runs around $55–65 depending on where you buy. For the components, art, and sheer amount of game inside, that's honest value. The expansions add more factions and player count, which is where the real long-term investment is — but the base game alone has enough replay value to justify it.

## Conclusion

Root rewards groups willing to put in the learning time. The asymmetric design is the best version of this idea I've seen in a board game — each faction feels like it was built from the ground up for a different kind of player. If your group likes strategy games and doesn't mind a multi-session learning curve, this earns a permanent spot on the shelf. Pull it out with patient players, expect a rough first game, and expect to talk about it for an hour after.
