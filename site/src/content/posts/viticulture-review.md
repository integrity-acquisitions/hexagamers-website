## Overview

You own a small vineyard in Tuscany that you inherited with almost nothing in it. Plant vines, hire seasonal help, build a cellar, and turn grapes into wine that can actually fill an order. The catch is that every worker you place takes a spot your neighbour wanted, and the year is only so long.

## Ryan's Review

### Likes
- The wake-up chart, where picking your turn order is a real decision every single year
- Visitor cards give you a reason to keep playing after you think you've solved it
- Components are genuinely lovely, and the box insert actually works
- Scales down to two players better than most worker placement games
- The theme is doing real work, not just sitting on top of a points salad

### Dislikes
- Visitor card draws can swing a close game
- The first year feels slow while you wait for your vines to be worth anything
- Six players stretches it well past the 90 minutes on the box
- The wine-order maths is fiddly to teach to someone who's never played

## Getting It to the Table

I came to Viticulture backwards. We had already put it at number nine on our [worker placement list](/best-worker-placement-board-games-reviews/) on the strength of the theme and the components, and I had played it enough at other people's tables to know I liked it — but I hadn't owned a copy or taught it myself. Teaching a game is the real test. You find out very quickly which rules are load-bearing and which ones you have been hand-waving for years.

My honest first reaction, years ago, was that this looked like a game trading on its theme. Wine, Tuscany, a nice box. I have been burned by pretty games before. The thing that changed my mind was not the vineyard at all. It was the wake-up chart.

## Thoughts

**The wake-up order is the best idea in the box.** At the start of each year, everyone places a rooster on a chart to claim their turn order. Going first means you get the pick of the action spaces. Going later means you get a bonus: a card, a coin, a temporary worker for the year. So every year opens with a genuine dilemma. Do you need that specific space badly enough to give up the freebie? Most worker placement games hand you turn order and move on. Here it's a decision you make with real information about what you need.

**The seasons split your workers in half.** A year runs summer then winter, and your workers have to cover both. Summer is planting, building, and giving tours for cash. Winter is harvesting, making wine, and filling orders. You never have enough people for everything you want to do, and a worker spent in summer is not there in winter. The squeeze is the game.

**The Grande worker is the pressure valve.** You get one oversized meeple who can occupy any action space even if it's already taken. Knowing you have exactly one of those per year changes how you sit on a spot. I have spent mine in the first season out of panic more times than I would like to admit, and then spent the rest of the year regretting it.

**Nothing happens fast, and that's the design.** You plant a vine, and it does nothing that year. You harvest it, and now you have grapes doing nothing. You crush them, and now you have wine that has to age before it's worth anything to the better orders. Your first year can feel like you're losing. It isn't a pacing problem so much as the game telling you what kind of game it is. But it does mean a new player's first hour is the least fun hour they'll have, and that's worth warning them about.

**Visitor cards are where the arguments start.** Each one bends a rule slightly in your favour, and the Essential Edition includes a much larger, better-filtered set than the original release. They're what stops the game turning into a solved routine. They're also the part I'd criticise: in a tight game, someone drawing the right visitor at the right moment can decide it. Not often, and Stonemaier clearly pruned the worst offenders, but if your group hates any randomness deciding a close finish, this is where it will come from.

**Player count matters more than the box suggests.** At two it's tight and quiet, and the action spaces rarely get contested, which some people prefer and some find toothless. Four is the sweet spot — enough competition for spaces that the wake-up chart really bites. At six the board fills up and the downtime between your turns gets long. The box says 90 minutes; six players with anyone thinking hard will run well past that.

**A note on what edition you're buying, because this trips people up.** Viticulture: Essential Edition is the version to get, and it's the one linked here. It folds in the parts of the *Tuscany* expansion that people actually wanted: Mama and Papa cards for varied starting resources, field cards you can sell when you're desperate for cash, the wider visitor deck, and the Automa solo deck. Jamey Stegmaier and Alan Stone designed the original; Uwe Rosenberg, of [Agricola](/agricola-review/) and Caverna fame, helped assemble this edition. If you like the way those games squeeze you, that lineage is not a coincidence.

One clarification on player count, since Amazon and the box disagree. Competitively it's **2–6 players**. The Essential Edition also ships with the Automa solo variant, which is why the box and some listings say 1–6. Both are true; you're not buying a different product.

**Components and price.** The wooden pieces are excellent, the glass tokens for grapes and wine feel good in hand, and the insert holds everything without a bag rodeo at the end of the night. It sits at the upper end of mid-price for a worker placement game. Against [Agricola](/agricola-review/) it's the friendlier and better-produced box, where Agricola is the meaner and more demanding one. I would not call either a better game, and I'd happily own both.

## Conclusion

Viticulture takes a genre known for grim subsistence farming and makes it pleasant without making it soft. The wake-up chart and the summer/winter split still force real decisions every year. It's a good fit for groups who want a medium-weight strategy game with a theme that carries, and it's an easier sell to newcomers than most games of its depth. If you're building out a worker placement shelf and you already have something punishing on it, this is the one that gets played more often.
