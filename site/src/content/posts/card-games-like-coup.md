Coup has a specific problem: it's too short. Not in a bad way. You finish a game in twelve minutes, someone immediately reshuffles, and three hours later your group has played eleven rounds and nobody has suggested anything else. Then a month goes by and the table wants something *adjacent*. Same shape, different clothes.

That's a harder request than it sounds. Search for games like Coup and you get handed Werewolf, Secret Hitler, and a pile of full-size social deduction boxes that need nine people and forty minutes. Those are great games. They are not what you want when you're looking for the thing that fits in a jacket pocket and resolves an argument in ninety seconds. We went looking for the actual neighbours: small card games where you claim something you might not have, and someone across the table has to decide whether to believe you.

A quick note on what we mean by "like Coup," because it splits into two families and mixing them up is why so many recommendation lists feel off.

## What actually makes a game feel like Coup

Coup runs on **claim-and-challenge**. You say "I'm the Duke." You might not be the Duke. Anyone can call you on it, and whoever is wrong loses an influence card. That's the whole engine, and it's why the game is more about your face than your hand.

Two things follow from that. The bluff is *declared out loud and publicly punished*. You're not quietly hoping nobody notices, you're making an assertion and daring someone to test it. And the punishment is immediate. There's no waiting until the end to find out you were lied to.

So this list leans on games with that same challenge loop, and treats the "hidden role, argue for twenty minutes" games as a related but separate genre. If that's what you're after, our [Best Social Deduction Board Games](/best-social-deduction-board-games/) list covers that ground properly, and [Best Games Like Secret Hitler](/best-games-like-secret-hitler/) narrows to the political end of it. We also skipped anything that needs more than about ten minutes to teach. Coup's whole appeal is that you can explain it while shuffling.

One thing worth saying up front: only two games on this list are direct mechanical cousins. The rest capture one specific piece of what Coup does well. We've flagged which is which rather than pretending they're all interchangeable.

## 1. Skull

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Skull_kfpcyd.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08R2BPLSN/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If you want the closest thing to Coup's nerve, this is it. Each player has four cards: three roses and one skull. You place cards face down, then someone starts bidding on how many they can flip over without hitting a skull. Win two bids and you win the game.

That's the entire rulebook. It sounds like nothing written down, and it is genuinely one of the tensest things you can put on a table. The reason is the same as Coup's: you are betting on your read of a person, not on odds. When someone bids four, you are not calculating probability. You are trying to work out whether they're confident or performing confidence, and those look identical until you flip the card.

Where it beats Coup is the silence. Coup is loud, everyone talking over each other. Skull goes very quiet right before a big flip. That shift is worth experiencing.

The catch is player count. It wants at least four to sing, and at three it's thin. It's also over fast enough that you'll want to play three in a row, which is exactly the Coup problem again. Consider that a feature.

## 2. Cockroach Poker

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Cockroach_Poker_etvuop.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00FZY9EDK/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The other true mechanical cousin, and the one most people have never heard of. You slide a card face down to another player and announce what it is: "this is a rat." They either accept your claim and guess whether you're lying, or peek at it and pass it along to someone else with their own claim attached. Collect four of the same critter and you lose.

What makes it Coup-like is that the lie is mandatory and constant. You're not waiting for a good bluffing opportunity, every single card handoff is one. And because a card can travel around the table picking up new claims, you end up bluffing about a bluff about a bluff. The person who finally accepts it has to untangle three layers of who-knows-what.

It's meaner than Coup, in a good way. You choose your target, so it rewards paying attention to who's nearly dead. Everyone will gang up on the leader, which is either the best part or mildly upsetting depending on your friends.

Cheap, tiny box, plays up to six. The artwork is deliberately ugly and grows on you.

## 3. Love Letter ([Full Review Here](/love-letter-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Love_Letter_yl7dle.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0F2J8DVHH/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Love Letter gets recommended alongside Coup constantly, and it's worth being precise about why, because the two are near-opposites. Coup is heavy bluffing with light deduction. Love Letter is heavy deduction with light bluffing. You hold one card, you draw a second, you play one of them. You're rarely lying outright. You're working out what everyone else is holding from what they chose to play.

So why is it here? Because it scratches the same *slot*. Sixteen cards, two minutes to teach, a full game in the time it takes to decide what to order. When the group wants "another quick one," this and Coup are competing for the same moment in the evening.

The tell you learn to read is hesitation. Someone pausing before they play a Guard means they're deciding whether to name you, and that pause is information. It's a quieter skill than Coup's outright lying, but it's the same muscle.

Worth owning both. They're different enough that having one doesn't make the other redundant, and they're both cheap.

## 4. Citadels

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Citadels-2016_zu5wlu.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MDLM6I2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Here's the connection nobody mentions: Citadels is the game Coup's character roles came from. Coup is set in the Resistance universe, but the "everyone secretly claims a role with a special power, and roles can attack each other" structure is lifted straight out of Bruno Faidutti's design. Playing them back to back makes the lineage obvious.

The difference is that in Citadels you actually *are* the role you picked. You choose it secretly from a passed deck. The bluffing is in the deduction that follows. The Assassin has to guess which character to kill, the Thief has to guess who's holding gold, and everyone is trying to read what you drafted based on what you needed. When the Assassin names your character and you have to flip it over, that's the Coup challenge moment with a build-up.

This is the longest game on the list at 30 to 60 minutes, and it has an actual city-building layer on top. So it's the pick when you want the Coup dynamic to be the spine of a real evening rather than a filler.

Fair warning: with eight players the downtime between your turns gets long. It's best around five or six.

## 5. Sheriff of Nottingham ([Full Review Here](/sheriff-of-nottingham-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/sheriff-box-1024x1002.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007EZMABG/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Coup's bluff is a snap decision. Sheriff of Nottingham stretches that exact decision out into a negotiation, and it turns out the tension survives being slowed down.

You load a bag with goods, declare what's inside, and hand it to whoever is playing Sheriff that round. They can open it or wave you through. Everything about that exchange is Coup's challenge mechanic, except you get to *talk*. You can plead, bribe, offer a cut, or point at someone else's obviously suspicious bag. The Sheriff can bluff back about whether they're even going to bother inspecting.

The bribery is what elevates it. Offering coins to be let through is a tell in itself, which means you sometimes bribe while holding a completely legal bag just to muddy the read. Watching someone accept a bribe to ignore a bag of apples is very funny.

It runs an hour and only goes to five players, so it's a different commitment from Coup. But if the part of Coup you love is the staring contest, this is the version with room to breathe.

## 6. One Night Ultimate Werewolf ([Full Review Here](/one-night-ultimate-werewolf-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/One_night_ultimate_werewolf_pvnehl.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00HS7GG5G/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Traditional Werewolf has a fatal problem: get eliminated early and you spend twenty minutes watching. One Night fixes it by compressing the whole thing into a single night phase and one vote. Nobody sits out.

The Coup overlap is claiming a role you don't have. Everyone wakes in order, does their thing, and then argues. Someone says they're the Seer and saw you as a werewolf. Are they? The counter-claim ("no, *I'm* the Seer") is the exact Coup moment of two people asserting the same thing and the table having to sort it out.

The wrinkle that makes it genuinely hard is that roles can be swapped while your eyes are shut. You might have gone to sleep as a villager and woken up a werewolf without knowing. People end up lying accidentally, which is a strange and great thing to watch.

Ten minutes a game. It needs the free app for the night phase narration, which some people find annoying and some find essential. Best at five or more.

## 7. The Resistance: Avalon ([Full Review Here](/the-resistance-avalon-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/The-Resistance-Avalon.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B009SAAV0C/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Avalon shares Coup's universe and almost none of its structure. It's on this list because it's where Coup groups tend to graduate to, and the reason is straightforward. It takes the "I don't trust you" feeling and makes it last half an hour instead of ten seconds.

You're on a hidden team. You vote on who goes on missions. Missions fail, and suspicion attaches to everyone who backed them. Where Coup's bluffing is one-off and disposable, here it accumulates: a lie you told in round one gets re-examined in round four when the picture changes. That's a different and heavier kind of pressure.

It also fixes Coup's biggest weakness. Coup dies at the table if nobody bluffs. Conservative players just play it straight and drain the game. Avalon doesn't allow that. If you're evil you have no honest option, so the deception is structural rather than optional.

Needs five players minimum, which is the main barrier. But if your Coup nights consistently pull six or seven people, this is the obvious next box.

## 8. Saboteur

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/saboteur-box_chsknr.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B013FAC1JO/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Saboteur is the cheapest way to get a hidden traitor onto your table. Dwarves are digging a tunnel toward gold. One or two of them secretly don't want it to get there. Everyone plays path cards to extend the tunnel, and the saboteurs quietly make bad-looking-good decisions.

The Coup connection is thinner here, and it's worth being honest about that. There's no challenge mechanic and no direct claim. What it does share is the specific pleasure of doing something suspicious and then explaining it away with a straight face. "I had no other card I could play" is the Saboteur equivalent of claiming Duke, and it's just as often a lie.

The play-a-card-and-justify-it rhythm keeps everyone busy, which suits groups that get restless during pure-argument games. It's also the most forgiving on this list. You can play at three or at ten without it falling apart.

Rounds run about thirty minutes across three hands. Bottom of the list because the bluffing is background rather than the point, but it's a few dollars and it consistently earns its place in a bag.

## Conclusion

If you want one game and you want it to feel like Coup, buy **Skull**. It's the purest version of the thing Coup is actually good at — a bet placed entirely on reading a person — and it costs less than lunch. **Cockroach Poker** is the deeper cut and the better choice if your group has already worn Skull out.

Going the other direction, **Sheriff of Nottingham** and **Citadels** take Coup's bluff and give it an actual game to live inside, for nights when a twelve-minute filler isn't what anyone wants. And if your Coup evenings routinely draw six or more people, **Avalon** is where that group is heading whether you buy it now or in six months.
