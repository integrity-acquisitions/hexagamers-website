Ask ten people what a "war game" is and you will get ten answers. Some picture a hex map covered in tiny cardboard counters and a rulebook the size of a phone book. Others picture plastic tanks marching across a map of Europe. And plenty of people mean something looser — any game where the whole point is to knock the other side off the board.

I lean toward that wider definition here. A war game, for this list, is a game where conflict is the engine. Not a side activity, not a tiebreaker — the reason you sit down. That covers the hardcore historical stuff, but it also covers the big thematic epics and the leaner tactical games that get you fighting in 20 minutes.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](#best-tabletop-war-games-comparison-table)

One thing I'll flag up front: this is a list of board and card war games, the kind that come complete in a box. If you're after miniatures war gaming (Warhammer 40K, Star Wars: Legion, A Song of Ice and Fire), that's a related but different hobby with its own painting, list-building, and buy-in. Everything below you can open, punch, and play the same night.

I've tried to spread the picks across the whole range: quick to marathon, two-player duels to six-player free-for-alls, gentle on-ramps to genuinely heavy systems. Each entry gets the honest version — what makes people love it, and where it frustrates them. Because these games are not cheap, and "it depends who you're playing with" is a real answer for most of them.

## Top Ten Tabletop War Games

## 1. Twilight Struggle

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/twilight-struggle.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B007XDRP1E/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If a war game can be a classic, this is the one. Twilight Struggle put a card-driven system on the whole Cold War — the US versus the Soviet Union, jockeying for influence across every region on the map without ever quite firing the big weapons. It sat at the top of BoardGameGeek's overall rankings for years, and it earned that spot the hard way.

Here's the twist that makes it sing. Every card you hold has a number for spreading your own influence, but it also has an event printed on it — and half the events help your opponent. You have to play those cards anyway. So each turn is a small tragedy: do I use this card for my own gain and hand the enemy their event, or trigger my own weaker event just to keep theirs off the table? That tension never lets up.

**Why people love it:** the theme is welded to the mechanics so tightly that history seems to replay itself at the table. The Cuban Missile Crisis, the space race, the scramble for the Third World — they all surface through the cards, and it never feels bolted on. Two evenly matched players get a nail-biter every single time.

**The catch:** it's a two-player-only game with real weight. There's a big deck to learn, a lot of geography, and the scoring can swing hard if you don't understand it — a mistimed scoring card can gut you. New players often lose their first few games badly before it clicks, and the DEFCON track means you can straight-up lose by accidentally starting nuclear war. It rewards study, which some people adore and others find like homework.

If you want the definitive two-player war game and you have a regular opponent, start here. It stays great for years.

## 2. War of the Ring (Second Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/war-of-the-ring.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1903385113/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the Lord of the Rings war game the theme always deserved. One player takes the Free Peoples — Gondor, Rohan, the elves, the whole coalition trying to survive. The other takes Sauron and the vast armies of Shadow. And the beauty of it is that the two sides are not trying to do the same thing at all.

Shadow wins by conquest, grinding the map down with sheer numbers. The Free Peoples can win that way too, in theory, but their real hope is the Fellowship — quietly marching the Ring toward Mount Doom while the military situation collapses around them. So the whole game is a race between two clocks. The Shadow player is always asking: do I chase the Ring-bearer, or do I just take another city and win the war outright?

**Why people love it:** the action dice are fantastic. Each turn you roll a fistful of custom dice and the faces dictate what you're even allowed to do — muster troops, move armies, advance the Fellowship, play event cards. You're constantly wrestling with a hand you didn't fully choose, and the theme pours out of every mechanic. Few games make you *feel* an epic the way this one does.

**The catch:** it is big and it is long. Setup takes a while, the rulebook is dense, and your first game will involve a lot of chart-checking. It's strictly two-player at its best (there's a multiplayer mode, but it's not why people buy it). And the asymmetry, while brilliant, means both players need a few games before it feels fair. Early on, whoever understands their side better tends to run away with it.

For LOTR fans and anyone who wants a two-player epic, it's about as good as it gets.

## 3. Scythe

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/scythe.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01IPUGYK6/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Scythe is the war game for people who think they don't like war games. It's set in an alternate-history 1920s Eastern Europe where diesel-punk mechs stomp across farmland, and the box art alone has sold a lot of copies. But underneath the gorgeous production is a tight engine builder with combat as the sharp edge, not the whole knife.

Most of what you do is build your little nation up — work the fields, gather resources, upgrade your abilities, deploy your mechs. Fighting exists, and it matters, but battles are quick and bloodless in a specific way: you secretly commit power and a combat card, reveal, and the loser retreats. Nobody's army gets wiped out. That single design choice is why Scythe crosses over so well.

**Why people love it:** it's beautiful, it plays smoothly once you know it, and everyone is doing something every round — there's very little downtime. The board control and the constant threat of a mech showing up on your border keep the tension high without the game turning into a slugfest. The solo mode (the Automa) is genuinely good too, which is rare.

**The catch:** the marketing sells "combat" harder than the game delivers it. If you sit down expecting a bloody war game, you may be surprised how little actual fighting happens — a lot of Scythe is threat and positioning, and some players go whole games barely swinging. It also has a real rules ramp for a first play, and the sheer number of icons can overwhelm newcomers. And there's a well-known quirk: winning a fight costs you resources, so the game sometimes rewards *not* attacking.

If you want a war game with a euro brain and a stunning table presence, this is the crossover pick. It earns its place.

## 4. Star Wars: Rebellion

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/star-wars-rebellion.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B017MLIGP0/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If you have ever wanted to *be* the Rebellion hiding from an Empire that outguns you at every turn, this is the game. Rebellion puts the entire original-trilogy galaxy on the table (Star Destroyers, the Death Star, dozens of named characters from Luke to Vader) and hands two players wildly different jobs.

The Empire has the fleets and the firepower, and it's hunting for one thing: the hidden Rebel base. Find it, crush it, win. The Rebels can't win a straight fight, so they don't try. They run, they sabotage, they build sympathy across the galaxy, and they stall — because for the Rebels, simply surviving long enough *is* victory. The whole game is a manhunt across a galaxy, and the tension of hiding that one base is unlike anything else.

**Why people love it:** the theme is total. Leaders go on missions, get captured, get rescued; you can freeze Han in carbonite or turn Luke to the dark side. Every session generates its own little Star Wars story, and the asymmetry means the two sides feel like completely different games. For fans, it's a dream.

**The catch:** it's a commitment — three to four hours, a large footprint, and a first game where both players are learning two separate rule sets at once. It's strictly two-player at heart (the four-player mode just splits each side into a team). And the hidden-base mechanic, brilliant as it is, can lead to a slow-burn middle where the Empire feels like it's searching in the dark for a long stretch. Patience required.

For two Star Wars fans who want an epic, few things on this list beat it.

## 5. Memoir '44

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/memoir-44.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0002TV2NS/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the on-ramp. If everything above sounds like too much, Memoir '44 is the WWII war game you can teach in ten minutes and finish in under an hour. It uses the Commands & Colors system — the board is split into left, center, and right, and the cards in your hand tell you which section you're allowed to give orders to this turn.

That one idea does a lot of heavy lifting. You might have a great plan for your right flank and simply not have the cards to execute it, so you improvise on the left instead. Battles are resolved with chunky custom dice, terrain matters, and the whole thing has the feel of a real engagement without asking you to memorize a rulebook. Each scenario recreates a specific historical battle, from Omaha Beach to the hedgerows.

**Why people love it:** it's fast, it's tactile, and it lowers the barrier to war gaming more than almost anything else. The scenario book means huge replay value out of the box, and the massive library of expansions (Eastern Front, Pacific, air power) can take it as deep as you want. It's a fantastic gateway for someone curious about the genre.

**The catch:** the card-driven orders introduce real luck. You can get stuck with a hand that won't let you act where you need to, and losing to a card draw stings. The historical scenarios aren't always balanced — some favor one side, which is why serious players play each map twice, once per army. And veterans of heavier war games sometimes find it too light, more beer-and-pretzels than grand strategy.

As a first war game, or a quick one for a weeknight, it's hard to beat.

## 6. Root ([Full Review Here](/root-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/root.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F454YF3/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Root looks like a children's book and plays like a knife fight. Cute woodland animals wage a genuine war for control of the forest, and the hook that made it a modern hit is that every single faction plays by completely different rules. This is asymmetry taken to its limit.

The Marquise de Cat is an occupying industrial power, building and spreading. The Eyrie birds are a rigid bureaucracy that must keep expanding or collapse under their own decrees. The Woodland Alliance is a guerrilla insurgency, weak until it isn't. And the Vagabond is a lone adventurer playing everyone off against each other. Four players can be doing four unrelated things, all fighting over the same board.

**Why people love it:** nothing else feels quite like it. Learning a new faction is like learning a new game, so the replay value is enormous, and the political table talk about who's winning and who needs to be knocked down is half the fun. When it clicks with the right group, it's one of the sharpest, most dynamic war games out there.

**The catch:** the asymmetry is also its biggest hurdle. One person usually has to teach four different rule sets, and a table where everyone's on their first game can be a rough, lopsided experience while people figure out their factions. It also needs players willing to gang up on the leader — if nobody polices the front-runner, the game can end abruptly. It rewards a group that plays it more than once.

Get past the learning curve and it's brilliant. Just don't make your first game your only game.

## 7. Axis & Allies: 1942 Edition

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/axis-and-allies-1942.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BZ9NJKQB/?tag=hexagamers-20)

For a lot of people, Axis & Allies *is* tabletop war gaming — it's the one they played in a basement as a teenager, pushing plastic tanks and infantry across a map of the whole world. The 1942 edition is the cleaned-up, streamlined take on that classic, dropping you into the war at its peak with five powers split across two sides.

The loop is grand strategy at its most recognizable. You collect income based on the territory you hold, spend it on new units at the factory, then move and fight across land, sea, and air. Big battles get resolved with fistfuls of dice, and holding the industrial centers is everything. It scratches the exact itch of commanding armies on a global scale.

**Why people love it:** the scope is the appeal. You're running an entire national war economy, making the kind of sweeping decisions about where to push and what to build that most lighter games never touch. The plastic minis and the world map have a nostalgic pull, and for many players this is a lifelong favorite they keep coming back to.

**The catch:** it's long, and the dice can be brutal — a well-planned attack can fall apart on bad rolls, which frustrates strategists who want their planning to matter more. Player elimination is possible, so someone can get knocked out and sit around waiting. And balance leans on players of similar skill; a newcomer against a veteran usually gets rolled. It's also very much of its era in feel: chunky and old-school rather than elegant.

If you want the classic push-armies-across-the-globe experience, this is the accessible way in.

## 8. Undaunted: Normandy

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/undaunted-normandy.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/1472834704/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Undaunted is the clever modern one. It takes deck-building, the mechanic from games like Dominion where you slowly improve a personal deck of cards, and welds it onto a tight WWII squad-level fight. Your cards *are* your soldiers, and it works far better than it has any right to.

Every card you draw is a specific soldier or order. Play a scout to expand onto the map, a rifleman to fight, a supporting order to gain the initiative. Casualties are handled beautifully and cruelly: when a soldier is hit, that card gets removed from your deck, so your force literally thins out as the battle grinds on. Managing your deck *is* managing your platoon, and losing your best cards hurts in a way a health bar never could.

**Why people love it:** it's fast, it's brutal, and it fits on a small table in under an hour. The deck-building gives it a strategic layer that most quick war games lack, and the campaign of linked scenarios gives you a reason to keep coming back. It's a smart, sharp two-player duel that respects your evening.

**The catch:** it's two-player only, and it leans more abstract than the plastic-army crowd might want — these are cards and simple counters, not sculpted minis. The fog-of-war and card draw introduce swing, so a run of bad luck can cost you a scenario. And some players find the theme a little thin compared to how tactile the mechanics are. It's a system you appreciate more than one you get swept up in.

For a modern, portable, genuinely strategic two-player war game, it's one of the best on this list.

## 9. Twilight Imperium (Fourth Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/twilight-imperium-4th-edition.webp)](https://store.asmodee.com/products/twilight-imperium-4th-edition)

Twilight Imperium is the all-day event. This is the one people plan a weekend around — three to six players building galactic empires, fighting fleet battles, and stabbing each other in the back across a table that takes up an entire room. It's a 4X game (explore, expand, exploit, exterminate) as much as a war game, but the war is very real and often decides everything.

Each player runs a wildly different alien race, and the galaxy is built fresh every game from tiles, so no two setups match. You research technology, build fleets, and grab objectives — but the beating heart of it is the negotiation. Alliances form and shatter, deals get made and broken, and the person who wins is often the one who read the table best, not the one with the biggest guns.

**Why people love it:** there's nothing else with this scope. When it comes together, a session of Twilight Imperium is a story you'll retell for years — the double-cross, the last-ditch fleet, the empire that came from nowhere. The politics and the sheer galactic sprawl make it a legendary experience for the right group.

**The catch:** it's an eight-hour game, minimum, and that's the whole review right there. It demands a full day, a big table, four-plus committed players who all know the rules, and a willingness to be attacked. Player elimination isn't formal, but a badly beaten player can spend hours mostly out of contention. And it is heavy — this is not a "let's give it a go tonight" purchase. It's a special-occasion monument.

If your group can field the time and the players, it's a bucket-list war game. If not, it'll sit on the shelf looking impressive.

## 10. Commands & Colors: Ancients

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/commands-and-colors-ancients.webp)](https://ca.nobleknight.com/PG/14105)

I wanted one pick for the players who mean the *real* thing when they say war game — hex maps, historical battles, block-and-counter tactics. Commands & Colors: Ancients is the best on-ramp to that world. It uses the same left-center-right card system as Memoir '44, but pushes it into the era of Rome and Carthage, with more depth and a lot more bite.

You command ancient armies of legions, war elephants, and cavalry across a hex board, refighting real battles like Cannae. The cards dictate which part of your line can act, so you're always balancing the plan you want against the orders you actually drew. Combat uses custom dice, unit types genuinely matter, and the block components (with stickers you apply) give it a proper old-school wargame feel.

**Why people love it:** it hits the sweet spot between accessible and meaty. It's a fraction of the complexity of a hardcore hex-and-counter game but scratches the same tactical itch, and the huge library of historical scenarios means you can refight the ancient world for years. For a lot of people, this is the game that turned them into war gamers.

**The catch:** the card-driven system carries the same luck issue as Memoir '44 — a hand that won't let you act where you need to can lose you a battle through no fault of your planning. Applying the block stickers up front is a chore. It's strictly two-player, and the presentation is functional rather than flashy; you're buying a system, not a showpiece. Scenario balance also varies, so serious players swap sides and play each twice.

If the harder end of war gaming calls to you but a phone-book rulebook doesn't, start right here.

## Conclusion

There's no single best tabletop war game, because the genre stretches so far. If you want the one most people should try first, **Memoir '44** is the easy on-ramp and **Twilight Struggle** is the two-player peak once you're ready for weight. Want an epic to build a whole evening around? **War of the Ring** and **Star Wars: Rebellion** are the thematic heavyweights, and **Twilight Imperium** is the once-a-year monument. And if you want a war game that doesn't feel like one, **Scythe** is the crossover that keeps pulling in people who swore they hated the genre.

The honest advice with any of these: match the game to your table before you match it to your taste. Most of these are two-player at heart, most reward playing more than once, and the biggest ones live or die on whether you can get the right people around the table for long enough. Pick the one that fits the group you actually have.
