Most "party game" lists top out around 6 to 8 players, because most party games top out around 6 to 8 players. Once your group hits double digits — a family reunion, an office party, a birthday where half the block shows up — the usual picks start falling apart. Someone's not playing this round. A round takes forever because everyone's waiting on one player's turn. The game technically supports 12 people the way a card table technically supports 12 plates.

We already cover the 6-to-8 range in our [best board games for 8 players](/best-board-games-for-8-players/) list, and general party-night picks in [best party board games](/best-party-board-games-with-reviews/). This list is narrower on purpose: every game here is built so the *entire* group plays at once, split into a couple of teams or working as one big crowd, with no one benched waiting for their turn to come back around. If a game needs you to break into separate 4-person tables to actually function, it's not on this list — that's a different problem with a different solution.

## What Makes a Game Actually Work for a Large Group

We looked for three things. First, no elimination — nobody's out in round two watching everyone else finish. Second, simultaneous participation — the whole team is doing something on every turn, not just the one person holding the cards. Third, rounds stay short even as the group grows, because the thing that kills a 12-person game night fastest is a single turn eating five minutes.

## 1. Wavelength

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/wavelength.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07T446163/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Wavelength splits the table into two teams and asks one person per round to give a clue that lands a hidden target somewhere on a spectrum — Hot to Cold, Overrated to Underrated, whatever the card says. Everyone else guesses where the dial actually sits. It sounds almost too simple to work at 14 people, and then you watch a room full of coworkers argue about whether "The Office" counts as "Overrated" or "Underrated" and understand exactly why it works.

The reason it holds up at scale is that the guessing is a group decision. A team of six has to talk it out and land on one answer, which means everyone's engaged even when they're not the one giving the clue. Nobody's staring at their phone waiting for their turn — there is no individual turn to wait for once you're on a team.

We've seen this one specifically requested for office holiday parties, and it's easy to see why. It's not raunchy by default, the clues can be tuned to the crowd, and the rounds move fast enough that a 16-person game doesn't drag into a second hour. The dial and stand feel a little flimsy for the price, which is the one real knock against it — everything else about the design is close to perfect for what it's trying to do.

## 2. Herd Mentality

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Herd_Mentality_qjwucc.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B093HBBMPT/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Herd Mentality reads a question off a card — "Name a fruit," "Name a Kardashian" — and everyone writes down their own answer at the same time. Whoever matches the most common answer gets a point. Whoever's alone on their own answer gets stuck with the Pink Cow of Doom and loses a point. There's no team-building, no strategy card, nothing to teach beyond "write down what you think everyone else will write."

That's the whole appeal for a group this size. There's genuinely no ceiling on players because everyone is doing the exact same thing on every turn, simultaneously, with a pencil. We've had it work fine with a card table's worth of people and seen reports of groups pushing well past 20 with a bigger scoreboard. The box says 4 to 20 and doesn't seem to be exaggerating.

The catch is that it's shallow by design. This isn't a game you play for an hour straight — it's the game you open when a party needs a reset, plays for 20 minutes, and gets put away again. Play it back to back with something that has more meat and it earns its price fast.

## 3. Hues and Cues ([Full Review Here](/hues-and-cues-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/hues-and-cues.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B084D2XQ9F/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Hues and Cues is built around a giant grid of colour squares. One player draws a card with a hidden target square and gives a one- or two-word clue — everyone else places a peg where they think that square is. Points get handed out for how close you land, and everyone's placing at the same time, so there's no downtime between turns because there aren't really individual turns.

We played this with a decent-sized group and it held up exactly the way you'd hope — virtually no setup, and once the first clue drops, everyone's moving pegs within seconds. Scoring stays simple enough that nobody needs a rules refresher halfway through, which matters when you've got 10 people at the table and half of them have never played before.

The honest weak spot is the insider-clue problem: sometimes a clue giver's word choice clicks for a subset of the table and not the rest, and the people who don't get it just wait for someone who does. Our workaround was to cluster near whoever seemed to be reading the clue right, which isn't elegant but keeps the round moving. It's also not a game for anyone colourblind — sorry, Tito.

## 4. Trivial Pursuit

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/trivial-pursuit.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01N4MXCK4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Trivial Pursuit is the one everybody already half-knows, which is exactly the point at a big gathering — you're not spending 15 minutes teaching rules to 14 people. Roll, land on a category, answer a question, collect a wedge. The version everyone remembers is built for a handful of individual players, but the format bends easily into teams once your group outgrows individual turns, and that's how we'd recommend running it here.

Split into teams of three or four, rotate who answers, and suddenly a game built for 6 works fine for 20. The category wedges keep it from turning into one person's personal trivia showcase — nobody's an expert in Geography, Entertainment, and Science all at once, so the team format naturally spreads the load.

Where it drags is pace — a full board takes real time, and at a big party you're better off setting a house rule (first team to 4 wedges wins, skip the hub-square rule) than playing it straight through. The classic edition's questions also skew dated in spots, which is worth knowing before you buy the box expecting current-events trivia.

## 5. Cranium

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/cranium.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0D6MFPGT7/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Cranium mixes trivia, sculpting, drawing, and charades into one board, on the theory that any given person is only going to be good at one or two of those. Teams move around the board hitting different challenge types, so the friend who can't hum a tune to save their life gets to redeem themselves two spaces later with a word puzzle.

That mix is what makes it work for a mismatched crowd — cousins, coworkers, people who just met at the reunion. Nobody's stuck being bad at the whole game, because the whole game keeps changing what "good at this" means. Teams can run four or five people deep without anyone feeling like dead weight, since every challenge needs different skills to pull off.

It runs long — a full game with big teams can eat well over an hour — so at a party with a lot going on, treat it as the centerpiece activity rather than a quick filler between other things. The clay and drawing components also wear out with repeated play; if your copy's a few years old, check that the pens still work before you commit a group to it.

## 6. Monikers (Family Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/monikers.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FVBB5W1W/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Monikers is charades' smarter cousin. You're guessing names off cards — people, characters, things — across three rounds, and each round gets harder: full verbal clues in round one, a single word in round two, no words at all by round three. Same deck, same names, but by the third pass your team is decoding pure gibberish gestures based on what was already said twice.

That structure is what makes it scale so well. Big teams don't slow the game down — they add more clue-givers, which means more variety in how each name gets described, which is genuinely funnier with more people involved, not despite it. We'd point large groups toward the **Family Edition** specifically; the original Monikers deck is written for a 17+ crowd and leans hard into pop culture and adult humor that won't land the same way at a mixed-age reunion.

The one thing to plan around is that round three (silent charades) can bog down if your team hasn't built any shorthand yet — that's a natural side effect of a bigger, less-familiar group. Running one warm-up round before it counts smooths this out.

## 7. Family Feud (Ultimate Edition)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/family-feud.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DSCGV9VC/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Family Feud translates the TV format almost exactly: survey says, two teams, buzz in, guess the most popular answer, steal if the other team whiffs. If your group has ever watched the show together, you already know how to play this one before you've read a rule.

The team-vs-team format is what earns it a spot here — it doesn't matter if one side has 4 people and the other has 9, because the whole team is contributing to a shared guess rather than taking individual turns. It's genuinely one of the easiest large-group games to run cold, with almost no setup and a rule set you can explain in under a minute.

The trade-off is depth — there's no real strategy here beyond guessing what the average person thinks, so it's a great opener or late-night wind-down and a weak choice if your group wants something with more decision-making. The included question set also has a shelf life; once your group's cycled through the cards a couple of times, the surprises are gone and it's time for a refill pack or a different game.

## Conclusion

If you only buy one, make it **Wavelength**. It's the closest thing on this list to a game with no ceiling — the team-guessing format gets funnier, not clunkier, as the crowd grows, and it works just as well at a game night with 8 people as it does at a reunion with 20. **Herd Mentality** is the cheaper backup for a truly chaotic crowd, and **Family Feud** is the one to reach for when you need zero setup and everyone already knows the format from TV.
