There are two blue boxes with a steam train on the front, they cost about the same, and the shop has both. That's the situation most people are in when they ask this question. The internet mostly answers it with "Europe is the better one" and no explanation of what that actually costs you.

We own Europe. Ryan bought it for Kaitlyn after we travelled Europe together, because half the cities we'd visited were printed on the board. It's the game we've handed to more beginners than anything else in the collection. So we're not neutral. But "which one do we own" and "which one should you buy" aren't the same question, and the honest answer depends almost entirely on who's going to be sitting at your table.

Both games are the same at heart: collect coloured train cards, spend matching sets to claim routes between cities, and try to connect the two cities printed on your secret Destination Tickets before someone else takes the track you needed. Europe adds three rules on top of that. Everything below is about whether you want those three rules.

## The Only Real Difference: Three Rules

Strip away the map art and Europe is the original game plus three additions. They're the entire decision, so here they are properly.

**Tunnels.** Some routes are marked as tunnels. You play your cards as normal, then flip the top 3 cards of the draw pile. For every flipped card matching the colour you played, you owe one more card of that colour. Can't or won't pay? You take everything back and your turn is gone.

That last part is what people underestimate. A failed tunnel doesn't cost you cards. It costs you a whole turn, in a game that only lasts twenty-odd turns. Attempting a long tunnel without spare cards in hand is how new players lose games they were winning.

**Ferries.** Routes crossing water show a locomotive symbol, and those spaces must be paid with actual locomotive cards. No substitutions. Locomotives are the wildcard in both games, and in Europe they're suddenly a required resource rather than a nice bonus. Taking a face-up locomotive costs your entire turn, which turns an obvious decision into a real one.

**Stations.** You get three. Placing one in a city lets you borrow a single route belonging to another player through that city, purely to finish your own tickets. The first costs 1 card, the second 2 matching, the third 3.

Stations are the addition that changes the *feel* of the game most, and it's the one nobody mentions. In the original, if someone takes the track you needed, you're finished. Reroute the long way round or eat the penalty. In Europe you have a parachute. Each unused station is worth 4 points at the end, so pulling it costs you something, but you're never truly locked out.

## Which Map Is Better?

The US map is cleaner. Wide open middle, obvious long horizontal runs, routes that mostly read at a glance. The Europe map is denser and more tangled, with the tunnels and ferries breaking up what would otherwise be straightforward connections.

Europe's board is also just nicer to look at, and cities are printed in their native language — Bruxelles, not Brussels; München, not Munich. Some players find that harder to scan. We think it adds a lot, but if you're playing with kids or anyone who'd rather not hunt for a city they know by a different name, it's worth knowing before the box is open.

There's one asymmetry in the original's favour that rarely gets said out loud: the US map has more long routes, and the coast-to-coast runs are the most satisfying thing either game offers. Europe spreads its rewards out more evenly.

## Destination Tickets and Replay Value

Europe wins this one clearly. It ships with **46 Destination Tickets** against the original's **30**.

That matters more than it sounds. Once a group has played the original a dozen times, people start recognising the tickets and knowing roughly where opponents are heading. Europe takes considerably longer to reach that point.

Even so, Europe's ticket pool has a flaw we've flagged before: there aren't many *long* destination cards, so experienced players get an edge over new ones by knowing where the valuable routes run. The Europa 1912 expansion adds more tickets and fixes this, and it's worth buying once you've played a few times. It's a separate purchase though, so factor it in if you're comparing total cost.

## Teaching a New Player

This is the argument for the original, and it's stronger than most comparisons admit.

The original teaches in about five minutes. Three actions: draw cards, claim a route, take more tickets. That's the whole game. You genuinely can sit someone down who has never played a modern board game and have them playing confidently in one turn.

Europe takes roughly twice as long to teach, because tunnels and stations both need explaining before they come up rather than when they come up. Stations in particular need a *why* as well as a *how*. It's not hard. It's just no longer the five-minute teach that made Ticket to Ride famous.

If the box is going to live with a family, come out at Christmas, and get taught to a different set of relatives every time, the original's simplicity is worth more than Europe's extra rules. If it's going to a regular game group who'll play it twenty times, the extra ten minutes is a rounding error.

## Player Count

Identical on paper. Both play 2 to 5, but they don't feel the same at the extremes.

At **2 players**, both are good and Europe is better. The board's density means there's still competition for space, whereas the US map at two players can feel a bit roomy and non-confrontational.

At **4 to 5 players**, both get genuinely tense as routes disappear. Here the original is the meaner game, because being blocked is permanent. Europe's stations take some of that edge off. Whether that's a plus depends on your group: if being cut off ruins someone's evening, Europe handles it better. If you like the knife-fight, the original delivers it.

## Price and What's in the Box

Both sit at a similar price and both fluctuate. Neither is a budget game, and neither is a bad deal for the hours you'll get.

Component quality is the same across both: the same chunky plastic trains, the same card stock, the same box insert that stops holding anything in place the moment you sleeve your cards. Europe includes the plastic train stations, which the original has no equivalent of.

One warning worth more than any of the above: there is a **15th Anniversary Edition** of Europe in a much larger box with train miniatures and 108 Destination Tickets. It's a lovely thing, it costs substantially more, and its components don't match the standard edition. If you're buying to play with someone who already owns standard Europe, or you're following a rulebook from the internet, get the standard box. Check what you're adding to the cart.

## So Which One?

**Buy the original Ticket to Ride if:** you're teaching this to people who don't play board games, it's a family or gift purchase, you want the shortest possible teach, or you want the cleanest, least fussy version of the game. It's the better first box for most households.

**Buy Ticket to Ride Europe if:** you already know you like this kind of game, you're buying for a group that plays regularly, you want more variety before the tickets start repeating, or you want the version where being blocked isn't fatal. It's the better game for people who'll play it a lot.

**If you've never played either and nobody at your table has:** get the original. The extra rules in Europe are much easier to absorb once the base game is muscle memory, and starting with Europe means learning tunnels and stations while you're still working out what a Destination Ticket is for.

They also don't cannibalise each other. Plenty of groups own both and rotate, and the maps are different enough that it doesn't feel like playing the same game twice. If you end up loving whichever one you pick, the other is a safe second purchase rather than a duplicate.

Our own answer, for what it's worth: Europe is the one that's stayed on our shelf and the one we hand to friends starting out with strategy games. But we bought it because of a holiday, not because we'd compared the boxes. The original is the one we'd have recommended if someone had asked us first.
