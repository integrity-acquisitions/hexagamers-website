We wrote a [full map of every Pandemic expansion and version](/every-pandemic-expansion-and-version/) and deliberately refused to rank anything in it. That was the right call for that article. It's a reference. You go there to find out what Hot Zone actually is, or whether In the Lab needs On the Brink.

But "what is this" and "which should I buy" are different questions, and people kept asking the second one.

So here's the ranking. One caveat first, and it genuinely matters: there is no single best Pandemic expansion, because the boxes with Pandemic on them aren't competing for the same slot. On the Brink and Pandemic Legacy: Season 1 are both excellent, and comparing them is like comparing a good set of tyres to a road trip. One improves the thing you own. The other is a twelve-session commitment you'll never play again.

So I've ranked within categories, then given an overall pick at the end for people who just want to be told.

## The Short Answer

- You own Pandemic and want more of it: [On the Brink](#1-on-the-brink)
- You want a campaign: [Pandemic Legacy: Season 1](#1-pandemic-legacy-season-1)
- You want something that feels new: [Pandemic: Iberia](#1-pandemic-iberia)
- You want Pandemic but shorter: [Hot Zone](#the-budget-and-travel-pick)
- Best single purchase overall: [Season 1](#the-overall-pick), if your group can commit. On the Brink if it can't.

## Ranking the True Expansions

These three need base Pandemic. If you don't own it, skip down to the standalones.

### 1. On the Brink

[![Pandemic: On the Brink](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-on-the-brink.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00CAG5LJ2/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Buy this first. Not because it's the most interesting design of the three (I'd argue In the Lab is), but because it's the one that makes every future purchase work.

Here's the thing most rankings get wrong. You'll see In the Lab put at number one fairly often, and on pure design merit that's arguable. But In the Lab needs On the Brink to use everything in the box. Recommending it as a first buy is quietly recommending a two-box purchase. On the Brink is the foundation, and it happens to also be the best value.

What you get: a fifth player slot, a big pile of new roles and event cards, and three optional challenges you can layer on independently.

**The roles are the underrated part.** Role variety is most of what keeps Pandemic fresh across dozens of plays. The base game's seven roles start feeling samey around play fifteen, and this roughly doubles them. That alone justifies the price.

Of the challenges, Virulent Strain is the standout. One disease turns nastier as the game goes on, with escalating penalties drawn each epidemic. It's the best difficulty dial in the whole family, and it costs about four sentences of rules explanation.

Bio-Terrorist is the divisive one. It hands a player a hidden traitor role and turns a co-op into a hidden-movement chase. Our group played it a handful of times and drifted back to Virulent Strain. It's slower and fiddlier than everything around it.

Get it if you own and still play base Pandemic. That's most people. The exception is if you've played five times and it isn't hitting, because more Pandemic won't fix that.

### 2. In the Lab

[![Pandemic: In the Lab](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-in-the-lab.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00CE3IA44/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The most interesting design of the three, ranked second on logistics rather than quality.

It fixes the one genuinely repetitive part of Pandemic. Normally curing is: collect five matching cards, stand in a research station, done. In the Lab replaces that with a separate lab board where you move samples through sequencing, characterising and testing. Curing stops being a formality and becomes a sub-game with its own decisions and its own ways to fail.

It also adds a proper solo mode and a team mode where two teams race on the same board. If you mostly play at two, or alone, that pushes this well up your list.

**The cost is real time.** The lab procedure adds length to every session, and the first play is slow while everyone learns the sequence. Pandemic stops being the 45-minute weeknight co-op and becomes a proper evening game. Some groups don't want that, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which group you're in.

This one's for people with a lot of plays behind them who find the cure step mechanical. If you're new to Pandemic, or you value it precisely because it's quick, leave it.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00CE3IA44/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

### 3. State of Emergency

[![Pandemic: State of Emergency](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-state-of-emergency.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00SDUQ3LE/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The one you buy when you've worn out the other two. Third place isn't an insult here.

Three more challenges. Superbug is the standout by a distance: a fifth disease that cannot be treated at all, only cured. That removes the safety valve Pandemic normally hands you. You stop firefighting and start racing, and it changes how the game feels in a way the other challenges don't quite manage.

Emergency Events shuffles nasty one-offs into the player deck, so drawing cards carries risk instead of being pure upside. Good addition, less dramatic.

Hinterlands adds animal-borne disease spreading in from rural regions, plus new board sections. It's the weakest thing in any of the three expansion boxes. Board sprawl for a modest payoff.

One genuinely nice thing: it doesn't require the other expansions, just the base game. Unusually accommodating for a third expansion.

**The real problem is accumulation.** By the time you own three expansions you're managing a lot of modules, and game night opens with a negotiation about which ones you're using. That's a tax the box doesn't warn you about.

Worth it once Virulent Strain has stopped scaring your group. Not worth it if you own fewer than two expansions already.

## Ranking the Legacy Campaigns

All three are standalone. No base game needed, and the numbering isn't a play order. What you do need is the same 2 to 4 people across roughly a dozen sessions, which is the actual barrier.

### 1. Pandemic Legacy: Season 1

[![Pandemic Legacy: Season 1](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-legacy-season-1.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00TQ5SEAI/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Still the one, eleven years on, and still the right entry point.

Season 1's advantage is that it's mechanically closest to base Pandemic. If you know the base game you can start the campaign in ten minutes. That sounds like a small thing and it isn't. It means the surprises land as story rather than as rules confusion. You're never sitting there wondering whether you misread something. You know how this game works, which is exactly what makes it land when it stops working that way.

Twelve in-game months, two attempts each. Characters gain scars. Cities get permanently damaged. Sealed boxes open based on what happens at your table.

We wrote play logs going through it, [January](/pandemic-legacy-s1-logs-january/) and [February](/pandemic-legacy-s1-logs-february/), both with spoiler warnings on them.

What it costs you is full game price for something you play once. The last couple of months also lean harder on story than on interesting decisions.

Red and blue editions are identical apart from cover art. Buy whichever is cheaper.

Any group that can reliably get the same people around a table a dozen times should own this. If your group composition changes week to week, don't. Campaigns die on attendance, not difficulty.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00TQ5SEAI/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

### 2. Pandemic Legacy: Season 0

[![Pandemic Legacy: Season 0](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-legacy-season-0.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08F73L5BM/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A Cold War prequel set in 1962, and the safest second campaign.

You're medical students recruited by the CIA to shut down a Soviet bioweapon programme. Instead of disease cubes you're clearing Soviet agents off the board, and each character carries alias identities that let them operate in different regions. There's a spycraft layer over the standard engine where you recruit contacts and build a dossier on the target.

It sits between the other two on how far it strays from base Pandemic. Further than Season 1, nowhere near as far as Season 2. That middle position is exactly why it's second: it's the one most likely to work for a group that liked Season 1 and wants more of that feeling without a full reinvention.

Season 0 has champions who rate it above Season 1, and I understand the argument. The alias system gives characters more identity than a role card usually does, and the whole thing is tightly built. It's second here because the story doesn't hit Season 1's highs, and some months feel like they're marking time before the next reveal.

Buy it as your second campaign, particularly if Season 2 sounded like too much. Don't start here if you've never done a legacy game.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08F73L5BM/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

### 3. Pandemic Legacy: Season 2

[![Pandemic Legacy: Season 2](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-legacy-season-2.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0716K46YP/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the ranking I'm least comfortable with, because Season 2 is the most ambitious design in the entire family.

Set 71 years after Season 1, on a world that didn't recover. The core inversion: you're not removing disease cubes, you're placing supply cubes. Cities run out and collapse, so you're running a logistics network out of a handful of havens instead of firefighting infection. The map starts mostly blank and you reveal regions as the campaign runs.

That blank map is the best single idea in any Pandemic product. Watching the board fill in over months, knowing your group chose where to expand, gives the campaign a shape Season 1 doesn't have.

So why third? The early months are punishing. The supply economy is unforgiving before you've built any infrastructure, and groups bounce off in the first few sessions. That happens often enough that I can't recommend it as anyone's first campaign. Push through and it opens up, but "push through" is a lot to ask of four people committing a dozen evenings.

It's also the furthest from Pandemic of the three. If your group wants Pandemic with a campaign wrapped around it, this is a different game wearing the name.

Right for groups who finished another season and want something new, and for anyone who likes a logistics puzzle. Wrong as a first campaign, and wrong if early losses discourage your table.

## Ranking the Standalones

Nothing here combines with base Pandemic. These are separate games running the same engine.

### 1. Pandemic: Iberia

[![Pandemic: Iberia](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-iberia.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09XZVFQNY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The best non-Legacy Pandemic there is, and the one I'd hand to someone who says they're bored of the base game.

1848, the Iberian Peninsula, four historical diseases. No air travel, because it's 1848. You move by rail and boat, and building railways is a core action. That single change does more than anything else in the family. In base Pandemic, geography is nearly free; you fly anywhere with the right card. Here the map genuinely constrains you, and the network you build in the first third of the game decides what's possible in the last third.

Water purification is the other addition. Cleaning a region's water slows infection there permanently, which creates a real tension between treating what's on the board now and doing preventive work that pays off in twenty minutes. Base Pandemic doesn't offer that choice.

There are also no cures in the modern sense. You research treatments that reduce spread rather than eliminating a disease, so eradication works differently than you'd expect.

For experienced players who want the engine to feel different without learning a new one. Not the one to teach a new player on, because it's harder than base Pandemic and less forgiving of a bad opening.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09XZVFQNY/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

### 2. Pandemic: Rising Tide

[![Pandemic: Rising Tide](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-rising-tide.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0778WSWL9/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The Netherlands, and the enemy is water. You're holding back floods with dikes and pumping stations.

The clever bit is geometric. Dikes are built on the borders between regions rather than in cities, so you're managing edges rather than spaces. Flooding spreads through those borders, and a breach cascades in a way that feels meaner than a disease outbreak, because you can see it coming several turns out and often can't stop it. That specific dread is unique in the family.

It's the most strategically demanding of the standalones, with long-term construction projects running alongside the immediate crisis management.

Second rather than first for one specific reason: no solo mode. Given how many people play Pandemic alone, that's a real gap, and it's the only thing separating this from Iberia for me. Setup also runs longer than base Pandemic.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0778WSWL9/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

### 3. Pandemic: Fall of Rome

[![Pandemic: Fall of Rome](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-fall-of-rome.webp)](https://www.zmangames.com/game/pandemic-fall-of-rome/)

395 AD, and the diseases are barbarian tribes marching along historical invasion routes toward Rome.

The most changed of the reskins, because it adds combat. You recruit legions, march them around, and fight with dice. You can also forge alliances with tribes instead of fighting them, which is the cure equivalent and usually the smarter play. The invasion routes give the map real directionality: threats come from the edges inward rather than popping up anywhere, so defensive positioning matters in a way it doesn't in the base game.

Dice are the sticking point. Base Pandemic has zero randomness once the cards are dealt. Here you can plan a defence perfectly and lose the roll. Whether that reads as exciting or infuriating is a genuine group-personality question, and I've seen both reactions at the same table.

It supports 1 to 5 players with an official solo mode.

One practical note that's part of why it's third: it's out of print on Amazon, and searches for it now land on other Pandemic boxes. Check [Z-Man's page](https://www.zmangames.com/game/pandemic-fall-of-rome/) for stockists, or look second-hand.

### 4. Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu

[![Pandemic: Reign of Cthulhu](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-reign-of-cthulhu.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01DN2BM9O/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Lovecraft country. Four towns instead of a world map, cultists instead of disease cubes, and shoggoths that spawn from unchecked cultist buildup.

The sanity mechanic is the best thematic touch in any Pandemic reskin. Investigators go insane and gain a permanent madness effect that changes how they play rather than just penalising them. Shoggoths advancing toward you create a visible, mounting threat that a disease cube never quite manages.

It's fourth because the smaller map means less strategic room, and it's swingy. A bad Evil Stirs sequence can lose you the game with less warning than an epidemic gives. Great atmosphere, thinner game underneath.

Good for groups who love the theme and want a shorter session, since it runs about 40 minutes. Wrong pick if you want depth over atmosphere.

<a href="https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01DN2BM9O/?tag=hexagamers-20" class="check-price-btn" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener">Check Price on Amazon</a>

### The Budget and Travel Pick

[![Pandemic: Hot Zone – North America](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/pandemic-hot-zone.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B084ZZDSZY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Pandemic: Hot Zone is outside the ranking because it's solving a different problem. Smaller map, three diseases, 30 minutes, much lower price.

This is the answer when base Pandemic never gets played because it's a 45-minute commitment with real setup. Hot Zone is out of the box and running in a couple of minutes. Nothing important was cut to get there, which is the impressive part.

North America came first. Europe adds Mutation challenge cards, and the two boxes are designed to be mixed if you want a bigger game.

The case against is narrow: if you already own and regularly play base Pandemic, this is a lighter version of a game you have. Three diseases means less to juggle and a less tense endgame.

## The Ones I'd Skip

Not bad games. Poor buys as Pandemic purchases, which is how most people find them.

**[Pandemic: The Cure](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00M4KGF5S/?tag=hexagamers-20)** is Pandemic as a dice game. Six regions, 30 minutes, setup under a minute. That last part is a genuine selling point. But dice mean the careful planning that defines Pandemic is largely gone, and you spend the game reacting to rolls rather than building a plan. If you want short Pandemic, Hot Zone does it better while still being Pandemic.

**[Pandemic: Rapid Response](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07PKRNMLH/?tag=hexagamers-20)** is real-time, with a two-minute timer and everyone acting at once. It's a decent 20-minute filler. The problem is that the dominant-player issue base Pandemic already has gets much worse when there's no time to discuss anything, and the loudest confident person just ends up running it. Buy it as a real-time filler, not as Pandemic.

**[Pandemic: Contagion](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00M4KGFE4/?tag=hexagamers-20)** puts you in charge of the diseases, competitively. The premise is funny and it lands as a change of pace with people who know the original, but it's an ordinary card game once the novelty wears off. It's also the only Pandemic-branded product that isn't cooperative at all.

**The licensed Pandemic System games** aren't skips exactly, but they're a different recommendation. [Wrath of the Lich King](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B099NNRP9F/?tag=hexagamers-20) is the best of the three and the one to start with, followed by [Star Wars: The Clone Wars](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BG9XZKTG/?tag=hexagamers-20) and [Fate of the Fellowship](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0DYF9D44F/?tag=hexagamers-20). Buy these if you love the licence. All three run longer than base Pandemic and layer quests and dice combat on top, so "Pandemic System" means the skeleton is familiar and not much else is.

## The Overall Pick

If you're buying exactly one thing with Pandemic on the box, and your group can commit to a dozen sessions: Pandemic Legacy: Season 1. It's the reason people still talk about this game in 2026, and no expansion produces the moments a legacy campaign does.

If that commitment isn't realistic, and for most groups it honestly isn't, On the Brink is the pick. It's cheap, it improves a game you already own, and it asks nothing of your calendar.

And if you already own base Pandemic but never reach for it, the honest recommendation isn't an expansion at all. It's Iberia, which is different enough to feel like a new game. Or nothing. More of a game you've cooled on rarely reheats it.
