## Overview

You have a handful of woodland critters and a patch of empty valley. Send them out to gather twigs, resin, pebbles, and berries, then spend that haul to play cards into your city: critters and constructions that feed each other points and resources. You get fifteen card slots and four seasons to fill them. Everyone is pulling from the same pool of cards in the middle of the table, so the city you planned is rarely the city you end up with.

## Ryan's Review

### Likes

- The card combos. Chaining a construction into the critter that occupies it, for free, never stops being satisfying
- Table presence that stops conversations, and the tree isn't even the best part of the art
- Seasons give the game a real arc instead of just counting down rounds
- Genuine interaction for an engine builder. The Meadow is shared, and people take what you wanted
- Fifteen city slots forces hard cuts; you can't just play everything you can afford

### Dislikes

- The teach is long. Budget a rules video before your first session rather than learning at the table
- The tree is a cardboard assembly that does almost nothing mechanically
- Solo mode is functional but nowhere near Wingspan's Automa
- It's expensive, and the price has crept up since release
- Four players stretches the downtime more than the box suggests

## Getting It to the Table

I came at Everdell backwards. We'd already put a lot of hours into Wingspan, and I'd written up the comparison between the two before I'd properly sat down with Everdell across a full evening — which is a slightly uncomfortable thing to admit. My expectation was "Wingspan with squirrels." Prettier, roughly the same shape of game.

That was wrong in a specific way. Wingspan is a game you can teach in ten minutes because the player board explains itself. Everdell isn't. The first session had three of us stopping to re-read card text, and about forty minutes in someone asked the question that fixes the game: wait, if I build the construction, do I get the matching critter free? Yes. That's the engine. Once that lands, the game stops feeling fiddly.

We nearly didn't get there. The first half of that first play felt like admin.

## Thoughts

**The core loop is smaller than it looks.** On your turn you do one of three things: place a worker, play a card, or move to the next season. That's it. All the apparent complexity is in the cards, not the rules, which is why the game plays much faster than the teach implies. By the second session nobody was reaching for the rulebook.

**Construction-to-critter is where the game lives.** Every construction in your city lets you play its matching critter for free. Building a Farm and then dropping the Husband on top of it costs you a fraction of what those two cards should cost. Strung together, these pairs turn a mid-game turn into four or five actions. The turn where your city finally starts paying you back is the best turn in the game.

**Seasons are the pacing mechanism, and they're clever.** You move through spring, summer, autumn, and winter at your own pace, and moving on gets you more workers back. Going early means more actions but a weaker board. Going late means you squeezed the season dry but everyone else is a step ahead. Nobody at our table has agreed on the right moment to move, which is usually a good sign.

**The Meadow makes it competitive in a way Wingspan isn't.** Eight cards sit face-up in the middle, shared by everyone. You watch someone's city and you can see what they need. Taking a card you don't especially want, purely because the person to your left has been building toward it all game, is a legitimate and slightly mean play. Wingspan almost never gives you that.

**Fifteen slots is the real constraint.** Not resources. Resources come. Space doesn't. Late game you'll hold a card that's worth good points and have nowhere to put it, and the decision about what your city *isn't* going to be is more interesting than most of the decisions about what it is.

**On the components.** The art is the reason this game sells, and it deserves the reputation. Andrew Bosley's illustrations carry it. The resources are chunky and pleasant. The tree, though: it's a cardboard assembly that holds the Meadow cards up, and it does nothing you couldn't do by laying eight cards on the table. It looks incredible and it's functionally decoration. Worth knowing before you pay for it.

**Price is the honest sticking point.** This is a premium-priced box and it has drifted upward. You're paying for the art and the insert as much as the design. If you already own Wingspan and want one gorgeous nature-themed engine builder, the case for a second one is weaker than the internet suggests — they're closer than people admit. If you own neither and want the deeper of the two, this is the deeper of the two.

**Player count matters here.** Two is tight and quick. Three is the sweet spot. Four is where turns start to stack up, and because the Meadow keeps shifting between your turns, you can't plan much during the wait — you just wait. It caps at four for a reason.

## Conclusion

Everdell is a worker placement game with a card engine sitting on top, and the moment those two halves start feeding each other is genuinely great. It asks more of you than Wingspan does (longer teach, more interaction, more painful cuts) and rewards the group willing to spend a second session on it. If you want the prettiest game on the shelf that still has real decisions underneath, buy it. If you want something you can teach to new players in ten minutes, buy Wingspan instead and come back to this one later.
