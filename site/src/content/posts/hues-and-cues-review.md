## Overview

One player draws a card, picks a colour, and gives a one-word clue. Everyone else drops a token on a grid of 480 coloured squares, trying to land on the exact one. Then a second clue, a second token, and a 3x3 frame drops onto the board to show how close everyone got. That's the whole game, and it works because "banana" means something slightly different to every person at the table.

## Ryan's Review

### Likes

- Basically zero setup — unfold the board, hand out tokens, start playing
- Scoring is simple enough that nobody needs the rulebook after round one
- Moves quickly when people are paying attention
- Goes to ten players, which almost nothing else in our collection does
- Anyone can play it, kids included, with no rules explanation longer than a minute

### Dislikes

- Insider clues wreck a round: if two people at the table share a reference nobody else does, everyone else is guessing blind
- Easy to coast by copying where other people placed their tokens
- Scores stay so tight that the winner rarely feels earned
- With ten players, waiting for your turn gets slow
- The clue-giver think time is the only place the game drags
- Not playable if someone at the table is colourblind

## Getting It to the Table

We played this over a weekend with a decent-sized group, and my first reaction was that it looked too simple to hold anyone's attention for a full game. A grid of colours and one-word clues. That's it?

It held up better than I expected. Not because the guessing is deep, but because everybody has an opinion about what colour "seafoam" is, and everybody is a little bit wrong. The gap between what you meant and what the table heard is where the fun lives.

## Thoughts

**The two-clue structure does more work than it looks like.** You give one word, everyone places a token going clockwise. Then a second clue of up to two words, and the direction flips to counterclockwise so the person who had to guess first now gets to guess last. That reversal matters more than I gave it credit for. Going first on an unhelpful one-word clue is genuinely rough, and getting the last look on the second pass evens it out.

![A player lowering the 3x3 scoring frame onto the Hues and Cues board](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-reviews/hues-and-cues-gameplay-2.webp "The 1s and 2s are printed right on the frame, so nobody has to memorise the scoring")

**Scoring is where the game earns its accessibility.** Frame lands on the board with the target in the middle. Exact square is 3 points, inside the frame is 2, touching the outside edge of the frame is 1, and you score for each of your two tokens separately. The clue giver picks up a point for every token that ended up inside their frame, which is the piece that makes bad clues actually cost you something. The numbers are printed directly on the frame, so nobody at our table needed that explained twice.

**Insider clues are the real problem, and the game has no defence against them.** We had a few rounds where somebody gave a clue that a couple of people instantly understood and the rest of us had nothing. A shared reference, an inside joke, something from a show only half the table watched. The clue giver isn't cheating — they genuinely think it's a good clue. The workaround we landed on was ugly but effective: wait for the person who obviously knows to place their token, then cluster near them. It salvages the round. It also means half the table is playing follow-the-leader instead of guessing.

**Which points at the bigger issue.** Copying is always available. If a clue means nothing to you, you don't have to think — you just find the general neighbourhood everyone else is heading toward and drop your token there. You'll pick up a point or two for proximity and you'll never be far behind. That's exactly why the scores stayed tight for us the entire game, and I have mixed feelings about it. Nobody gets blown out, which is good for a group with kids or newcomers. But a close finish that came from everyone hedging is a different thing than a close finish that came from everyone playing well.

**The board doesn't always match the card, and that's on purpose.** A couple of people in our group swore the square they were looking for on the board looked different from the swatch on the card. It's not a printing issue. Surrounding colours change how you read a hue, so a square sitting next to a bright neighbour reads differently than the isolated swatch in the clue giver's hand. Worth knowing before you accuse anyone of giving a bad clue.

![A close-up along the surface of the Hues and Cues board showing how gradually neighbouring colours shift](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-reviews/hues-and-cues-gameplay-3.webp "Neighbouring squares shift by almost nothing, which is exactly the problem")

**Ten players is a real number here, with a real cost.** Almost nothing in our collection scales that high, and being able to hand out tokens to a full room is genuinely useful. But every extra player is another token placement to sit through, twice per round. At the top end you spend a lot of the game watching. More people also makes hitting the exact centre square harder, since the good squares get crowded and the race for the perfect guess gets tighter.

**Setup is the quiet best feature.** Board out, tokens distributed, deal with the first clue giver. There's no shuffling ritual, no player boards, no fiddly bits to sort. When you're trying to get a game going and half the group is still finding chairs, that matters. This is the kind of box you grab when you have twenty-five minutes and no patience for a teach.

**On value:** it sits at a normal party game price and you get a big board, a lot of tokens, and a stack of cards. Fine deal for what it is. Just go in knowing it's a filler, not a centrepiece.

## Conclusion

Hues and Cues does one thing very well: it gets a large, mixed group playing something within two minutes of opening the box, and it keeps everyone in it because the scores never run away. It's for big gatherings, family tables, and any night where you need a game that an eight-year-old and a grandparent can both play without a rules lecture. I'd happily bring it out with a few different groups. I also suspect I'd be done with it somewhere around the tenth play — the guessing doesn't get deeper, it just gets familiar. We'll see if that holds.

One caveat that isn't the game's fault but is worth saying out loud: if anyone at your table is colourblind, this one is off the menu. Sorry, Tito.
