Small box, a handful of cards, fifteen minutes, costs about the same as a pizza. Coup and Love Letter get recommended in the same breath so often that people assume they're the same kind of game. We own both, and I'd put money on them landing very differently at your table.

Coup is one of my favourite games full stop. I watched one round at a games night and knew I needed to play it. Love Letter is the one Kaitlyn reviewed for us, and she liked it with reservations: great filler, not much depth. Both of those takes still hold. What's changed since we wrote them is the Love Letter box itself, and that matters for anyone deciding today.

**Buy Coup if** your group likes lying to each other's faces, you usually have four to six players, and everyone's a teenager or older. **Buy Love Letter if** you mostly play with two or three, you're buying for families or younger players, or you'd rather work out what someone's holding than bluff about it.

## The Core Loop, Side by Side

In Coup you hold two face-down character cards, your two lives. On your turn you take one action. Some are open to anyone: take one coin, take two, or pay seven to Coup someone and knock out one of their cards. The good actions belong to characters. The Duke takes three coins, the Assassin kills a card for three, and the Captain steals two. And here's the catch: **you can claim any character, whether you hold it or not.** Anyone can challenge you. Whoever's wrong loses a card. Lose both and you're out.

In Love Letter you hold one card. On your turn you draw a second, then play one of the two and do what it says. The Guard lets you guess another player's card, and if you're right they're out of the round. The Baron makes you compare hands, and the lower card goes out. The Handmaid protects you for a turn. The Princess is the highest card, and if you ever have to discard her, you're out of the round. The last player standing wins the round, or the highest card if the deck runs out. Win enough rounds and you win the game.

Same size box, same length. Completely different skill being tested.

## Bluffing vs Deduction

This is the part that decides it.

**Coup is a lying game.** You claim a card, and the only question is whether anyone has the nerve to call you on it. It's loud, and it's personal. We once had all six players claim Duke at the same time, and that's when the accusations start flying. The fun comes from getting away with something, or from catching someone who thought they had.

**Love Letter is a reading game.** Every card you play is face up and has to do what it says. You're rarely lying outright. You're tracking what's been played, working out what's left, and guessing what someone kept from the way they played. It's quieter. A good Love Letter turn feels like solving a small puzzle.

There is one bluff in Love Letter, and it's a lovely detail. If you're holding the Countess with the King or the Prince, you *must* discard the Countess. But you're also allowed to discard her any time you like. So when someone plays the Countess, everyone at the table has the same thought: are they stuck with a King, or do they just want us to think so? It's about as close as Love Letter gets to Coup's claim-and-challenge.

If your group's idea of fun is "I'm definitely the Duke" with a straight face, get Coup. If you'd rather watch someone discard the Countess and try to work out why, get Love Letter.

## Player Count (The Love Letter Box Changed)

Our Love Letter review lists player count as its biggest limit, and at the time that was fair. The original 16-card edition only played two to four, and our regular group is six, so it sat on the shelf a lot.

**The current edition fixes that.** Z-Man's version, which is what you get on Amazon today, adds two new characters, the Spy and the Chancellor. That takes the deck to 21 cards and the player count to **2–6**. The 2025 refresh keeps those rules and changes the packaging: a nicer display box with a velvet bag and no single-use plastic. So the "only four players" complaint is gone.

With both games now at two to six, the question is where each one is best.

**Coup is best at four to six.** More players means more claims flying around and more people who might call you out. At two, you're just reading one opponent, and it loses most of what makes it fun. I wouldn't buy Coup for two.

**Love Letter is excellent at two and three**, and still fine at five or six, where the rounds get chaotic in a good way. It's one of the better two-player fillers you can buy for this price.

## Player Elimination

Both games knock people out, and how long you sit out is different.

**In Coup you're out for the rest of the game.** Games are short, so it's usually a few minutes. But in a six-player game, the first person eliminated can end up watching for a while. The upside is that nobody minds much, because they're already planning revenge for the next game.

**In Love Letter you're only out for the rest of the round**, and rounds take a few minutes at most. Then everyone's back in with a fresh card. It's a much gentler kind of elimination, which is one reason it works better with kids.

## Who You're Playing With

**Families and younger players: Love Letter.** It's rated 10+, the cards tell you exactly what to do, and nobody has to lie convincingly to do well. The Guard's "I guess you're holding the Priest" turns into a game kids love on its own.

**Teens and adults who enjoy some chaos: Coup.** It's rated 13+, and that fits. The game is built around lying to people you know and punishing the people you catch. Sometimes you have to Coup your mum even though she made you dinner.

**Quiet players** can be a problem in Coup. If nobody bluffs and nobody challenges, the game goes flat. Our house tip is to call someone out in the first couple of rounds, even on a hunch, to loosen everyone up. Love Letter doesn't have that problem. The cards push the action forward whether anyone's feeling brave or not.

## Where Each One Falls Short

**Coup's Captain gets blocked too easily.** Six of the fifteen cards can block a steal, so stealing often goes nowhere. It's a small balance quibble and doesn't stop us playing.

**Love Letter can drift into guessing.** Early in a round, with only a few cards played, a Guard guess is close to a coin flip. The deduction sharpens as more cards come out, but as Kaitlyn's review points out, by the time the real decisions show up the round is usually almost done.

## Portability and Price

Both are about the size of a deck of cards and cost about the same, usually under $20. We took Coup to Mexico without thinking twice. Love Letter's current box is built for display, but the velvet bag inside holds the whole game and slips into a jacket pocket.

At this price, you don't have to choose too carefully. Neither is a risky buy.

## Which One Should You Buy?

**Buy Coup if:** your group is four to six people who enjoy bluffing, calling each other out and the arguments afterwards. It's the one with more to it, and the one I'd play five times in a row.

**Buy Love Letter if:** you mostly play in twos and threes, you're buying for a family with kids around ten, or your group prefers working things out to lying about them. It's also the easier game to teach someone who's never played a modern card game.

**Buy both if:** you can. They cost about the same as one bigger game, and they don't overlap much. Love Letter is for two people after dinner or getting a slow crowd warmed up. Coup is for when the whole group is there and ready to get loud.
