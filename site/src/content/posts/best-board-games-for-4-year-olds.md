Four is a strange, brilliant age for board games. A four-year-old can take a turn, match a colour, and count a small pile of things. What they can't do is read, wait five minutes for their next go, or reliably survive losing. That narrow window is what we aimed this list at.

If you're shopping for preschoolers generally rather than for a specific four-year-old, this is the list you want. Four is the middle of the preschool range and the point where most of these games land best, so everything below works for a capable three-year-old or an early five-year-old too.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](#best-board-games-for-4-year-olds-comparison-table)

Our criteria were simple. No reading, because most four-year-olds aren't there yet. Fifteen or twenty minutes, because attention runs out well before a long game does. Pieces chunky enough to survive being grabbed and dropped. And the one people forget: the game has to be tolerable for the adult sitting across the table, because you are going to play it eleven times in a row.

We leaned cooperative for a good chunk of the list. At four, losing to your older sibling is a genuinely big deal, and a game where everyone plays against the board sidesteps that entirely. But we didn't go all-co-op. Four is also the age where kids start to want to actually win something, and a couple of gentle competitive games here scratch that itch without creating a meltdown.

If you've got a younger one coming up behind, our [best board games for 3 year olds](/best-board-games-3-year-olds-reviews/) list steps down a notch. Going the other way, [best board games for kindergarten](/best-board-games-for-kindergarten/) covers the five-and-six crowd, and several games below carry straight over. Between those two, this preschool list is the one most parents actually need.

## 1. Hoot Owl Hoot! ([Full Review Here](/hoot-owl-hoot-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/hoot-owl-hoot.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004HVKAAI/?tag=hexagamers-20)

If you buy one game off this list, buy this one. Everyone works together to fly the owls back to the nest before the sun comes up. Play a coloured card, move any owl to the next matching space, and hope the sun card stays buried.

The reason it works so well at four specifically is that it's cooperative *and* the decision is real. You choose which owl to move, and moving the wrong one costs you. A four-year-old can absolutely make that call, and when the group gets all six owls home there's genuine celebration.

It also has two setups, a short one for the youngest players and a longer one as they grow into it. That's the difference between a game you play for four months and one you play for two years.

The only knock is that an adult can quietly steer the whole thing without anyone noticing. Feature or bug, depending on your household.

## 2. Dragon's Breath

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/dragons-breath.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B078Y3GBN4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A tower of coloured stones sits in the middle of the table, trapped in rings of ice. Each round one player pulls off the top ring and everyone grabs the sparkling stones that tumble out. The catch is that before the pull, each kid has to pick the colour they're betting on.

This is the one on the list adults quietly enjoy most. You pick your colour, then watch the ice ring come off, and the wait is the same small thrill whether you're four or forty. Kids shriek when their colour cascades out. It won the Kinderspiel des Jahres in 2018, which is about as close to an objective stamp of approval as this category gets.

There's counting at the end, no reading anywhere, and the acrylic stones feel great in small hands. One caveat: those stones are a choking risk if you've also got a toddler roaming the house.

It's competitive, but so gently that a losing four-year-old usually doesn't clock it.

## 3. My First Orchard

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/my-first-orchard.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004BW8TYC/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The whole table works together to pick all the fruit before the raven reaches the orchard. Roll the colour die, take the matching fruit off the tree. Roll the raven and he moves one step closer.

It's the simplest game on the list, and for a lot of four-year-olds that's exactly the point. A kid can run the whole thing themselves with no adult reading or arbitrating anything. The wooden fruit is chunky and satisfying, there's no counting past a handful, and nobody loses to another person.

It's aimed a little younger than four, so a sharp four-year-old may outgrow it inside a year. We'd still put it here because it's often the first game the whole family finishes together with nobody crying, and that's worth the shelf space on its own.

Take the raven seriously. Kids get properly invested in beating him.

## 4. Monza

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/monza.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0FN47LKSK/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Six coloured dice, one wooden car, and a racetrack. Roll all six, then move your car forward along any sequence of squares whose colours match the dice you rolled. Longest chain wins the turn.

Monza is the sneakiest game here, because it looks like pure luck and it isn't. Finding the best path through your six dice is real spatial planning, and four-year-olds get visibly better at it over a few weeks. Watching that improve is lovely.

It handles up to six players in under fifteen minutes, which makes it the one on this list that actually works at a birthday party. The wooden cars are sturdy and the board is bright without being frantic.

The box says 5+ and we think that's a touch conservative. A four-year-old who can match colours will manage, though they'll need a nudge on spotting the longer routes for the first few plays.

## 5. The Sneaky Snacky Squirrel

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/sneaky-snacky-squirrel.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00486ZVC4/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Spin the spinner, then use a squirrel-shaped squeezer to pick up the matching acorn and drop it into your log. First to fill all five holes wins.

The squeezer is the entire reason this game is on the list. Working it is a real fine-motor task, and four is right about when a kid can manage it on their own, which makes them feel enormously capable. It's the sort of thing they keep doing after the game has technically ended.

There's a "steal an acorn" space on the spinner that brings in a little light meanness. That's actually useful. It's a low-stakes place for a four-year-old to learn how that feels from both sides, and the game is short enough that a loss is five minutes from a rematch.

The spinner is flimsier than the rest of the components, and past operating the tongs it's pure luck. Fine at this age. It won't hold a six-year-old.

## 6. My First Castle Panic

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/my-first-castle-panic.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07KMLHPMK/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Monsters march toward your castle and the players work together to stop them by matching the colour and shape on their cards. It's the toddler-scaled version of Castle Panic, and it's a surprisingly faithful shrink-down.

What makes it work at four is that it teaches a real board game idea, that a threat you ignore gets worse, with no reading or arithmetic involved. Kids start to grasp that the closest monster matters more than the interesting-looking one at the back. That's actual strategy in a game a preschooler can run.

The monster art has personality without being scary, and because it's cooperative a bad round is the group's problem rather than one kid's fault. It scales up to a full family without dragging.

At around twenty minutes it's the longest game here, so save it for a good day rather than the end of a long one.

## 7. Outfoxed!

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/outfoxed.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B00UB7P0XY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A fox has made off with the pot pie, and the players work together to gather clues and identify the culprit before it escapes. A plastic clue decoder reveals whether a suspect is innocent, and kids love working it.

This is deduction for preschoolers, and it's the real thing: you eliminate suspects who don't match the evidence. A four-year-old won't run it unaided, but they follow it fine with an adult narrating, and the moment they cross off the last wrong fox and point at the guilty one is a proper thinking win.

The decoder is the piece that sells the whole game. It gives the reveal a bit of theatre that a card flip never would.

It sits at the older end of what a four-year-old handles, so it suits the back half of the year and then keeps going long afterwards. If your kid is newly four, start elsewhere on this list and come back to it in six months.

## 8. Snug as a Bug in a Rug

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/snug-as-a-bug-in-a-rug.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B006S9L0OC/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Everyone works together to tuck all the bugs under the rug before three stink bugs turn up. The bugs are sorted by colour, shape, or number depending on which version you're playing, so the game quietly grows with the kid.

That adjustable difficulty is why it earns a spot. Play the colour version with a fresh four-year-old and the number version a year later, out of the same box. Not many games in this category stretch that far.

The bug tokens are thick cardboard and pleasant to handle, and hiding them under the felt rug is a satisfying physical action rather than an abstract one. It's cooperative throughout, so nobody loses on their own.

Honestly, it's the least interesting game here for an adult. You're along to help rather than to play. But it teaches sorting and turn-taking without pressure, which is the job it was built for.

## 9. Sequence for Kids

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/sequence-for-kids.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0072ZN4EA/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Play an animal card from your hand, put your chip on a matching animal on the board, and try to get four in a row. Unicorn cards let you place anywhere, dragon cards remove someone else's chip.

This is the most strategic game on the list, and the "no reading required" label on the box is accurate. It's all animal pictures. Four-year-olds start out just matching animals, then somewhere around the tenth play they notice they can block someone. Watching that switch flip is the whole appeal.

The dragon card is a proper introduction to direct competition and it stings a little. Worth knowing before you buy if your kid is sensitive about that sort of thing. It's the most cutthroat game here, which is admittedly a low bar.

Cheap, compact, and still interesting well past the point where the rest of this list has been outgrown.

## 10. Count Your Chickens ([Full Review Here](/count-your-chickens-review/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/count-your-chickens.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B004HVKAAS/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Mother Hen moves around the board and the group collects her chicks and puts them back in the coop. Spin, move to the next matching space, and count the chicks in that square as you collect them.

It's counting practice dressed as a game and it's honest about that. Everyone plays as one team against the board, so there's no losing, and the counting is the actual mechanic rather than a bolt-on. For a four-year-old working on numbers up to ten, it's about as painless as practice gets.

The pieces are pleasant and the fox space adds a little jeopardy without threatening anyone. Fifteen minutes, start to finish.

As a game it's the thinnest one here, and no adult will be entertained. But if your four-year-old is mid-way through learning to count, it earns a year on the shelf and then goes to a younger cousin.

## What Makes a Good Preschool Board Game?

If you're picking something not on this list, four things separate a preschool game that gets played from one that gets shelved.

It has to work without reading. Anything that needs a card read aloud puts an adult in the loop permanently, and the game stops being something the kid can own.

It has to finish in about fifteen minutes. Preschoolers don't quit games because they're bad, they quit because they're long.

The pieces have to be sturdy and big enough to handle. Small cards get bent, tiny pieces get lost, and anything under about an inch is a choking risk for a younger sibling.

And it should give a four-year-old something to actually decide. The difference between Candy Land and Hoot Owl Hoot! is that one draws a card and moves you, and the other asks you which owl to move. Preschoolers notice that difference far more than parents expect.

## Conclusion

If you want one game, get Hoot Owl Hoot!. It's cooperative, there's a real decision in it, and the two difficulty setups mean it lasts. If you want one the adults will look forward to as well, make it Dragon's Breath.

The honest advice at age four is to own two or three of these rather than one. A short co-op for tired evenings, something with a physical hook like the squirrel tongs or the ice tower, and one game like Sequence for Kids that they'll grow into instead of out of.
