We're using "adventure" the way the hobby actually means it, not the way the toy aisle does. This isn't a list of games with a fantasy sticker slapped on the box. These are games where you're actually going somewhere — sailing into uncharted water, crawling through a dungeon your party has never seen before, stranded on an island with nothing but your wits. The board changes as you play. Your character changes as you play. That's the line we drew.

[Click here to jump to the comparison table...](#best-adventure-board-games-comparison-table)

That means we left out pure dungeon-crawlers with no larger arc and anything where "adventure" is just a marketing word for "has a dragon on the box." What made the cut are games built around discovery — new tiles, new story beats, new consequences that carry forward. Some are one-and-done sessions. A couple are campaigns that will eat your next six months. We tried to cover both.

If you already know you want something tighter and more tactical — tile-by-tile rooms, combat as the main loop — check our [Best Dungeon Crawl Board Games](/best-dungeon-crawl-board-games/) list instead. And if you want the pen-and-paper side of this same itch, see [Best Tabletop RPG Games](/best-tabletop-rpg-games/).

## 1. Robinson Crusoe: Adventures on the Cursed Island

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/robinson-crusoe.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01HDYFCJO/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the adventure game that punishes you for treating it like a normal board game. You and up to three friends wash ashore with nothing, and the game genuinely does not care whether you figure it out. Hunger, weather, and wild animals are all coming for you from turn one, and there's no rulebook hand-holding once you're in it.

The appeal is the same reason people watch survival shows. You're not managing an abstract resource track, you're deciding whether to build shelter tonight or push for food and risk the cold. Every choice feels like it could be the one that sinks the run. Win rates are famously low, and that's the point.

It earned its spot at the top because nothing else on this list nails "you are actually stranded" this hard. It's brutal, it's slow to teach, and it will lose games for you that feel unfair. Go in knowing that, and it's one of the most memorable co-op experiences in the hobby.

## 2. Sleeping Gods

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/sleeping-gods.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B084GWHLGK/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Your ship crashes into an unmapped sea, and from there the game hands you an atlas, a stack of story books, and almost total freedom about where to sail next. There's no rail. You choose a direction, flip to the matching entry, and the world responds.

What separates Sleeping Gods from a lot of "story in a box" games is how much of it is systemic rather than scripted. Combat, trading, and crew management all sit underneath the narrative layer, so you're not just reading text and rolling a die — you're actually managing a ship and a crew that can succeed or fail on their own terms. Losing a fight has real consequences that ripple into later chapters.

It's a big investment of both money and shelf space, and the campaign runs long if you play it start to finish. But if the idea of an open-world board game appeals to you at all, this is the one enthusiasts point to first.

## 3. Gloomhaven ([Is It Worth It?](/is-gloomhaven-worth-it/))

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/Gloomhaven_tvaycr.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0H4HZ1JCJ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

We already wrote a whole piece on whether this one's worth buying, so we won't repeat all of it here. Short version: it's the game that made "legacy dungeon crawler with a real campaign arc" a mainstream idea, and the scale of it is still hard to overstate. Dozens of scenarios, a card-based combat system that replaces dice with hand management, and a world that actually remembers what your party did to it.

What earns it a place on an adventure list specifically, rather than a pure dungeon-crawl list, is the overworld layer. You're choosing which town to travel to, which faction to help, which side quests to chase — the map opens up as you go, not just the dungeons inside it.

It's a serious commitment in time, table space, and setup effort. This isn't a warm-up game. But if your group wants one thing to sink a year into, this is still the benchmark everyone else gets compared to.

## 4. The Quest for El Dorado

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/quest-for-el-dorado.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0BPML8599/?tag=hexagamers-20)

Reiner Knizia built this one as deck-building's answer to a jungle race, and it's the easiest entry point on this whole list. You're buying cards to build a faster, more capable deck, then playing them to move your pawn across a hand-built jungle map toward the golden city. First one there wins.

The trick is that the terrain matters as much as your deck. A path full of swamp tiles needs different cards than a path full of mountains, so you're constantly weighing "build for speed" against "build for whatever's actually in front of me." It plays in under an hour and the rules teach in about five minutes.

This is the one we'd hand to someone who says they like the idea of adventure games but gets intimidated by 100-page rulebooks. It's light, it's fast, and it still has real decisions in it.

## 5. Mice and Mystics

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/mice-and-mystics.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B009TNOF9G/?tag=hexagamers-20)

A kingdom falls, and the royal family gets turned into mice as a last-ditch escape. From there it plays like a proper dungeon crawl — tile-by-tile exploration, dice-based combat, real stakes — but with a chapter-book story sitting on top that a parent can actually read aloud.

The mouse-scale worldbuilding is what makes this one stick. You're hiding under teacups from a house cat that's suddenly the size of a monster, and the miniatures sell that scale shift well. It's genuinely tense for a game that's approachable enough for a strong 7-year-old to play alongside adults.

We'd put this at the top of the "family adventure game" bracket specifically. It's not watered down, it's just told through a lens that works for a wider age range than most crawlers manage.

## 6. Stuffed Fables

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/stuffed-fables.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0774X4J44/?tag=hexagamers-20)

From the same studio as Mice and Mystics, but built for a younger table. You're stuffed animals defending a child from nightmares, and the whole thing unfolds out of a genuine storybook — the box itself is designed to look like one on the shelf.

What's smart about the design is that it teaches itself. Each page of the story introduces the next rule right when you need it, so there's no separate rulebook to read before you start. A parent can open the box and be playing within a few minutes, picking up mechanics one page at a time alongside the kids.

It's shorter and gentler than most of this list, and that's exactly the point. If you want your youngest players to feel like they're on a real adventure without the difficulty spikes of Robinson Crusoe or Gloomhaven, this is the pick.

## 7. HeroQuest

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/heroquest.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B08Q4J1XHZ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

The 2021 Hasbro relaunch of the game that got a lot of people into this hobby in the first place. One player runs the dungeon as the Game Master, everyone else plays a hero archetype — Barbarian, Wizard, Dwarf, Elf — and you crawl through a mission book of increasingly nasty scenarios.

It's simpler than most of what's on this list mechanically, and that's a feature, not a gap. Combat is a handful of dice, movement is straightforward, and the GM role is light enough that a first-timer can run it without much prep. What it has over a lot of lighter dungeon crawlers is the expansion ecosystem — new hero packs and quest packs keep adding actual content rather than just more minis.

Think of this as the on-ramp for the rest of this list. Finish a HeroQuest campaign and want more, and that's exactly when Gloomhaven or Tainted Grail starts to make sense.

## 8. Tainted Grail: The Fall of Avalon

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/tainted-grail.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B09B4GPYKN/?tag=hexagamers-20)

This is the one for a group that wants the choices to actually matter. Tainted Grail leans hard on Arthurian and Celtic myth gone wrong, and it plays closer to a tabletop RPG than a board game — a huge chapter book of numbered entries, real consequences for the decisions you make, and a world state that shifts permanently based on what your party did three sessions ago.

The exploration loop is the hook. You're not following a set path through the campaign; you're deciding which parts of Avalon to investigate, which factions to trust, and living with whatever that choice costs you later. It rewards note-taking and punishes trying to "win" instead of just playing your character.

It's a bigger box and a bigger price tag than most of this list, and the campaign length is a serious commitment. But if your group has burned through Gloomhaven and wants the story side turned up even further, this is where to go next.

## 9. Tales of the Arabian Nights

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/tales-of-the-arabian-nights-v2.webp)](https://www.playtozgames.com/game/tales-of-the-arabian-nights-40th-anniversary-edition/)

The choose-your-own-adventure book, but as a board game. You move around a map of the ancient Middle East collecting Story and Destiny points, and almost every space you land on triggers a paragraph out of a massive bound book — react to it, and the game tells you what happens next.

The scale of the writing is what makes this one worth calling out. There are literally thousands of possible story branches in the book, and encounters chain into each other in ways that genuinely surprise you, even after a dozen plays. One bad decision with a genie can spiral into three more encounters you never saw coming.

It's less strategic than everything else on this list — this is a game about generating stories at the table, not optimizing a path to victory. If your group likes reading a passage aloud and laughing at how badly it went, this belongs on the shelf.

The version currently in print is the 40th Anniversary Edition from Play to Z, which isn't reliably stocked on Amazon yet — the classic Z-Man printing shows up secondhand, but for a new copy your best bet is ordering directly.

## Conclusion

If you want one adventure game and nothing else, start with HeroQuest — it's the easiest to teach and the easiest to build a whole game night around. If your group wants to go all-in on something with real weight, Gloomhaven and Tainted Grail are both waiting for that level of commitment, and Sleeping Gods is the pick if you want the freedom of an open world without the combat crunch. None of these are one-night games in the traditional sense — that's what makes them adventures instead of just games.
