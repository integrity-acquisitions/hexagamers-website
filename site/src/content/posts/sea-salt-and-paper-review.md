## Overview

A set-collection card game where the art is folded paper and the tension is knowing when to stop. You're building a hand of ocean creatures, playing pairs for bonus effects, and watching your point total climb — until someone decides they're far enough ahead to end the round. Play to a target score and the highest total wins.

## Ryan's Review

### Likes
- The STOP / LAST CHANCE decision is genuinely agonizing every single time
- Gorgeous origami artwork that photographs beautifully
- Teaches in three minutes, plays in thirty
- Excellent at two players, which is rarer than it should be
- Small box, travels anywhere

### Dislikes
- Card draw luck can decide a close round
- Four players stretches the downtime more than the game needs
- The mermaid instant-win can end a game abruptly and unsatisfyingly

## First Impressions

I'll be honest — I bought this one for the art. A friend had it out on a coffee table and I picked it up thinking it was a design object rather than a game. Folded paper crabs, origami sharks, a colour palette that looks like a boutique stationery shop rather than a card game.

Then we played a round and I completely misjudged the ending, called STOP with seven points while someone quietly sat on twelve, and immediately wanted to play again.

That's the whole game in one sentence, really. It looks gentle and it is absolutely not.

## How It Plays

On your turn you do one of two things: draw two cards from the deck and keep one (discarding the other face-up), or take a face-up card from one of the two discard piles.

That's it. That's the turn. The game is what you do with the cards.

**Pairs trigger effects.** Play two matching cards from your hand and you get something:

- **Two crabs** let you dig through a discard pile and take what you want.
- **Two boats** give you an extra turn immediately.
- **Two fish** pull a card straight off the deck.
- **A swimmer and a shark** — the one mismatched pair — let you steal a card from an opponent's hand.

Those effects chain. A boat pair granting an extra turn that lets you play a crab pair that digs out the exact card you needed is the kind of small, satisfying combo the game hands out regularly.

**Other cards just score.** Shells, octopuses, penguins and sailors are collection sets that pay out more the more you gather. There are multiplier cards that scale off how many boats or fish or penguins you've got. And mermaids score based on your most-collected colour.

**The colour bonus is the quiet strategy.** Every card has a colour, and at the end of a round your most-common colour can be worth a chunk of points. Beginners collect for the creatures. Experienced players collect for the colours and let the creatures happen.

## The Decision That Makes the Game

Once your revealed cards total seven or more points, you get a choice at the end of your turn. This is the entire game.

**Say STOP** and the round ends right there. Everyone scores what they have. Safe, and often correct.

**Say LAST CHANCE** and you're betting you'll still be ahead after everyone else takes one more turn. Pull it off and you're rewarded. Get overtaken and the bet turns against you — the player who beat you gets the better end of it.

Or say nothing, keep playing, and try to build something bigger while praying nobody else hits seven first.

**This is where the game lives.** Every round you're doing the same arithmetic: I have nine points, they're holding six cards, do I know what's in them? Did they take that shark on purpose? Is the person to my left about to call it? The push-your-luck pressure is constant and it's calibrated beautifully — seven points comes fast enough that you're always nearly there and never comfortable.

I've won rounds by stopping at eight. I've lost rounds by pushing for fourteen. Neither felt random. Both felt like decisions I made.

**The mermaids are the exception.** Collect all four in a single round and you win the entire game on the spot, no matter the score. It's a real possibility that keeps everyone watching the mermaid cards, and it produces the occasional anticlimactic ending. Rare enough that it hasn't spoiled a game for me, but it happens.

## Player Count

**Two players is excellent** and it's the count I'd buy it for. The discard piles stay readable, you can genuinely track what your opponent is collecting, and the STOP timing becomes a proper duel. Target score is 40. A lot of two-player card games are compromises. This one isn't.

**Three players is the sweet spot for most groups.** Target 35. More cards circulating, more chaos in the discards, and enough uncertainty that reading the table gets properly hard.

**Four players is fine but loosest.** Target 30. Cards move fast and someone can hit seven before you've assembled anything at all. There's a bit more waiting and a bit less control. Still good — just the count I'd choose last.

## The Physical Game

Small box, roughly 60 cards, and card quality that's held up to a lot of shuffling. The origami art by Lucie Arnoux is the reason this game gets picked up off a shelf, and it deserves the attention it gets — every creature reads instantly even though it's abstract folded paper.

One practical note: the cards are the standard small format and they get handled constantly. Sleeves aren't a bad idea if you're the sort of person who sleeves things.

## Expansions

There are two small expansions, **Extra Salt** and **Extra Pepper**, each adding a handful of new cards and effects.

Consensus among people who've played both — and my own read — is that **Extra Pepper is the stronger of the two**. Extra Salt adds flexibility and a few new combos, but the base game doesn't feel like it's missing anything without it. Neither is essential. Play the base game a dozen times before you think about either.

## Who Should Buy It

**Buy it if** you want a small-box card game that plays well at two, teaches in minutes, and has real decisions in it. It's an ideal café game, travel game, or opener before something heavier. If you liked the tension in [No Thanks!](/best-board-games/) or push-your-luck games generally, this is a more attractive, slightly deeper version of that feeling.

**Skip it if** your group only plays at four and wants everyone maximally engaged the whole time, or if card-draw luck deciding an occasional round bothers you.

For the price and the box size, it's one of the easier recommendations I've made this year.

## FAQ

**How many players is Sea Salt & Paper best with?** Two or three. It's genuinely great at two, which is unusual, and three adds chaos without much cost. Four works but has the most downtime.

**How long does a game take?** Around 30 minutes, sometimes less. Individual rounds are only a few minutes each — you play several to reach the target score.

**Is it good for kids?** The box says 8+ and that's about right. The rules are simple enough for younger kids, but the push-your-luck judgement and colour-bonus scoring reward a bit of maturity.

**Do I need the expansions?** No. The base game is complete. If you eventually want more, Extra Pepper is the better pick.

**Who designed it?** Bruno Cathala and Théo Rivière, published by Bombyx, with Pandasaurus handling the North American release. Cathala's name is on a lot of very good light games, and this fits the pattern.

## Conclusion

Sea Salt & Paper is the rare game that's as good as it looks. The origami art gets it onto the table and the STOP decision keeps it there — it's a genuinely tense little game hiding inside a very pretty package.

It's not deep, and it isn't trying to be. What it does is give you one excruciating decision per round, over and over, in thirty minutes. That's a good trade. It's earned a permanent spot in my bag.
