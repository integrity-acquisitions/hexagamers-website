Ticket to Ride has been running since 2004, and Days of Wonder has never really stopped making them. There are standalone boxes, city editions that play in fifteen minutes, a kids' version, a twelve-game legacy campaign, and a numbered series of map expansions that need a base game you might not own.

Walk into a shop and you'll see four or five blue boxes with trains on them. Almost none of the packaging makes it obvious which ones are complete games and which ones are map add-ons that do nothing on their own. That's the problem worth solving, so it's the one we've built this around.

Ranked below are the versions you'll actually run into on a shop shelf or an Amazon search, with the standalone-or-expansion question answered up front for each one. The series is bigger than this list — there are region-locked and out-of-print boxes like Märklin, Germany and Northern Lights, plus a standalone card game — and there's a section near the bottom covering those, so you know what you're looking at if one turns up secondhand.

## First, The Only Rule That Matters

**Standalone** means the box contains everything — board, trains, cards, tickets. You can buy it as your only Ticket to Ride game.

**Expansion** means it contains a map and destination tickets and nothing else. You supply the train cards and plastic trains from a base game you already own. The numbered Map Collection volumes are all expansions, every one of them.

This trips people up constantly, because a Map Collection box looks just as substantial as a standalone box on a shelf. If you're buying someone their first Ticket to Ride, the volume number on the spine is the warning sign.

## The Ranking

### 1. Ticket to Ride: Europe

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-europe.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B000809OAO/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Standalone.** 2-5 players, 30-60 minutes, ages 8+.

The best all-round box in the line, and the one we own. Europe is the original game plus three additions: tunnels that can eat your whole turn, ferries that demand locomotive cards, and stations that let you borrow an opponent's route when you're blocked.

Stations are why it takes the top spot. In the original, if someone claims the track you needed, you're finished. Reroute the long way or eat the loss. Europe hands you three escape hatches, each worth 4 points if you never use it, so being blocked turns into a decision instead of a disaster. That one change is what makes it the better game for groups who'll play it a lot.

It ships with 46 destination tickets against the original's 30, too, which pushes back the point where regular players start recognising cards.

The costs are real though. Ten minutes to teach instead of five, and the ticket pool is short on genuinely long routes, which quietly favours whoever has played most. Europa 1912 fixes that if you get far enough to care.

### 2. Ticket to Ride (the original US map)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/0975277324/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Standalone.** 2-5 players, 30-60 minutes, ages 8+.

Second as a game, first as a purchase for most people. Worth stating plainly rather than burying it in the ordering.

The original is the cleanest version of this design. Three actions, five minutes to teach, and a map with open space and long transcontinental runs that remain the most satisfying thing the series does. If the box is going to get taught to new people over and over, relatives at Christmas, friends who don't play games, that simplicity is worth more than anything Europe adds on top.

It's the meaner game at four and five players, as well, because being blocked is permanent and there's no station to bail you out. Some groups want exactly that.

One buying note: a 2025 refresh of the original is in circulation (ASIN `B0F8PKN4B7`) alongside the long-running listing. Same game, new packaging.

### 3. Ticket to Ride: Nordic Countries

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-nordic-countries.webp)](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-nordic-countries.webp)

**Standalone.** 2-3 players, 30-60 minutes, ages 8+.

The specialist pick, and the best version of Ticket to Ride at two players.

Every other box says 2-5 and plays fine at two while feeling a bit empty. A big map with two people on it doesn't generate much conflict. Nordic Countries is built for 2-3 and nothing else. The map across Denmark, Finland, Norway and Sweden is deliberately narrow, routes are contested from the first turn, and it keeps tunnels and ferries so you're not trading away depth for the smaller count.

If you're a couple, or your game nights are mostly you and one other person, this is a better buy than Europe. Almost nobody tells you that.

The ceiling is the catch. It stops at three players, so it can't be your only copy if you ever host four. Availability is the other one — it's a 2007 box and it's drifted out of print, so expect to hunt or pay over the odds. If that's where you land, **London** at #4 is the in-print two-player answer, and **Northern Lights** covers the same Scandinavian map at 2-5 players if you can source it.

### 4. Ticket to Ride: London

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-london.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07SG5QWJY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Standalone.** 2-4 players, 10-15 minutes, ages 8+.

The one we should have called out separately from the start, because it's the answer to a question a lot of people are actually asking: what's the cheapest way to find out whether you like Ticket to Ride, and what works at two.

London is 1970s buses across a scaled-down map of the city, and it's the pick of the small boxes at two players. Where Europe and the original US map both feel roomy with only two people on them, London's map is cramped by design — you and one other person are competing for the same handful of routes from the first turn, and a game is genuinely done in fifteen minutes. That's the same argument we make for Nordic Countries above, except London is still in print and costs a fraction as much.

It teaches in about three minutes, too, which makes it the easiest box on this page to put in front of someone who says they don't like board games.

The limits are the obvious ones. Four players is a squeeze on a map this small, and fifteen minutes doesn't leave room for a long-route plan to pay off — the thing the full-size boxes do best. It's a great first purchase and a great second one, but if your group is usually four or five people it shouldn't be your only copy.

### 5. The other city editions — New York, Paris, Berlin, San Francisco, Amsterdam

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-new-york.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07F46X4GY/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Standalone.** 2-4 players, 10-15 minutes, ages 8+.

Small boxes, small maps, one city each, done in a quarter of an hour. New York is 1960s taxis, Amsterdam is 17th-century horse carts, San Francisco is cable cars, Berlin is trams and subways, Paris is 1920s buses. Some are branded as **Express** editions.

These are much better than they have any right to be. You get the core decision loop intact, competition for routes starts immediately because the map is tiny, and the box fits in a coat pocket. For a lunch break or a train journey they're excellent, and they're cheap enough to own two or three.

What they're not is a replacement for a full-size box. Fifteen minutes doesn't leave room for a plan to develop, and the tickets are short enough that the long-route payoff never really lands. Buy one as your second Ticket to Ride, not your first.

San Francisco and Berlin get picked out most often as the strongest maps of the set after London, and both hold up well at two players.

### 6. Ticket to Ride Legacy: Legends of the West

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-legacy-legends-of-the-west.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B0C6V67YZC/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Standalone.** 2-5 players, twelve-game campaign, ages 10+.

Released in late 2023, designed by Rob Daviau and Matt Leacock alongside Alan R. Moon, which is to say the people behind Pandemic Legacy got their hands on it. Twelve linked games heading west across 19th-century North America, with the board and the rules changing permanently as you go.

When it works it's the most memorable thing in the series by a distance. The map fills up with your group's history, and rules unlock in ways that genuinely change what the game is.

It sits at fifth for practical reasons rather than quality ones. It needs the same people, in roughly the same order, twelve times. Groups who can actually commit to that are rarer than they think, and a campaign abandoned at game four is the worst value on this page. It's also the most expensive box here, and being legacy, it ends.

Buy it if your group has already finished a campaign together. Otherwise you're buying an intention.

### 7. Ticket to Ride: Rails & Sails

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-rails-and-sails.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01IHOV8ME/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Standalone.** 2-5 players, 90-120 minutes, ages 10+.

Two oversized double-sided maps — the World, and the Great Lakes of North America — and a second currency. You're now managing train cards *and* ship cards, plus harbours that score at the end.

It's the biggest and most strategic version, and for a group who find the base games too light it's the right answer. Juggling two card types instead of one adds a real layer, and the World map has a scale nothing else in the line comes near.

The play time isn't a rounding error, though. Ninety minutes to two hours turns Ticket to Ride from the game that warms up an evening into the game that is the evening. That's a different product for a different table, and it gives up the thing that made the series famous, because you can't teach this one in five minutes.

### 8. Ticket to Ride: First Journey

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-first-journey.webp)](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B01MQ4IKQJ/?tag=hexagamers-20)

**Standalone.** 2-4 players, 15-30 minutes, ages 6+.

The kids' version. Simplified US map, bigger cities, shorter routes, and you win by completing six destination tickets rather than counting points. There's a Europe version of it too.

It does its job properly, which is more than most junior editions manage. The actions are identical to the real game, draw cards, claim routes, complete tickets, so a child who plays this at six moves up at eight or nine with nothing to unlearn. That's why we put it on our [best educational board games](/best-educational-board-games/) list.

It sits here only because the audience is narrow. For the six-to-eight-year-old it's aimed at, it's as good a purchase as anything on this page. For anyone else it's too slight, and an eight-year-old already playing strategy games will find it patronising.

### 9. The Map Collection volumes (1-7)

[![Hexagamers](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-map-collection-vol-5-uk.webp)](https://img.hexagamers.com/hexagamers-box-art/ticket-to-ride-map-collection-vol-5-uk.webp)

**Expansions — these are not standalone games.**

- **Vol. 1:** Team Asia / Legendary Asia
- **Vol. 2:** India / Switzerland
- **Vol. 3:** The Heart of Africa
- **Vol. 4:** Nederland
- **Vol. 5:** United Kingdom / Pennsylvania
- **Vol. 6:** France / Old West
- **Vol. 7:** Japan / Italy

Each volume holds a map, usually two, with its own destination tickets and its own rule twist. No train cards, no plastic trains. You need the original or Europe to play any of them.

They're last because of that dependency, not because of quality. Several are excellent, and for someone who's played their base game thirty times a new map beats a seventh copy of the same one. UK (Vol. 5) adds a technology-upgrade system that's the most substantial change anywhere in the series. Nederland (Vol. 4) brings toll roads. Team Asia (Vol. 1) is one of the very few good team-based route games.

Just don't buy one first. A Map Collection box as somebody's introduction to Ticket to Ride is a gift they can't open.

## What We Haven't Ranked

The list above is the versions you can walk into a shop and buy today. The series has more than that, and these are the ones most likely to turn up in a search or on a secondhand shelf:

- **Märklin** (2006) — a German map with a passenger-pickup system layered on top. Long out of print and collectible, so it sells well above what a current box costs.
- **Germany** (2012) — effectively Märklin's map reissued without the licence, with a passenger rule of its own. Also hard to find new.
- **Northern Lights** (2023) — the same Scandinavian region as Nordic Countries, but 2-5 players with end-game bonus cards. Sold mainly in Scandinavia, which is why it's absent above rather than because it's bad. If you want the Nordic map and can't find Nordic Countries, this is the one still being printed.
- **Ghost Train** (2023) — a Halloween-themed First Journey variant for the same 6+ age bracket.
- **The Card Game** (2008) — no board at all. A different game wearing the name.
- **Regional one-offs** — Switzerland began as a 2007 standalone and now only exists inside Map Collection Vol. 2. India, Nederland and the UK have similar histories.

None of these change the buying advice below. They're listed so that a box we didn't rank doesn't read as one we missed.

## So Which One Should You Actually Buy?

**Never played it, buying one box:** the **original US version**. Five-minute teach, cleanest design, most forgiving to hand to non-gamers.

**You know you like this style, buying for a group that plays regularly:** **Europe**. More tickets, more replay value, and stations mean nobody gets locked out and checks out of the game.

**It's mostly two of you:** **Nordic Countries** is still the best two-player map in the series if you can find it — but it's scarce now, so **London** is the realistic answer. It's in print, it's cheap, and a small map is exactly what two players want.

**You want to spend as little as possible finding out if you like it:** **London**, for the same reasons.

**Buying for a child aged 6-8:** **First Journey**.

**You want something in a bag for travel or lunch breaks:** any **city edition** — London, then San Francisco or Berlin.

**Your group finishes campaigns:** **Legacy: Legends of the West**.

**You already own a base game and want more:** a **Map Collection volume**, starting with UK (Vol. 5).

The honest summary is that the series peaked early. The original and Europe are still the two best boxes twenty years on, and most of what followed is a variation built for a particular situation rather than an improvement on the formula. We don't mean that as a knock. Nordic Countries at two players and a city edition in a backpack are both doing something the flagship boxes can't. But if you're buying one box and you want the best odds of it actually hitting the table, you're choosing between the first two here.

One last warning worth repeating. There's a 15th Anniversary Edition of Europe in a much bigger box, with train miniatures and 108 destination tickets. It's lovely and it costs a fair bit more, but its components don't match the standard edition, so if you're buying to play alongside someone who owns standard Europe, get the standard box.
